The short tour. The case starts at Can AI outgrow the planet’s power grid?. This page is one sitting through the same ground — not a second textbook. Where a section overlaps a deep page, the deep page wins; follow the links.

Take two light switches and two lamps, wire them the right way, and they add: flip both on and the lamp that means “two” lights up. A chatbot answering a question is, underneath, that same trick done by a few hundred billion switches flipping in a building that draws as much power as a town. This essay builds that machine up from a single switch you can tap, and follows the three things that had to get cheap together: the switch, the joule and the example to learn from.

It starts with arithmetic made of switches, then the idea that one machine can run any program. Then the transistor, and the fifty years in which chips got better by getting smaller, until heat called time. Then a different way to program a computer: not writing rules, but nudging numbers until the machine gets examples right. Finally, what happened when people found that doing more of that, on more data, kept working, and what it now asks of the electricity grid.

This is the short tour of the series; the deeper posts are listed at the top of the page. It also leans on a sister series, How Electricity Works, wherever power and prices come in.

1A sum made of switches

Deep: Rules.

Take two light switches and two lamps. Wire the first lamp so it lights when exactly one switch is on, and the second so it lights only when both are. Now read the switches as numbers, off is 0 and on is 1, and read the lamps as a two-digit answer.

Every case comes out right. 0 + 0 lights nothing. 1 + 0 lights the right-hand lamp. 1 + 1 lights only the left-hand one, which reads “10”: one two and no ones, in the counting system that uses only the digits 0 and 1, called binary. The two boxes are the first logic gates of this essay; engineers call them XOR (“exclusive or”) and AND.

Claude Shannon’s 1937 thesis showed that relay circuits are Boolean algebra made physical: arithmetic, comparison and memory are patterns of switches driving switches.1

To add bigger numbers, copy the box. Each copy adds three bits, one from each number plus the carry from the column to its right, exactly as in long addition at school.

Two things already show. First, the answer is not instant: each column has to wait for the carry from its neighbour, so a wider adder is slower unless it is cleverer. Second, every change flips some of the little switches inside: two to five usually, and up to thirteen of the twenty when a carry ripples all the way along. Hold on to both. Speed and the number of flips will decide everything that follows.

The maths: binary and the full adder

A binary number with digits b3b2b1b0 means 8b3 + 4b2 + 2b1 + b0. One full adder takes bits a, b and an incoming carry c and produces

sum = a ⊕ b ⊕ c, carry = (a ∧ b) ∨ (c ∧ (a ⊕ b))

where ⊕ is XOR, ∧ is AND and ∨ is OR: two XORs, two ANDs and one OR, the five gates the figure counts.

How a relay clicks, how a valve amplifies, and why a transistor’s current falls tenfold for every 70 millivolts or so are worked through in Rules.

2Rules on a tape

An adder does one thing. The leap that made computers was a machine that could be told what to do — Babbage’s unfinished Analytical Engine and Lovelace’s notes sit on Rules; what matters here is the 1936 answer.2 Alan Turing asked what it means for anything to be computable at all, and answered with a machine so simple it fits in a figure: a tape of squares, a head that reads and writes one square at a time, and a short table of rules.3

Turing’s point was that a single machine of this kind can read a description of any other such machine from its tape and imitate it. That is a general-purpose computer: the wiring stays fixed and the program is just data. Everything since, from ENIAC to the chip in a phone, is a faster version of this idea, with the tape replaced by memory.

3Faster switches, and what a flip costs

Deep: The price of a flip (Landauer peek included).

Relays take milliseconds; tubes have no moving parts but burn heaters whether they switch or not — ENIAC’s 17,468 tubes drew about 150 kW for 5,000 additions a second.45 The Rules and Flip pages open those mechanisms. What matters for the rest of this tour: every transistor flip fills or empties a tiny wire and the next gate with charge, like a bucket from a tank to a drain. The energy leaves as heat.

A full flip and back costs the bucket’s charge times the tank’s height — half lost charging, half dumping. That is chip economics for forty years: a smaller transistor has a smaller bucket; lower voltage helps twice (charge and push). Halve both and each flip costs an eighth. The maths and the measured joules-per-tick sit on Flip.

The maths: energy per switch

A capacitance C charged to voltage V holds charge Q = CV and energy ½CV². Charging it from a supply at V draws CV² from the supply; half is stored and half is dissipated in the transistor that does the charging. Discharging dissipates the stored half. So each full on-off cycle turns CV² into heat, ½CV² per transition, and a chip with N gates switching on a fraction α of its f clock ticks per second dissipates about

P ≈ α · N · C · V² · f

What sets the smallest line a chip factory can print, and what that costs in light and yield, is in The Price of a Flip: printing with light.

4Moore, Dennard and the heat wall

Gordon Moore’s 1965/1975 doubling rule held, give or take, for fifty years — from the Intel 4004’s ~2,300 transistors (1971) onward.78 Why smaller also meant faster and no hotter is Dennard’s 1974 rule: shrink every dimension and the voltage together; energy per flip falls, clocks rise, heat per area stays flat.9 The figure tracks the biggest chips’ transistors, clock, power and cores.

Drag past 2005: transistors keep climbing, but clocks stall near 3–5 GHz and power flattens near a hundred watts. Voltage stopped falling near one volt — lower and the switch leaks when “off” — so a faster clock only meant more heat. The green dots are the answer: cores. Split the job across many slower workers. That hinge is where modern AI hangs.

The maths: Dennard scaling

Shrink lengths and voltage by a factor k > 1. Capacitance falls as 1/k, voltage as 1/k, and delay as 1/k so frequency rises by k. Power per transistor, CV²f, falls as (1/k)(1/k²)(k) = 1/k². Transistors per area rise by k², so power per area is unchanged. Once V stops falling, power per transistor falls only as 1/k while density still rises as k², and power density grows by k every generation at the same clock.

The energy per transistor per clock tick, measured on half a century of processors, and the “dark silicon” that the end of Dennard scaling left behind are in The Price of a Flip.

5A joule per operation

Put the two trends together, smaller switches and lower voltages, and the cost of one operation in energy has fallen by a number too large to picture. ENIAC used about 30 joules for every addition, 150 kW divided by 5,000 additions a second. A modern AI chip does more than a trillion operations per joule.

Run GPT-3’s training on ENIAC and the energy comes to about 80,000 years of all the electricity the world now generates, and the time to about two trillion years. Run it on one H100 and the chip alone needs about 60 megawatt-hours, although it would take about ten years. Neither is how it was actually done, but the gap, a factor of about forty trillion, is why the methods of section 7 waited half a century to work.

The comparison mixes two different things on purpose. The supercomputers count everything they draw, including memory, networks and the machine’s own cooling, and use 64-bit numbers. The chips count only the chip at its rated power and use 16-bit numbers, which are much cheaper. Section 13 adds back the server, the building and the cooling.

6Many slow hands

Deep: Parallel.

After 2005 a faster computer meant more workers, not a faster one. How much that helps depends on the job. Some work must happen in order, each step needing the last; some can be shared out.

With even 5% of the job in order, a thousand workers are only about twenty times faster than one. Most ordinary programs are like that, which is why a laptop with sixteen cores does not feel sixteen times faster. But some jobs have almost no grey. Drawing a frame of a video game means working out the colour of two million pixels that do not depend on each other. Graphics chips, GPUs, were built for exactly that: thousands of simple arithmetic units doing the same thing to different data.

In 2007 Nvidia released CUDA, which let programmers use those units for any arithmetic, not only pictures.16 Scientists used them for physics. As section 9 shows, the arithmetic of a neural network is almost all grey-free multiplication and addition, which made GPUs the right machine for a kind of program that, in 2007, hardly anyone thought worth running.

The maths: Amdahl’s law

If a fraction s of the work is serial and the rest is spread perfectly over N workers, the speed-up is

S(N) = 1 / ( s + (1 − s) / N ),

which approaches 1/s however large N grows.

Why a matrix multiply is ideal work for many hands, and why a chip often waits on memory instead of computing, are worked through with a roofline you can move in Parallel.

7Learning instead of programming

Deep: A model.

Everything so far is a computer following instructions someone wrote. Now try a problem where nobody can write the instructions. Below are red and blue dots. Draw a straight line that keeps them apart.

You did it by eye. The machine does it with a rule so simple it fits in a sentence: pick a dot it has got wrong, and nudge the line a little towards putting that dot on the right side; repeat. The line’s position is set by three numbers, its tilt in two directions and its offset, and learning is nothing more than turning those three knobs. They are called weights.

Frank Rosenblatt described this machine in 1958 and built it in hardware as the Mark I Perceptron, with photocells for eyes and motor-driven knobs for weights.17 It could learn to tell simple shapes apart. Nobody wrote a rule for what a triangle looks like; the rule was in the examples. That is the whole idea of machine learning, and everything in the rest of this essay is that idea scaled up by factors of billions.

It ran into trouble quickly. Some patterns, as section 9 shows, cannot be split by any single line, and the perceptron has no way to do better. For two decades the field mostly moved on.

8Rolling downhill

The perceptron’s nudges only work for one line. To adjust millions of knobs at once we need a better picture of what “getting it less wrong” means. Measure how wrong the machine is with a single number, the error (or loss). Every setting of the knobs has an error, so the errors form a landscape, with one direction for every knob. Learning is then finding a low point in that landscape. With only two knobs it can be drawn as real ground: below, the two knobs run across the floor and the height is the error.

A real model’s landscape cannot be drawn; it has far too many dimensions. But at any point you can work out which way is downhill, the gradient, and take a step. Small steps crawl along the curved floor of the valley. Large steps are faster until, past some size, every step overshoots the floor and lands higher up the other wall, and the ball zigzags or flies off entirely. Choosing that step size is still, seventy years on, one of the fiddliest parts of training a large model.

The maths: gradient descent

With weights w and loss L(w), each step is

w ← w − η ∇L(w),

where ∇L is the vector of partial derivatives and η the step size (learning rate). On a valley with curvature λ across it, the steps converge only if η < 2/λ, which is why the ball in the figure starts to zigzag and then escapes as the step size grows.

9Layers

Here is a pattern no straight line can split: a blue cluster surrounded by a red ring. The fix is to use several lines at once and combine them. Each dashed line below is one neuron: it is exactly the line from section 7, with its own three knobs. A final neuron combines their outputs. The whole thing is a neural network, and the ball from section 8 is rolling in a landscape with one dimension per knob.

With two lines there is no way to fence in the blue cluster, and the error stalls. With three or more, the network finds a triangle-ish fence and then rounds it off. It finds that shape itself. The trick that makes this possible for networks with many layers is called backpropagation: working out the downhill direction for every knob at once, by passing the error backwards through the network one layer at a time. It was described in the 1970s and made famous by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams in 1986.18

Look at what the computer is doing on each step. For every dot, for every neuron, multiply each input by a weight and add them up. Then the same backwards. It is the same few multiply-and-add operations, repeated over and over on different numbers, with almost nothing that has to wait for anything else. That is the grey-free job of section 6. Once networks got big, they were a perfect fit for GPUs.

Why one line cannot draw XOR, how the blame is passed backwards, and why momentum helps are worked through with figures in the companion post A Model.

10Data, and 2012

A network with millions of knobs can learn almost anything, including the accidents of whatever examples it was shown. To learn something general it needs many examples. In 2009 Fei-Fei Li’s group at Princeton and Stanford published ImageNet, millions of photographs each labelled by people, through Amazon’s crowd-work service, with one of thousands of categories.19 From 2010 a yearly contest asked programs to label 150,000 test photos in 1,000 categories, scoring an answer as right if the true label was among their five guesses.20

Hand-designed features stalled near 25% error in 2010–11. In 2012 Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton’s AlexNet (~60M weights, two GTX 580s, about a week) cut it to 16%.22 The idea was old; the labelled data and cheap parallel arithmetic were new. Within three years almost every entry was a network, and the best beat a trained person. Plotted against training operations, the winners line up: AlexNet → ResNet-152 is ~22× compute for a 4.6× fall in contest error.

11Attention

Deep: Language.

Language is harder than pictures because meaning depends on order and on words far apart. In “the robot put the cup on the table because it was empty”, “it” is the cup, and only “empty” says so. Through the 2010s the best language networks read a sentence one word at a time, carrying a summary of everything so far in a memory that had to be updated before the next word could be read.

In 2017 eight researchers, most of them at Google, proposed the transformer, built around a mechanism they called attention: every word computes, all at the same moment, how much to draw from every other word.23 In a model that writes text, like the tiny one above, each word looks only backwards, at itself and what came before. Nothing has to wait for the previous word, so a whole passage can be processed in parallel, and nothing fades with distance. It was a better model and, just as important, a better fit for GPUs: grey-free arithmetic again. Nearly every large language model since is built on it.

Queries, keys and values, the memory problem attention solved, and a next-word predictor counted from Alice in Wonderland are in A Model.

12Scaling

Deep: Scaling · The data.

The next discovery was not a new idea but a measurement. In 2020 a team at OpenAI trained language models of many sizes and found that their error fell along a smooth, predictable curve as they added parameters, data and compute: ten times the compute bought roughly the same improvement each time, over seven orders of magnitude.24 In 2022 DeepMind in London refined the recipe, showing that for a fixed budget the models of the day had too many parameters and too little data; their 70-billion-parameter Chinchilla, trained on 1.4 trillion words and word fragments, beat models four times its size.25 If error falls predictably with compute, the way to a better model is to buy more compute. People did. In the next figure each dot is a notable model, coloured by where it was built: the United States, China, the UK or elsewhere.

For sixty years the compute used to train notable models grew about as fast as Moore’s law. Since about 2010 it has grown far faster, roughly fourfold or more every year, which Epoch AI’s own analysis of the largest models also finds.27 Chips did get better: from the GTX 580 of 2010 to the B200 of 2024, operations per joule rose about 350-fold (section 5), much of it from moving to 16-bit numbers. Over the same fourteen years the fit says training compute rose by a factor of hundreds of millions. The rest came from buying more chips and running them for longer. Of the 540 models, 57% come from American organisations and 17% from Chinese ones; Britain, South Korea, Germany, Canada and a dozen other countries account for most of the rest.

The maths: compute, parameters and tokens

Training a transformer with N parameters on D tokens takes about C ≈ 6ND floating-point operations: two per parameter per token for the forward pass and four for the backward pass. Chinchilla’s fit put the compute-optimal ratio near D ≈ 20N. Converting to GPU-hours divides C by the chip’s peak rate times its utilisation (the share of peak a real training run achieves) and by 3,600 seconds.

How a fixed budget is best split between model size and data, whether the training data runs out, and what a frontier run costs are in Scaling, with the data wall and the training bill. What happens after pretraining, an industry of paid experts writing, ranking and grading for the next model, is the data flywheel.

13What a megawatt-hour buys

Deep: The demand · The yardstick.

A GPU-hour is now a unit of trade, rented by the hour from cloud companies. A kilowatt-hour is the unit of the electricity series. Here is the exchange rate. An Nvidia DGX H100 server holds eight H100 chips and draws at most about 10.2 kW, so 1.275 kW for each GPU’s share of the chips, processors, memory, fans and network cards.28 The building adds cooling and power conversion on top, measured by its power usage effectiveness (PUE), from 1.09 for Google’s fleet to 1.56 for the average site in Uptime Institute’s survey,29 so at full draw one megawatt-hour runs between about 500 and 720 H100-hours. The AI Factory follows that megawatt-hour from the meter to the chips.

A training run of a hundred thousand GPU-hours needs roughly 140–200 MWh. At €100/MWh, the electricity for one GPU-hour is 14–20 cents. The chip is far dearer: one H100’s share of a DGX server sold for about $33,600 in 2023, which spread over four years of round-the-clock use is about 85 euro cents an hour before the building.13 That is why AI companies buy chips first and electricity second, and also why, once chips are plentiful, it is the electricity, and the grid connection to deliver it, that runs out.

What sets that €/MWh, and why it differs so much by hour and by place, is the subject of the electricity series’ merit order and what a power plant costs.

14The factory and the grid

Deep: The AI factory · Orbital.

Put many servers together and the numbers leave the scale of buildings — xAI’s Memphis cluster (100,000 H100s, 2024) needed about 150 MW and ran gas turbines while it waited for the grid.30 The working-out is on The AI Factory.

A million H100s draw 1.4–2 GW day and night. Ireland’s data centres already take 22% of its electricity. In July 2024 ~1,500 MW in northern Virginia switched to backup within seconds after a voltage dip — power stations in reverse. Connection queues and transformers are the same story as wind and solar; the working-out is on The AI Factory.

Where a cluster’s power goes between the chip and the grid, why liquid replaces air, why the servers never pause for cheap hours, and how a training run’s power swings by tens of megawatts are worked through in The AI Factory.

Chip efficiency still rises (~2×/generation); compute bought rises faster. Binding limits: plants, lines, transformers, €/kWh around the clock. Free web text as training fuel is close to used up; paid examples take over — sized on The data.

Final words

Nothing on the way from two switches to a chatbot was a single leap — each step a cheaper switch, joule or example. Stacked over eighty years they built something that now has to be planned for like a steel works.
