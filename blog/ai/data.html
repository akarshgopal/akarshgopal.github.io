Llama 2 was trained on about two trillion tokens of mostly human text. The human preference comparisons that shaped its chat behaviour were about a billion tokens — roughly one comparison in every 1,800 pretraining tokens.10 This page asks whether that human supply caps how much AI can grow, or whether a deployed model can spin a flywheel that makes its own next examples.

Examples branch. No Act 1 slider is bound to this page — the meter’s U and T live on Demand and Scaling. This page asks whether human examples can keep feeding that growth after attention saturates. It sits on the case map because a capped example supply is a force against a rising U.

1The data flywheel

The data wall is about text people have already written. The other answer is to pay people to write more, of a particular kind. Training on the web makes a model that continues text. Turning it into an assistant that follows instructions, admits what it does not know and writes code that runs needs data nobody had written: examples of the answers wanted, judgements of which of two answers is better and, for the agents of 2025 and 2026, practice tasks that check the work automatically. OpenAI’s InstructGPT, the 2022 forerunner of ChatGPT, was tuned with the help of about 40 contractors hired through Upwork and Scale AI, who wrote example answers and ranked the model’s replies.9 A year later Meta tuned Llama 2 on 27,540 written examples and more than 1.4 million comparisons between pairs of answers. Drawn to scale, the public text it read first dwarfs the human comparisons.10

By volume the human part is almost nothing: a square you have to zoom in forty-two times to see properly, one token in about 1,800. Yet it is what turned a text predictor into Llama 2-Chat, and Meta’s team found the reward model it trained was still improving with every weekly batch of comparisons when they stopped.10 That is the economics of this section in one picture. The web gives the bulk for nothing; the part that decides how the model behaves has to be made by people, to order, and paid for.

Once a model is released, the loop closes. Its users bring prompts and ratings, which show what people ask for and where the model fails. Paid experts then write, rank and grade to fix those failures, and the next model is better and draws more users. ChatGPT had 100 million weekly users a year after its launch, and more than 1.2 billion by October 2026.32 OpenAI can train on chats in its consumer apps unless the user opts out; in 2025 Anthropic asked its consumer users to choose.11

What has changed most is who does the work. ImageNet, the photo collection behind the 2012 result in the short tour, was sorted between 2007 and 2010 by about 49,000 people in 167 countries through Amazon’s Mechanical Turk, a marketplace for small online tasks.12 A 2018 study of 2,676 Mechanical Turk workers found median earnings of about $2 an hour.13 That end of the market has not gone away: in 2023 TIME reported that workers in Kenya labelling violent and abusive text for OpenAI, through the contractor Sama, took home less than $2 an hour.14 What is new is the top end. Mercor said in October 2025 that it was paying more than 30,000 experts an average of over $85 an hour, more than $1.5 million a day.15 micro1 says some of its experts earn up to about $100 an hour.17 The customers want doctors, lawyers, mathematicians and programmers, because a model that already writes fluently learns little from someone who knows less than it does.

Agents add a third kind of work. To learn by trial and error, an agent needs a task and a way to score each attempt: a codebase with a failing test, a spreadsheet with a known answer, a website to navigate. People build these environments and their graders once, and a model can then practise in them many thousands of times. In September 2025 TechCrunch reported, citing The Information, that Anthropic’s leaders had discussed spending more than $1 billion on such environments over the following year.18

Two other sources run alongside. One is synthetic data, written by models for models. Microsoft’s phi-1, a small model for writing code, was trained partly on under a billion tokens of textbooks and exercises written by GPT-3.5.19 It is cheap, but it is not free of people: Ilia Shumailov and colleagues showed in 2024 that models trained over generations on their own output lose the rare cases first and then degrade, so synthetic data needs filtering, checking and fresh human data beside it.20 The other is the physical world, which text does not cover. Ego4D, a university consortium’s 2022 dataset, holds 3,670 hours of video filmed from the heads of 931 people in 74 places across nine countries.21 Open X-Embodiment pooled more than a million robot demonstrations from 22 kinds of robot in 34 labs.22 Companies now collect this to order. MicroAGI, a Munich start-up, released about a million depth-camera frames of household tasks, and in May 2026 its Shift app offered New Yorkers free home cleaning if the cleaners could film the work in first person, paying people who record their own chores $20 an hour plus bonuses.23

The companies that supply all this have grown faster than almost anything else in AI except the labs. The figure has every revenue and valuation figure for them that we could tie to a company statement or a named news report.

The biggest single transaction came in June 2025, when Meta paid $14.3 billion for 49% of Scale AI, without voting rights, valuing it at $29 billion; Scale’s founder Alexandr Wang moved to Meta to lead its new superintelligence lab. Google, Scale’s largest customer, which had planned to pay it about $200 million that year, then planned to stop, Reuters reported.24 In April 2025 Scale said it expected about $2 billion of revenue that year; it says it took just under $1 billion.25 Surge AI, which has taken no outside money, made $1.2 billion in 2024, more than Scale.26 Mercor was valued at $10 billion in October 2025, and in July 2026 its chief executive said it had reached $2 billion a year in gross revenue.15,16 micro1 went from a $500 million valuation in September 2025 to $4 billion a year later.17 Turing, Invisible Technologies and Handshake, a careers site for students that rebuilt itself around AI training work, are each in the billions.27–29

Is this a Manhattan Project? Wang’s parents were physicists at Los Alamos, where the bomb was designed, so the comparison is close to hand.25 The project cost $1.89 billion to the end of 1945, about $34 billion in 2025 dollars.30,31 Adding the latest figure for each company in the chart gives about $6.6 billion a year, against the $8.5 billion or so that the project spent in an average year of its four, in today’s money. That sum is ours. It mixes annual revenue with run-rates, counts gross billings, uses figures of different ages (Surge’s latest is for 2024, Turing’s from January 2025), and leaves out the labs’ own data teams and many smaller suppliers; no reliable total for what the labs spend on human data has been published. micro1’s chief executive put the market at $10–15 billion in late 2025, an estimate from someone selling into it.17 As a share of the economy the comparison fails: the project’s cost equalled 0.84% of America’s 1944 output, which today would be about $260 billion, around forty times the sum above.31 So the comparison holds in one sense and fails in the other. Year for year, in dollars adjusted for prices, the human-data business is now in the same range as the Manhattan Project. Measured against the size of the economy it is a fortieth of it.

Final words

If human examples run out and synthetic data fails, demand saturates and the Act 1 path bends down. If the flywheel works, tokens per task and tasks per day can keep rising after human attention caps. Either way, set the demand sliders on the index and read the bars. The Scaling page keeps the bill and the serving cost; this page keeps the examples.
