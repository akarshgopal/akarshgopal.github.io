The Data: Scale of the Collection

A modern language model is a next-token predictor trained on a pile of text measured in trillions of tokens. That pile did not appear from nowhere. It is the filtered residue of a public-web archive measured in petabytes, written over decades by hundreds of millions of people, then ranked and patched by tens of thousands of paid workers. This page puts numbers on that effort — how much, who, how long, how much money, and where the bytes go — and asks whether that supply caps how far AI demand can grow.

Examples branch. No Act 1 slider is bound to this page — the meter’s U and T live on Demand and Scaling. This page asks whether human examples can keep feeding that growth. It sits on the case map because a capped example supply is a force against a rising U. More tokens also mean more training FLOPs (about 6ND operations for N parameters and D tokens), which is why data scale shows up on the energy ledger at all.1

1Why so much data

Three reasons stack. First, the objective itself: a language model is trained to predict the next token. To cover the world’s languages, genres, facts and failure modes, the training distribution has to look like the world — which, for text, mostly means the public web plus code, books and whatever a lab can license.2

Second, scaling laws. From Kaplan (2020) through Hoffmann / Chinchilla (2022), loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters and tokens. Compute-optimal training wants on the order of ~20 tokens per parameter; many recent models are deliberately overtrained far past that so they are cheaper to serve. Meta’s Llama 3.1 405B saw about 15.6 trillion tokens, nearly 40 per parameter.3 More tokens is not a hobby — it is the other axis of the bet that loss is predictable.

Third, coverage and rarity. A model that has never seen a legal brief, a rare language, or a failing unit test will not invent them reliably. Labs therefore keep widening the mix: web, code, papers, books, then multimodal and agent trajectories. The data wall on the Scaling page is what happens when the cheap public-text axis runs out.

2How much text

Start from the public warehouse. Common Crawl, a non-profit, has been snapshotting the web since 2008. By September 2025 its archive held about 10.1 PiB across 300 billion+ pages, with roughly 2.5 billion pages added in a typical month (a few hundred tebibytes of uncompressed content per crawl).4 That is not yet training data. Most of it is boilerplate, near-duplicates, low-quality pages and formats a tokenizer should never see.

Open recipes show how violently the pile shrinks. Hugging Face’s FineWeb turns 96 Common Crawl snapshots into 15 trillion filtered tokens; its educational slice, FineWeb-Edu, is 1.3 trillion. NVIDIA’s Nemotron-CC reports 4.4 trillion unique real tokens (plus 1.9T synthetic) from a long Common Crawl span. Allen AI’s Dolma is a 3-trillion-token open mix in which Common Crawl alone contributes about 2.3T tokens, with code, papers, Reddit, books and Wikipedia filling the rest.5 Frontier labs do not publish their mixes; Llama 3.1’s 15.6T is a rare open-weight anchor for “how much a frontier run actually saw.”

Epoch AI’s 2024 estimate of the effective stock of quality- and repetition-adjusted public human text is about 300 trillion tokens (90% interval roughly 100T–1,000T). If dataset sizes keep growing as they have, that stock is projected to be fully utilised sometime between 2026 and 2032.6 The ladder below places that stock next to concrete corpora.

Multimodal data is a parallel mountain: Ego4D alone is 3,670 hours of first-person video from 931 people; Open X-Embodiment pooled more than a million robot demonstrations. Those are still tiny next to text token counts, but they are the kind of data text cannot replace.8

3How many people

“How many people” is four different questions. Confusing them is how the industry’s labour story gets washed out.

Creators. The people who wrote the web, the code, the books. Their number is not published as a headcount; it is “everyone whose public writing landed in a WARC.” Common Crawl’s hundreds of billions of pages are the fossil record of that labour. Payment for this use is usually zero.

Crowd annotators. ImageNet was sorted between 2007 and 2010 by about 49,000 Mechanical Turk workers in 167 countries. A 2018 study of 2,676 MTurk workers found median earnings of about $2 an hour. That end of the market has not vanished: in 2023 TIME reported Kenyan workers labelling toxic content for OpenAI via Sama at under $2 an hour.9

Expert raters and writers. InstructGPT (2022) used about 40 contractors through Upwork and Scale AI. Llama 2 used 27,540 written demonstrations and more than 1.4 million preference comparisons collected by Meta. By 2025 Mercor claimed more than 30,000 experts at an average over $85 an hour; micro1 cites rates up to about $100 an hour. These are the people who turn a text predictor into a product that follows instructions.10

Users. Once a model ships, prompts and ratings become data. ChatGPT passed 100 million weekly users a year after launch and more than 1.2 billion by October 2026. Whether those chats train the next model depends on the product terms.11

Drawn to scale against Llama 2’s pretraining text, the human preference tokens are almost invisible — about one token in 1,800 — yet they are what made the chat model.

4How much time

Three clocks run at once.

Crawl time. Common Crawl has been accumulating for ~17 years (2008–2025). A single monthly crawl is gathered over roughly two weeks. FineWeb’s 15T tokens rest on 96 of those snapshots — years of crawling, filtered in a much shorter compute burst.4,5

Human writing time. If a person produces on the order of 10³–10⁴ tokens of polished public text in a working day (a rough order-of-magnitude, not a measured census), then 15 trillion training tokens imply something like 10⁹–10¹⁰ person-days of prior writing — centuries of civilisation’s typing, not a labelling contract. That is why “just pay people to write more” cannot replace the scrape for pretraining volume.12

Training wall-clock. The GPU run that sees those tokens lasts days to months. Llama 3 405B’s public notes put the final pretrain on the order of months of cluster time; smaller open runs finish in days. The human centuries are front-loaded; the machine weeks are the bill that shows up in megawatt-hours on the Scaling and Factory pages.13

5How much money

The money splits into three pots, only one of which is well measured in public.

Licensing and acquisition. News, books and specialty corpora are bought or licensed; deals are mostly private. Common Crawl itself is free to download. The expensive part of “getting the web” is compute for cleaning, not the WARC fee.

Labelling and expert data. This is the visible market. Scale AI took just under $1 billion of revenue in 2025 after expecting about $2 billion; Surge AI reported $1.2 billion in 2024; Mercor claimed a $2 billion gross run-rate by mid-2026; micro1 crossed $1 billion run-rate talk in 2026. Meta’s June 2025 deal valued Scale at $29 billion for a 49% stake. Adding the latest public revenue figures for the big suppliers lands around $6–7 billion a year — within shouting distance of the Manhattan Project’s average yearly spend in 2025 dollars (~$8.5B), but about a fortieth of that project as a share of the economy.14 micro1’s CEO put the whole human-data market at $10–15 billion in late 2025; that is a vendor’s estimate, not a census.

Cleaning compute. Deduplicating and classifying petabytes is itself a GPU/CPU bill. Public papers rarely quote a dollar figure; treat it as “material but smaller than the pretrain” unless a lab says otherwise. Agent environments are a new line item: TechCrunch, citing The Information, reported Anthropic leaders discussing more than $1 billion on such environments over a year.16

6Where it goes

Data does not sit in a lake. It is filtered into a mix, burned into a checkpoint, specialised with a smaller high-touch set, then partially regenerated by the product itself.

Pretrain mix → checkpoint. This is where almost all the tokens, and almost all the training watts, are spent. Next-token prediction on web-scale text.

Post-train. SFT demonstrations, preference rankings, RL with graders. Tiny by token count; decisive for behaviour. Synthetic data (phi-1’s textbook stack; Nemotron-CC’s 1.9T synthetic tokens) sits here and in the mix — cheap, but recursive training on a model’s own output can collapse diversity unless fresh human data stays in the loop.17

Products and evals. Checkpoints become APIs, apps and agents. Eval sets are held out to measure, not train. Usage — when terms allow — re-enters as the flywheel below.

7The flywheel after release

Once a model is released, the loop closes. Users bring prompts and ratings; paid experts write, rank and grade to fix failures; the next model is better and draws more users. Agents add graded practice environments. Robots and first-person video cover what text cannot.

That loop is why a “data wall” on public human text is not automatically an end to growth — and why it is also not automatically a free lunch. Synthetic and usage data still need filtering, evaluation and fresh human judgement. Each extra useful token, however obtained, still wants training FLOPs, and those FLOPs still want watts.

Final words

The collection effort is civilisation-scale in the scrape, factory-scale in the labelling market, and cluster-scale in the training run. If human examples run out and synthetic data fails, demand saturates and the Act 1 path bends down. If the flywheel works, tokens per task and tasks per day can keep rising after attention caps. Either way, set the demand sliders on the index and read the bars. The Scaling page keeps the bill and the serving cost; this page keeps the examples.
