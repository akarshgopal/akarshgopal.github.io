The AI Factory: Chips, Cooling and the Grid

An Nvidia H100 is a slab of silicon the size of a postage stamp that draws 700 W, about as much as a microwave oven, and turns every one of those watts into heat. Put a hundred thousand of them in one building and you have the load of a small city. This page follows that electricity from the single chip to the grid: how much each layer adds, how the heat gets out, why the machines are never switched off, and what happens to a grid when they all move at once.

The short tour ended with the price of a GPU-hour and the size of today’s clusters. This page opens the building up. It stays with what is measured or published today; where a number is our own model, the figure says so.

1Seven hundred watts on a postage stamp

An Nvidia H100, the chip behind the training runs this series keeps returning to, Meta’s Llama 3 and xAI’s Colossus, is rated at 700 W.1 That is the power of a microwave oven, poured through a slab of silicon of 814 mm² — one die the size of a postage stamp. The chip is never alone: eight of them sit in a server with processors, memory, network cards and fans, and a DGX H100 draws up to 10.2 kW.2 Servers stack into racks, racks into halls, halls into a building.

Zoom out and two numbers move in opposite directions. The total climbs from 700 W to 150 MW, while the heat per square metre falls from about 860 kW at the die to about 2 kW over the whole building, because each level adds space around the chips: the server’s boards, the aisles between racks, the rest of the building. The hardest place to cool is the smallest. Notice too what happens per GPU. A DGX spends 1.275 kW for each of its 700 W chips; the rest feeds everything around the chip. Nvidia’s Blackwell racks are denser still: a GB200 NVL72 packs 72 GPUs, 36 processors and the switches that join them into one cabinet that draws about 120 kW.

Why each tick of that chip still costs energy, and why not all of it can run at full speed at once, is in The Switch.

2Getting the heat out

Every one of those watts leaves the chip as heat, and it has to be carried out of the building as fast as it arrives. For decades that was the job of air: cold air blown through the front of the rack, hot air out the back. Air is a poor carrier. Nvidia’s GB200 racks instead push liquid through manifolds to cold plates sitting on the CPUs and GPUs; one such rack draws about 120 kW, roughly three times the rack of four DGX servers above.3 In the figure, water runs through a channel over the die; slow the flow and the outlet warms by ΔT = P / (ṁ·c).

The numbers make the case alone. A litre of water stores about 3,500 times as much heat per degree as a litre of air, so a pipe a few centimetres wide does the work of a duct over a metre across. The heat still has to go somewhere: the warm water runs to a heat exchanger and from there to cooling towers or dry coolers outside, which is where much of a data centre’s water use comes from.

The maths: outlet rise

A mass flow ṁ of coolant with specific heat c, warming by ΔT, carries heat at

P = ṁ c ΔT, so ΔT = P / (ṁ c).

For water, c ≈ 4,180 J/(kg·K) and 1 L/min ≈ 0.0166 kg/s. A 700 W H100 at 1 L/min rises by about 10 °C; a 120 kW rack at 50 L/min rises by about 34 °C. The same ΔT in air needs roughly 3,500 times the volume flow, because water’s ρ c is that much larger per litre.

Follow the same die and plate one step further. Warm coolant leaves the plate for a CDU, a heat exchanger, and comes back cooled; that closed circuit is the technology cooling loop (TCS). On the far side of the exchanger a second loop of facility water (FWS) carries the heat outdoors to cooling towers or dry coolers and returns cooled in turn. That facility loop is still schematic here; the next figure sizes its share of the bill as PUE.

3The overhead

A GPU-hour is now a unit of trade, rented by the hour from cloud companies. A kilowatt-hour is the unit of the electricity series. Here is the exchange rate, worked on this page’s own numbers. An Nvidia DGX H100 server holds eight H100 chips and draws at most about 10.2 kW, so 1.275 kW for each GPU’s share of the chips, processors, memory, fans and network cards.2 The building adds cooling and power conversion on top — PUE from 1.09 (Google’s fleet) to 1.56 (Uptime survey average)7 — so at full draw one megawatt-hour runs between about 500 and 720 H100-hours. A training run of a hundred thousand GPU-hours needs roughly 140–200 MWh. At €100/MWh, the electricity for one GPU-hour is 14–20 cents; the chip’s share of a 2023 DGX, spread over four years, is about 85 cents an hour before the building.8 That is why AI companies buy chips first and electricity second — and why, once chips are plentiful, it is the electricity and the grid connection that run out.

Pumps, fans, chillers, lights and the losses in the building’s own transformers and backup systems all draw power too. The industry measures them with one ratio, power usage effectiveness or PUE: the electricity the whole site takes from the grid divided by what reaches the computers. Google reports a fleet average of 1.09; the Uptime Institute’s survey of operators puts the average at 1.56, unchanged for five years. In the figure, the energy that reaches the servers is blue and the overhead orange.7

Look inside: from the grid down to the die

A watt crosses a site transformer, a UPS, a server power supply and a voltage regulator before it reaches the silicon. Each step loses a few percent as heat. PUE is metered at the servers’ plugs, so only the transformer and UPS count as overhead; the power supply and regulator, the two biggest losses, are filed as IT load.7 The bulk of the overhead is cooling, which sits beside the chain rather than in it. Either way, “chip watts” understate what the meter sees.

The maths: GPU-hours per megawatt-hour

One GPU’s share of a DGX H100 is 10.2 kW / 8 = 1.275 kW. At load fraction u and power usage effectiveness PUE, each GPU-hour takes 1.275 × u × PUE kWh from the grid, so

GPU-hours per MWh = 1,000 / (1.275 × u × PUE).

At full load this gives 1,000 / (1.275 × 1.09) ≈ 720 for Google’s average and 1,000 / (1.275 × 1.56) ≈ 503 for Uptime’s.

The bands in the figure: overhead = 1,000 × (1 − 1/PUE) kWh; servers = 1,000/PUE; GPU chips = 1,000/PUE × 5.6/10.2.

4Why it never sleeps

The electricity series showed an electrolyser that earns its keep by running only in the cheapest hours. An AI data centre almost never does that, and the reason is the price of the chips. Spread over a working life, the hardware costs more per hour than the electricity it uses.

At the wholesale prices most of Europe pays most of the time, the electricity is a fraction of what the idle hardware loses. Pausing only pays for itself somewhere above €400–600 per MWh, prices that wholesale markets reach only in rare hours of scarcity. So the servers run day and night, and a training run of several months takes the same power at three in the morning as at six in the evening. To a grid that is both easy and hard: easy because it is predictable, hard because it never gives way when supply is short. What a flat 24-hour load does to a model day is in the electricity series’ grid-day figure.

The maths: the break-even price

A server of price S drawing P kW, written off over T hours, costs S / (P T) per kWh of its own consumption whether it runs or not. With S = $269,000, P = 10.2 kW and T = 4 × 8,760 h, that is $0.753 per kWh, or €666 per MWh at $1.13 to the euro. Running costs electricity price p times PUE per MWh of IT load, so stopping saves money only when p × PUE > 666, that is p > 666 / PUE: about €611 at PUE 1.09 and €427 at 1.56. The write-off is paid either way; pausing saves the electricity but throws away an hour of work that cost at least this much hardware to produce, so the comparison is between p × PUE and the value of that hour, of which €666 is a floor.

What a whole training run costs in hardware and electricity, at prices you set, is in Scaling.

5A load that breathes

“Day and night” does not mean steady. Training splits one model over thousands of GPUs, and after every step they must swap what they have learned before any of them can continue. All of them compute together, then all of them wait together. Meta’s report on Llama 3 describes what that looks like from outside: tens of thousands of GPUs raising or lowering their power at the same moment, for example while all of them wait for a checkpoint or for an exchange of results to finish, or when a whole job starts or stops, cause “instant fluctuations of power consumption across the data center on the order of tens of megawatts, stretching the limits of the power grid”.4 The same report notes a 1–2% daily rhythm in throughput: hotter middays made the GPUs lower their own clock speeds.

For a grid this is unusual. Households and factories switch on and off independently, and their millions of small changes average out. A training cluster is one load that changes all at once. In the electricity series we saw that every sudden imbalance between supply and demand shows up in the frequency; a step of tens of megawatts is small next to a large power station tripping, but it repeats every few seconds, and clusters are growing. Meta calls it an ongoing challenge. Possible remedies include batteries on site, keeping the GPUs busy while they wait, or limiting how fast the power may change.

Why thousands of chips must stop and exchange results in step, and how much time that takes, is worked through in Parallel: from one chip to thousands.

6Plugging in a city

Put many servers together and the numbers leave the scale of buildings. xAI’s Memphis cluster (100,000 H100s, September 2024) needed about 150 MW; the company ran gas turbines on site while it waited for a grid connection.5 Loads this size behave like power stations in reverse — see the electricity series’ frequency figure.

On 10 July 2024 a fault on a 230 kV line in northern Virginia dipped the voltage six times in 82 seconds. About 1,500 MW of data centres switched to backup at once; frequency rose to 60.047 Hz.9 NERC wrote it up as a warning: a block of load as large as a big plant can vanish in seconds.

In Ireland, data centres used 22% of metered electricity in 2024, up from 5% in 2015.10 Where the grid is slow, operators bring their own power. New loads join the same connection queues as wind and solar, and the same scarce transformers (Wood Mackenzie: 30% US shortfall expected in 2025).11 See why transformers are short and why new lines take a decade.

7Where the terawatt-hours go

The IEA counts about 415 TWh for all data centres in 2024 (~1.5% of world electricity) and about 485 TWh in 2025 (+17%).12 Small globally, concentrated: US 45%, China 25%, Europe 15%. Growth ~12%/yr since 2017. Base Case: ~945 TWh by 2030; accelerated AI servers ~30%/yr.

The IEA is explicit that these are scenarios and not forecasts: the spread for 2035, 700 to 1,700 TWh, is wider than the whole of today’s use. In its Base Case, data centres account for nearly half of the growth in US electricity demand to 2030. What decides which case happens is partly how good and how efficient the models get, and partly the subject of the last section: whether the grid can be built fast enough.

8What it adds up to

Follow the electricity through the building and each layer multiplies the one before. A 700 W chip becomes 1.3 kW once its server is counted, a little more again once the cooling is counted, and a hundred thousand of them make a load the size of a power station. Because the hardware is so expensive, that load runs flat out; because it trains in lockstep, it moves in steps of tens of megawatts; because it protects itself from voltage dips, it can disappear all at once.

Final words

From outside, an AI data centre looks like a warehouse with a large bill. Inside it is closer to one machine the size of a building, its pulse visible on the grid. The engineering is familiar; the question is scale and how fast concrete, copper and transformers can follow.

The short tour starts from a switch; this is where the switches end up. The electricity series is the other half.
