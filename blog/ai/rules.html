Take two light switches and two lamps, wire them the right way, and they add: flip both on and the lamp that means “two” lights up. A chatbot answering a question is the same idea, scaled by a factor of about 1015: billions of switches, flipping in a pattern that a program wrote down. This page asks what that pattern is, physically, before the rest of the series asks how much energy it costs.

It opens the physics stack of Act 1. The Yardstick set the bars; this page says what a computation is as switches. The Price of a flip prices one switch; A model asks what we are paying those flips to compute.

1A sum made of switches

Take two light switches and two lamps. Wire the first lamp so it lights when exactly one switch is on, and the second so it lights only when both are. Now read the switches as numbers, off is 0 and on is 1, and read the lamps as a two-digit answer.

Every case comes out right. 0 + 0 lights nothing. 1 + 0 lights the right-hand lamp. 1 + 1 lights only the left-hand one, which reads “10”: one two and no ones, in the counting system that uses only the digits 0 and 1, called binary. The two boxes are the first logic gates of this essay; engineers call them XOR (“exclusive or”) and AND.

In 1937 a 21-year-old Claude Shannon showed in his master’s thesis that any circuit of relays, switches flipped by electromagnets, could be described by the algebra of true and false that George Boole had written down in 1854, and that any such algebra could be built as relays.1 Arithmetic, comparison and memory are all just patterns of switches driving switches.

To add bigger numbers, copy the box. Each copy adds three bits, one from each number plus the carry from the column to its right, exactly as in long addition at school.

Two things already show. First, the answer is not instant: each column has to wait for the carry from its neighbour, so a wider adder is slower unless it is cleverer. Second, every change flips some of the little switches inside: two to five usually, and up to thirteen of the twenty when a carry ripples all the way along. Hold on to both. Speed and the number of flips will decide everything that follows.

The maths: binary and the full adder

A binary number with digits b3b2b1b0 means 8b3 + 4b2 + 2b1 + b0. One full adder takes bits a, b and an incoming carry c and produces

sum = a ⊕ b ⊕ c, carry = (a ∧ b) ∨ (c ∧ (a ⊕ b))

where ⊕ is XOR, ∧ is AND and ∨ is OR: two XORs, two ANDs and one OR, the five gates the figure counts.

2A switch that throws a switch

A light switch needs a hand; a relay needs only a current. Telephone exchanges were built from relays by the million. In his 1937 master’s thesis at MIT, Claude Shannon showed that circuits of relays obey the algebra of logic that George Boole had written down in 1854: contacts one after the other behave like AND, contacts side by side like OR.1 Wire two relays both ways below.

The important part is easy to miss: the keys never touch the lamp’s circuit. The lamp’s current comes from its own battery, and it could just as well feed the coils of other relays, so one switch can drive several more and logic can be stacked as deep as you like. Konrad Zuse’s Z3 of 1941 used about 2,600 relays this way.2 The catch is the lever. It has mass, so it takes milliseconds to move, and it wears.

Here is that stack as a mechanism: A’s contact throws B’s coil. No second key.

3Rules on a tape

An adder does one thing. The leap that made computers was a machine that could be told what to do. Charles Babbage proposed a mechanical Difference Engine to the Astronomical Society (later the Royal Astronomical Society) in 1822 and later designed a far more general Analytical Engine, programmed with punched cards; neither was finished in his lifetime.3 In 1843 Ada Lovelace published notes on the Analytical Engine that include a step-by-step method for computing Bernoulli numbers, usually counted as the first published program, and the observation that such a machine could manipulate symbols of any kind, not only numbers.3

In 1936 Alan Turing asked what it means for anything to be computable at all, and answered with a machine so simple it fits in a figure: a tape of squares, a head that reads and writes one square at a time, and a short table of rules.4

Turing’s point was that a single machine of this kind can read a description of any other such machine from its tape and imitate it. That is a general-purpose computer: the wiring stays fixed and the program is just data. Everything since, from ENIAC to the chip in a phone, is a faster version of this idea, with the tape replaced by memory.

Final words

A computation is a pattern of flips. Two switches make an adder; a chain of adders makes an arithmetic unit; a tape of rules makes a machine that can imitate any other. Nothing so far has said how much energy a flip costs, or why that cost stopped falling. That is the next page.
