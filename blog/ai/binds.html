What Binds, 2026–30: Chips, Power, Permits or Money?

Can AI outgrow the planet’s power grid? · a deep dive · the near-term constraints · by Akarsh Gopal, November 2026

Four companies plan to spend about $730 billion on data centres and chips in 2026, enough for about 19 GW of AI capacity in a year.1 Something has to deliver those gigawatts: chips, memory, buildings, power plants, wires, transformers, permits and the money itself. This page ranks them by how hard they bind between now and 2030, with the evidence for each, mostly from the United States, where nearly half of the world’s data-centre electricity is used.

The ranking is mine. The short version: energy is not the shortage; power at the right place and hour is. The United States is building more kilowatt-hours a year than its data centres will add, but the plant that can run at any hour, and the connections and transformers that bring it to a site, are booked years ahead. Chips and memory come next, and money last.

1Energy against power

Start with what the grid has to supply. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory estimates that US data centres used 176 TWh in 2023 and, in the reference case of its 2026 update, 649 TWh in 2030, with bounds of 521 and 843.2 That is about 68 TWh of new demand a year. The Energy Information Administration expects the country’s total electricity sales to grow from 4,135 TWh in 2026 to 4,211 TWh in 2027, by 76 TWh.3 On these numbers data centres are nearly all of America’s demand growth.

Now what is being built. Developers planned to add a record 86 GW of generating capacity in 2026: 43.4 GW of solar, 24 GW of batteries, 11.8 GW of wind and 6.3 GW of gas; 53 GW were added in 2025.4 Run at the average capacity factors of 2025, the solar, wind and gas would make about 159 TWh a year, more than twice what data centres add.5 In energy, the grid is keeping up.

At the peak the picture flips. Grid Strategies’ survey of utility forecasts has US peak demand growing 166 GW in five years, about 90 GW of it from data centres, or 18 GW a year.6 Against that, the 2026 build adds 6.3 GW of gas that can run at any hour and 24 GW of batteries that can cover a peak for a few hours. The 55 GW of solar and wind add energy but little power on a still winter evening. A data centre wants power every hour of the year, so it is the firm part of the build that it competes for, and the firm part is small.

The order is a judgement, and the evidence for the bottom of the table is the clearest. When the largest buyers report that spending is waiting on buildings and power, money is not what binds. The top is harder to prove, because a shortage of firm power shows up as delay and price rather than as a single number.

3Firm power and the connection

The quickest firm power is a gas turbine, and there are not enough of them. GE Vernova, the largest maker, ended the third quarter of 2026 with 122 GW of gas equipment in backlog and slot reservations, up from 116 GW a quarter earlier, and expects at least 130 GW by the end of the year; it shipped 5 GW in the quarter.7 At that rate the queue is six years long. Its executives have described the slots as mostly sold out through 2030.

So the largest sites build their own. xAI’s Colossus in Memphis received a permit in July 2025 for 15 gas turbines of up to 247 MW, and environmental lawyers counted more than twice as many on site before then; the first phase of the Stargate site at Abilene, Texas, was permitted with about 360 MW of its own gas generation.7 Generation behind the meter skips the interconnection queue, but not the turbine queue or the air permit.

Plants that do connect to the grid wait in line. At the end of 2025 about 8,200 projects, 1,312 GW of generation and 749 GW of storage, were waiting for an interconnection study in the United States, and the median project built in 2025 had spent more than five years between asking to connect and starting to operate.8 Only 13% of the capacity that asked to connect from 2000 to 2020 had been built by the end of 2025. The electricity series explains why studies take so long and who pays for the upgrades they find, in Planning, permitting and who pays.

Each new plant and each new campus also needs transformers. Wood Mackenzie estimated a 30% shortfall in the supply of large power transformers in the United States in 2025, with imports making up about 80% of supply.9 Why a transformer takes years to make is the subject of The transformer shortage in the electricity series, and what firm power costs to build is in What a power plant costs.

4Chips and memory

Chips bind too, but they have been growing faster than power. Epoch AI’s estimate of AI data-centre capacity, built from the chips sold, rose from 13.5 GW to 31 GW in 2025, a factor of 2.3.11 The tightest steps are packaging, which joins each GPU to its memory, and the memory itself. TSMC’s advanced packaging capacity is reported to grow by about 60% in 2026, and SK hynix has been reported as saying its high-bandwidth memory is effectively sold out for several years.10 Neither company publishes these figures directly, so they are the weakest evidence on this page. A chip without power is still a chip in a warehouse, and Microsoft’s report of delayed capacity suggests that this, not the reverse, was the more common problem in 2026.

5Money and permits

Money binds last, for now. The four largest buyers expect to spend about $730 billion in 2026, and Alphabet and Amazon expect to spend more in 2027.1 Microsoft lowered its 2026 outlook to about $170 billion because “some third-party capacity deliveries we expected in Q1 were delayed”: the money was ready before the buildings and the power were. That could change quickly if the revenue does not follow; The demand shows that the series’ grid ceiling for 2035 needs this spending to keep rising by about 8.5% a year.

Permits sit between. Air permits for on-site turbines, water for cooling, and local objections to campuses of a gigawatt or more slow individual sites, and they push developers towards places where power and permission are easier to get. They rarely stop the system as a whole.

Final words

This decade, AI’s growth is limited less by chips or money than by power that runs at every hour and can be connected where the data centre is. The United States is adding kilowatt-hours fast enough; it is not adding firm capacity, connections and transformers as fast as the money now committed would use them. The tripwire for this page is simple to check: if US data-centre use tracks LBNL’s reference case of about 650 TWh in 2030, the grid kept up; if it falls well short while spending stays high, power was the bind.
