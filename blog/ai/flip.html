Hold a glass bulb with three metal parts inside: a hot filament, a plate, and a mesh between them. A small voltage on the mesh steers a large current from filament to plate. That triode, invented in 1906, was the first switch with no moving parts. ENIAC would later pack 17,468 vacuum tubes into a room that drew 150 kilowatts.3 Today the same job is done by a transistor whose gate is a few dozen atoms long, and a chip the size of a postage stamp holds more of them than Earth has people. This page asks how cheap that flip got, and why it stopped getting cheaper.

The Rules page showed that two switches can add, and that a program is a pattern of flips. This page opens the switch itself, from the vacuum tube to 2 nm silicon, and hands the efficiency floor to the Act 1 meter.

1No moving parts

A vacuum tube switches a current without anything moving. A heated cathode boils electrons off into a vacuum; a positive plate collects them; and between the two a wire mesh, the grid, decides how many get through. Make the grid negative and it pushes the electrons back; in the tube below, about ten volts negative shuts the current off. Lee de Forest filed a patent on this three-electrode tube, the Audion, in 1907.3

A few volts on the grid control a current driven by the 250 V plate, so the tube is a switch and an amplifier with no moving parts. It switches in microseconds rather than milliseconds, a thousand times faster than a lever, and that is why the first electronic computers used tubes: Colossus at Bletchley Park from 1944, and ENIAC, with 17,468 tubes, in 1946. But the heater burns power whether the tube is switching or not, and a hot filament eventually fails. ENIAC drew about 150 kW, and the short tour works out that every addition cost it about 30 joules.4

The maths: the triode

Below saturation, the plate current of an idealised triode follows the Child–Langmuir law,

I ≈ K (Vg + Vp / μ)3/2,

where Vg is the grid voltage, Vp the plate voltage and μ the amplification factor. The current stops when Vg = −Vp/μ: with Vp = 250 V and μ = 20, at −12.5 V. A change of one volt on the grid does as much as a change of μ volts on the plate, which is why a tube amplifies.

2A switch made of silicon

The transistor replaced the hot cathode with a crystal. In the MOSFET, made at Bell Labs by Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng in 1959, a metal plate, the gate, sits on a thin layer of insulating glass above the silicon.5 (Since 2007 leading chips have used a hafnium-based insulator instead of glass, which leaks less at the same thinness.) Put a positive voltage on the gate and it draws electrons up to the surface, forming a thin conducting channel between the source and the drain. Remove the voltage and the channel closes. Nothing needs a heater, nothing moves, and the switch can be made very small.

Look inside: a gate is two transistors that are never both on

Pair an n-type transistor, which conducts when its gate is high, with a p-type, which conducts when it is low, and join their gates. A high input shuts the p-type and opens the n-type, which pulls the output to ground; a low input does the reverse. At rest one is always shut, so only a tiny leak flows. The real cost is the wire and next gate the output drives, a small capacitor. Each rise fills it from the supply, each fall dumps it to ground, and ½CV² turns to heat each time. The brief burst while both are half open adds only a little.

Every adder in the next sections is built from pairs like this.

The logarithmic plot holds the problem that ended the free lunch. Below its threshold voltage a transistor does not switch off cleanly; the current falls by a factor of ten for every 60 to 70 millivolts, and it cannot fall faster. That slope is set by the temperature of the electrons, not by engineering: at room temperature it can never be steeper than about 60 mV per decade, for any transistor that lifts electrons over a barrier (tunnelling designs that might beat it are still research).6 So the threshold has to sit a few hundred millivolts above zero to keep the “off” current small, and the supply voltage has to sit well above the threshold to make “on” strong. Lower the threshold to save voltage and the off-leak climbs tenfold with every step.

The maths: the subthreshold slope

Below threshold, electrons cross the barrier between source and channel by thermal energy, so the current follows a Boltzmann factor, I ∝ exp(q Vg / n k T). The gate voltage needed for a tenfold change, the subthreshold swing, is

S = n (k T / q) ln 10 ≈ n × 59.5 mV at 300 K,

with n ≥ 1 describing how well the gate controls the channel. The figure uses n = 1.2, giving 71 mV per decade.

3The price of a tick

The silicon gate could be made smaller with every generation, and the switches multiplied. In 1963 Frank Wanlass and Chih-Tang Sah of Fairchild presented CMOS, a pair of complementary transistors in which one is always off, so that no current flows except while switching.7 The energy then goes into filling and emptying the charge bucket of the short tour’s water analogy, ½CV² per flip. Here is that cost measured across fifty years of real processors, each dot one group of chips.

Look inside: how far a real operation is from kT ln 2

Erasing one bit cannot cost less than kT ln 2 ≈ 2.87×10⁻²¹ J at room temperature. One logic gate switching costs roughly a femtojoule, a few hundred thousand times that. A modern GPU FLOP, hundreds of such switches, sits a few hundred million times above it (≈7×10⁻¹³ J); a 64-bit DRAM read, about 5×10¹¹ times; a whole prompt, many more. The gap is the headroom left for the efficiency slider — large, but not infinite, and memory traffic eats most of it first.

Before 2005 the cost per transistor per tick fell by about a third every year; since then it has kept falling, more slowly. Before 2005 the voltage fell with every shrink, and since energy goes as the square of the voltage, that counted twice. After 2005 the voltage could hardly fall further, for the reason of the previous section, and only the bucket kept shrinking.

4When the voltage stopped falling

The short tour’s chart shows the result from outside: clock speeds stopped rising near 4 GHz and power flattened at about a hundred watts, while the transistor count kept doubling. Run the arithmetic forward and something strange happens. Each new generation still doubles the number of transistors on a chip of the same size, but each now uses only about 30% less power at the same clock, where 50% would be needed to keep the chip’s total power flat. At the same power, not all of them can be on at once: below, the lit cores are the ones the power budget allows.

This is dark silicon. A 2011 study by Hadi Esmaeilzadeh and colleagues projected that, at fixed power, 21% of a chip would have to stay off at the 22 nm node and more than half at 8 nm.9 That is gentler than the worst case above, because real chips still shaved their voltage a little and gave up clock speed, but the direction is the same.The industry’s answers are the ones that matter for AI. Spend the transistors on specialised circuits that do one job very efficiently and sit idle otherwise. Spend them on many slow, simple units instead of a few fast ones. And make the work itself cheaper per flip, by using fewer bits. The Parallel post shows how a GPU does all three.

The maths: power density after Dennard

Shrinking all lengths by k ≈ √2 per generation doubles the transistors per area and divides each transistor’s capacitance by k. With the voltage V and clock f fixed, the power per transistor, α C V² f, falls only by k, while the count rises by k². The power per area therefore grows by k per generation, and at a fixed power budget the active fraction falls as k−g after g generations: about 25% after four steps.

5Printing with light

Chips are printed like photographs. Light shines through a mask, a lens shrinks the pattern, and the image falls on a wafer coated with light-sensitive resist. Where the light lands the resist is washed away, and the silicon underneath can be etched or doped. The question is how fine a line a lens can print, and the answer was worked out for microscopes in the nineteenth century: the image of a sharp edge is blurred by about the wavelength of the light divided by the lens’s numerical aperture.

The industry’s path was to shorten the wavelength: from the 436 nm blue line of mercury lamps to 248 nm and 193 nm ultraviolet lasers, and then to fill the gap between lens and wafer with water, which raises the aperture above 1. The optics put a hard floor at about 36 nm for 193 nm light, and in production the best immersion scanner is specified at 38–40 nm;10 anything finer has to be split between two or more exposures. Extreme ultraviolet light at 13.5 nm, made by hitting droplets of molten tin with a laser, is so readily absorbed that it must travel in vacuum and be steered with mirrors. Only one company sells EUV machines: ASML in the Netherlands, with optics made by Zeiss in Germany. Its high-NA tools, with an aperture of 0.55, print lines down to 8 nm.10

Which brings us to the name. TSMC in Taiwan began volume production of its “2 nm” process, N2, in late 2025,11 but nothing on such a chip is 2 nm wide. Node names began drifting away from any physical feature in the late 1990s and are now labels for a generation; researchers have proposed counting transistors per square millimetre instead.12

The maths: the resolution limit

The smallest half-pitch (the width of one line or gap) a lens of numerical aperture NA can print with wavelength λ is usually written

half-pitch = k₁ λ / NA,

where k₁ depends on the illumination, the mask and the resist. Even with the best off-axis illumination the image contrast falls to zero at k₁ = 0.25, the limit the figure uses, and no single exposure can print finer. For 193 nm immersion at NA 1.35 that gives about 36 nm, against the 38 nm (k₁ ≈ 0.27) and 40 nm (k₁ ≈ 0.28) that ASML specifies for production; for EUV at NA 0.33, about 10 nm; at NA 0.55, about 6 nm. Factories work at k₁ of roughly 0.3 or more.

That mask image does not paint the whole wafer at once. It lands in one exposure field — about 26 by 33 mm on ASML’s tools — and the stage steps. An H100-sized die nearly fills that window, which is the bridge into yield.

6Why big chips cost so much

A wafer 300 mm across is printed with as many copies of a chip as fit. A speck of dust or a flaw in the crystal anywhere in a chip can ruin it, so the chance that a chip works falls with its area. The largest chips are near the largest area a lithography tool can expose at once, about 26 by 33 mm; Nvidia’s H100 is 814 mm². High-NA EUV tools expose only half that field, 26 by 16.5 mm, so a chip that large would have to be stitched together from two exposures.10 Below, blue dies work and orange ones caught a defect.13

Yield falls exponentially with area, so a chip twice the size costs more than twice as much per working copy. The way out is to make big chips from small ones: chiplets, tested separately and joined in one package. Nvidia’s Blackwell GPUs are two dies, each as large as a lithography tool can print, joined by a 10 TB/s link so that they act as one.14

The maths: Poisson yield

If defects land at random with an average density D per unit area, the number in a die of area A is Poisson-distributed with mean AD, and the chance it has none is

Y = e−AD.

At D = 0.1 per cm², a 1 cm² die works 90% of the time and an 8 cm² die 45% of the time. Splitting the large die into four 2 cm² chiplets gives each an 82% chance, and any four good ones can be combined.

Poisson is the pessimistic case for big dies: real defects cluster, so fabs use negative-binomial or Murphy models, which predict higher yields for large chips at the same average density.

7Smaller, until it got hot

The switch went from a grid steering electrons in a vacuum, to a gate pulling a channel open in silicon. Each step was faster, smaller and cheaper per flip, and for thirty years the shrinking came with a falling voltage that made every flip cheaper by the square. When the voltage hit the floor set by the leak, size kept falling but heat became the limit.

That is the turn this series hangs on. If more transistors can no longer run faster, they have to run in parallel, and do work that needs as little energy, and as few trips to memory, as possible.

Final words

Somewhere in the device showing you this page, billions of these switches are flipping billions of times a second. None of them clicks, and each one’s gate is only a few dozen atoms long. Yet each still does exactly what the relays of Claude Shannon’s 1937 switching theory1 and Konrad Zuse’s 1941 Z3 computer2 did: one signal decides whether another gets through. Everything else in this series, the multiplying, the learning and the buildings full of heat, is that single act repeated at a scale nobody in 1937 could have pictured.

The Parallel post follows the turn into the GPU. On the index meter, this page sets the efficiency floor: how cheap a flip can get, and when that cheapness stops.
