Hold a 100-watt lamp lit for a little under nine seconds. That is 0.24 Wh — Google’s median Gemini text prompt at the data-centre meter (idle chips and cooling included).1 Scale by today’s inferred volume and you get my estimate of AI inference: 55 TWh/yr (anchor band 40–80), ~0.2% of world electricity.2

That is small. The question is whether it stays small: chips get cheaper every year, and cheaper answers invite more of them — longer ones, and machines asking machines. Somewhere between a lamp and a continent the grid, not the chip, decides how much AI can happen.

The series tries the question as a case you can falsify. The pages that follow are evidence for the meter’s sliders; this page is the live verdict.

1Two questions, one machine

Act 1 asks: will AI inference become humanity’s largest energy expenditure? That sentence hides choices, so it is pinned down. “Inference” means serving trained models to users and agents, measured at the data-centre meter. “Largest” is tested on three rungs, each with a bar that today’s numbers can check: R1, at least a tenth of the world’s electricity by 2035; R2, bigger than any other single use of electricity by 2050; R3, bigger than any other single use of energy at all, fuels included, by 2050.

Act 2 asks the sharper version of a popular claim: is orbital compute the only answer to a hundred times more AI compute? Power times efficiency is compute, so any multiple of today’s AI turns into a number of gigawatts, and the question becomes who can supply them: the grid, orbit, or neither.

2Act 1: will inference become the biggest use of energy?

The meter is a range, not a point, for 2035 and 2050 against three bars. The orange band is inference electricity; the black lines are the bars. Five sliders — each a later page — set demand, tokens per task, efficiency, power built, and how much other uses grow.

On this path the meter fills the numbers: 2035 and 2050 energy, the three bars, and YES / NO / UNRESOLVED for each rung. Move a slider or a Low / Mid / High preset and this paragraph updates with it.

The meter cannot predict. Two tripwires keep the case falsifiable soon: 300 TWh of inference by 2030 and 1,000 by 2035. Miss them and R1–R3 are effectively dead under this model. For scale, the IEA’s 2035 range for all data centres is 700 to 1,700 TWh.4

3Act 2: what if the grid can’t?

Compute = power × efficiency. So 100× today’s AI ≠ 100× today’s power: at 40%/yr for a decade, (1.4)10 ≈ 28.9× more compute per watt → P = 31.2 × 100 / 28.9 ≈ 108 GW (~3.5× today’s AI data-centre capacity).6 Move the multiple and the efficiency below; watch where the grid and then orbit run out.

At 100× by 2035 the answer is a plain no: the grid on its present trend gets there without orbit. The claim bites at a thousand or ten thousand times — and there orbit is limited in tonnes (a hundred gigawatts is over a million tonnes; the FAA Starship cap is 145 launches a year).8 Try the launches slider under More assumptions.

Cost is two numbers: dollars per kilogram to orbit, and kilograms of satellite per kilowatt delivered. Together they set a floor on orbital power.

On launch alone, orbit clears the terrestrial band ($0.065–0.34/kWh) only near $100–200/kg and ≲ 25 kg/kW. Neither end of that joint has been demonstrated; the full cost is higher. SpaceX’s own stock filing says space data centres “may not be commercially viable”.10

4A coda: one star

Bookend: Earth intercepts 1.74×10⁵ TW of sunlight; the Sun emits ~2×10⁹× more. A computer on a meaningful slice of that is a star-mind, not a data centre. Nothing in the verdicts above depends on it. Working-out: Orbital · star-mind.

6Where to start

If you have an hour, take the short tour: switches, learning, scale and power in one sitting. If you want the physics of why efficiency gains slow, read Rules, then The price of a flip, then Parallel. If you want the demand side, A model, Language and Scaling. If you want the supply side, The AI factory. Each is written to be read alone. For how the grid itself works, the sister series How the World’s Electricity Works covers the transformer shortage and who gets to plug in.

For who else wants the cheap kilowatt-hours that data centres buy around the clock, see Cheap demand in the electricity series.

What this page is not. Not a forecast. M0 = 55 TWh is DERIVED (IEA × growth × a secondary 60% inference share); band 40–80. A factor-of-two miss does not flip Mid’s NO — the bars are 70–300× away. Verdicts do move with demand and efficiency; those defaults are my calibration. WORLD_ELEC_2050 = 60,000 TWh is my assumption for %-of-world readouts only — it is not a bar. Where something is an assumption, ⓘ data says so.

Final words

Two questions, five sliders, three bars. Under Mid assumptions Act 1 is a clear NO on every rung, and Act 2’s 100× by 2035 fits inside the grid without orbit. The interesting region is the joint tail: demand and tokens raised together, efficiency gains slowing, or a multiple far past a hundred. The tripwires — 300 TWh by 2030, 1,000 TWh by 2035 — keep the case falsifiable before 2050. Open the map, pick a card, and move one lever until the meter flinches.

For the condensed tour of the same ground, start at From Switch to Supercluster. For the ledger that freezes Low, Mid and High, see The Verdict.
