Can AI outgrow the planet’s power grid?

AI’s electricity is still small. By this series’ estimate, serving trained models to people and software used about 55 TWh in 2025, a fifth of one per cent of the world’s electricity; all AI data centres together, training included, perhaps twice that.1 It is also the fastest-growing load on the grid. Google handles seven times as many tokens as it did a year ago.2 Four companies plan to spend about $730 billion on data centres and chips in 2026, enough for about 19 GW of AI capacity in one year, when the world had 31 GW at the end of 2025.3 In the United States, data centres alone may add about as much demand each year to 2030 as the whole country’s electricity use is growing by now.4

So the question this series asks is a near-term one: this decade, can the grid connect the power that AI is buying? The evidence so far points one way. Energy is not the shortage: the United States is adding more kilowatt-hours a year than data centres will need, mostly from solar. Firm power is. Data centres run around the clock, and the gas turbines, transformers and grid connections that supply power at every hour are booked years ahead. What binds ranks the constraints with the evidence.

The long-run question, whether AI becomes humanity’s largest use of energy, is the bookend, not the lead. Today’s inference is 70 to 300 times smaller than the bars that would make it so, and on the series’ middle assumptions it stays well short of them; the Verdict keeps that case open with tripwires that can be checked by 2030.

1Two questions, one axis

The series asks two questions and measures them differently. Act 1 asks whether inference becomes the largest use of energy, and counts energy: terawatt-hours of inference a year. Act 2 asks whether orbital compute is the only way to supply a hundred times today’s AI, and counts capacity: gigawatts of AI data centres, training included. Turn capacity into energy, gigawatts × load × 8,760 hours, and the two can be read on one axis.

Read this way the two Acts describe one world. In 2025 Act 1’s 55 TWh of inference sits inside Act 2’s capacity, which at half its rating is about 100 TWh of all AI work, inside the IEA’s 485 TWh for every data centre. In 2035 Act 1’s Mid path reaches 486 TWh, about two-fifths of what Act 2’s grid ceiling of 316 GW delivers at that load. So the Mid path does not assume a grid that cannot be built; it assumes that inference takes a share of the power AI gets, with training and everything else taking the rest. The weak joint is the ceiling itself. It grows 26% a year, slower than the money now being spent would grow it in 2026, but faster than flat spending would sustain (The demand).

Orbit enters only past the ceiling. At 100× today’s compute by 2035, chips that improve 40% a year need about 108 GW, a third of the ceiling, so the grid suffices; the claim bites at a thousand times or more, where launch mass and cost decide it. Orbital works through the physics, and the Verdict holds the ceiling meter.

2The long run, in brief

Act 1 is pinned to three bars: at least a tenth of the world’s electricity by 2035 (about 3,800 TWh), more than any other single use of electricity by 2050 (a working bar of 6,000 TWh, set by space cooling), and more than any other single use of energy, fuels included, by 2050 (about 17,200 TWh, set by heating).6 On Low, Mid and High settings the answer is no on all three, with one unresolved under High. Two tripwires make that falsifiable early: 300 TWh of inference by 2030 and 1,000 TWh by 2035. The meter, built up one lever at a time, and the ledger are on the Verdict page.

4Where to start

If you have an hour, take the short tour: switches, learning, scale and power in one sitting. For the near-term question, read The demand, then The AI factory, then What binds. For why chips stop getting more efficient as fast, read Rules, The price of a flip and Parallel. For what a model is and why it grew, A model, Language and Scaling. Each page is written to be read alone. For how the grid itself works, the sister series Why Did Electricity Stop Getting Cheaper? covers planning, permits and the queue to connect, the transformer shortage and what power plants cost.

What this page is not. Not a forecast. The 55 TWh is derived, from the IEA’s data-centre total, a year’s growth and a 60% inference share taken from a secondary source; its band is 40–80 TWh, and the token counts companies publish explain only 3 to 8 TWh of it (The demand). Where something is an assumption, the figure’s ⓘ data panel says so.

Final words

AI’s electricity is small and fast-growing, and the money behind it is now large enough that the grid, not the chip, sets the pace this decade. Over the long run the series finds no path to AI becoming the largest use of energy without many levers moving together. The near-term case is the one to watch: whether firm power, connections and transformers keep up with what four companies are spending. Start with The demand or What binds, or open the map and pick a card.
