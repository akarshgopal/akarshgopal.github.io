Lift the cooler off an Nvidia H100 and you find a slab of silicon of 814 mm² (about the largest a factory can print), with stacks of memory chips pressed up against its sides. Almost everything it does for a neural network is multiply numbers and add up the results. That sounds like a job for the fastest possible multiplier, but it is not. Multiplying is cheap; fetching the numbers is expensive. The machines that won AI are the ones arranged to fetch each number once and use it many times.

The Switch post ended with a chip that has more transistors than it can power at once, and the short tour with Amdahl’s rule that many workers help only if the job can be split. This page follows the split.

1A layer is a matrix multiply

One neuron multiplies each of its inputs by a weight and adds the results, a dot product. A layer is many neurons reading the same inputs, and a batch is many inputs going through the same layer. Put the inputs in rows and the weights in columns and the whole calculation is one matrix multiply: every output is one row times one column.

The count hides the most important fact on this page. Doubling the size of the matrices multiplies the arithmetic by eight but the data by only four. Big matrix multiplies do thousands of operations for every byte they read. If a machine can keep those numbers close at hand and reuse them, it can keep its arithmetic busy; if it has to fetch them again for every operation, it will spend its time waiting.

2The roof and the slope

A chip has two speed limits: how fast it can do arithmetic, and how fast it can read from memory. Which one binds depends on the job’s arithmetic intensity, its operations per byte. In 2009 Samuel Williams, Andrew Waterman and David Patterson at Berkeley drew this as a roofline: a sloped line for memory, a flat roof for arithmetic.1

Every chip here needs a few hundred operations per byte before its arithmetic is the limit. An H100 can do 989 trillion 16-bit operations a second but read only 3.35 trillion bytes, a ratio of about 295. Training, with its large matrix multiplies over big batches, sits on the roof. Answering one user at a time does not: each new word needs every weight read once, so the chip waits on memory. That is why serving many users in one batch is so much cheaper per user, and why memory bandwidth has become the specification buyers watch.

The maths: the roofline

A kernel with arithmetic intensity I (operations per byte read from memory) on a chip with peak arithmetic rate P and memory bandwidth B can run at most at

attainable = min( P, B × I ).

The corner, the ridge point, is at I = P / B. A matrix multiply of n × n matrices in 16-bit numbers does 2n³ operations on 6n² bytes, an intensity of n / 3, which passes the H100’s ridge of about 295 once n exceeds about 900.

3Passing numbers hand to hand

How do you actually reuse a number? In 1978 H. T. Kung and Charles Leiserson at Carnegie Mellon proposed the systolic array: a grid of simple cells, each of which multiplies the two numbers it receives, adds the product to its own total and passes the numbers on to its neighbours, like a heart pumping blood.3 Inputs enter from the edges, staggered in time, and every number is used once by every cell it passes. No cell ever goes back to memory. Google’s first Tensor Processing Unit, in its data centres from 2015, built its matrix unit as a 256 × 256 systolic array of 65,536 multiply-add cells working on 8-bit numbers.4 Where the 4 × 4 array above uses each number it reads four times, an array that size uses each one 256 times. Nvidia added similar matrix units, which it calls Tensor Cores, to its GPUs from the V100 of 2017. Look back at the roofline: every TPU and GPU on it is mostly these arrays, surrounded by the memory that feeds them.

4Thousands of threads

Even with reuse, numbers have to come from memory sometimes, and memory is far away in clock cycles: a read from the chip’s main memory takes hundreds of cycles to return. A CPU core fights that delay with large caches and clever guessing about what it will need next. A GPU takes a blunter approach. It keeps many groups of threads on each core (Nvidia calls a group of 32 threads a warp) and, whenever one group is waiting, it switches to another that is ready, at no cost. Below, each group’s blue ticks are the cycles it computes; the long gaps are waits for memory.

That is why a GPU holds tens of thousands of threads at once, and why it is so good at work with no grey in Amdahl’s picture: drawing pixels, or the matrix multiplies of a neural network. When Nvidia released CUDA in 2007, the same trick became available for any arithmetic, and in 2012 two gaming GPUs trained the network that won ImageNet (the short tour tells that story).

5Fewer bits

The Switch post showed that every flip costs energy. A multiplier for 32-bit numbers is a large block of switches; one for 8-bit numbers is far smaller, because its size grows roughly with the square of the number of digits. Neural networks, it turned out, do not need 32-bit precision. Their weights are nudged by noisy gradients anyway, and an error of a fraction of a per cent is lost in the noise. Training moved to 16-bit numbers in the late 2010s, and inference and parts of training have since moved to 8 and 4 bits.5

With 4 bits the line has only fifteen values, and big numbers do not fit at all. That is why low-precision formats are used with a separate scale factor for each small block of numbers, so that the few values available sit where the numbers are. The pay-off is direct: on the H100, 8-bit arithmetic runs twice as fast as 16-bit, and on the B200 4-bit runs four times as fast, about 9 thousand trillion operations a second.2

6The cost of distance

Here is the reason behind all of the above, in picojoules, drawn on a package map. In 2014 Mark Horowitz of Stanford tabulated the energy of basic operations on a 45 nm chip. Adding two numbers cost a fraction of a picojoule. Reading a number from memory on another chip cost hundreds. On the map a number travels from HBM (still DRAM dies beside the logic die) through SRAM to a register beside the arithmetic; the bars give each step’s cost, to scale.7

Look inside: memory is a ladder, and every rung costs more

Between the multiplier and the DRAM sit rungs of small, fast memory on the chip itself. Each rung is bigger than the one below, and a bigger memory means longer wires and more to switch, so every read costs more. A few kilobytes cost two or three times the arithmetic; a megabyte, about twenty-five times; going off the chip, hundreds of times. Keep what you are working on on a low rung and the chip stays efficient.

Moving one number from off-chip memory costs over a hundred times as much as multiplying it, so how often each fetched number is reused, not how fast the multiplier is, decides how efficient a chip is. An accelerator is designed around distance. The systolic array keeps numbers in neighbouring cells; large on-chip memories keep the working set close; and the main memory itself has moved next to the processor. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) stacks DRAM chips on top of each other and places them beside the processor in the same package, joined by thousands of short wires. An H100 reads its 80 GB of HBM3 at 3.35 TB/s.2 Three companies make HBM: SK hynix and Samsung in South Korea and Micron in the United States.

7More than one chip

The largest models do not fit on one chip, and training them on one would take centuries. So the work is spread over thousands, and the cost of distance returns at a larger scale. In the simplest arrangement every GPU works on a different slice of the data and, after each step, all of them must add up their gradients so they stay in step. That exchange travels over links far slower than a chip’s own memory: 900 GB/s between the eight GPUs in a server over Nvidia’s NVLink, and 400 Gb/s, about 50 GB/s, from one server to another. Below, each step splits into computing and exchanging.2

Computing time shrinks with every GPU added; the exchange does not, so it ends up being most of each step. This is Amdahl’s grey from the short tour again, appearing as a network. It is why AI clusters are built around their networks, why GPUs are sold in tightly linked racks of 72, and why, in the AI Factory, thousands of GPUs pause together and the grid sees the power swing.

The maths: the cost of an all-reduce

In a ring all-reduce, each of N GPUs sends and receives 2(N − 1)/N times the gradient size G over a link of bandwidth B, so the exchange takes about

tcomm ≈ 2 (N − 1) G / (N B),

which approaches 2G/B however many GPUs join. For 7 billion 16-bit gradients, G = 14 GB, that is about 0.03 s over NVLink and 0.56 s over a 50 GB/s network. The computing, 6 × parameters × tokens divided by the cluster’s speed, falls as 1/N.

8Why GPUs won

A GPU was built to do the same arithmetic on millions of independent pixels, with thousands of threads to hide the wait for memory. Neural networks turned out to be the same kind of job, matrix multiplies with no grey, and the GPU’s makers then added what made them better at it: matrix units that pass numbers hand to hand, fewer bits per number, and memory stacked beside the chip. None of this needed faster switches. It needed switches arranged so that data travels as little as possible.

Final words

It is a slightly comic history. The chips that now train the largest models were built so that games could draw dragons and racing cars a little faster, and they won because a neural network turned out to look, to the hardware, exactly like a screen full of pixels. The cleverness that remains is mostly about not moving things: numbers handed from cell to cell, memory stacked right beside the chip, a few bits where thirty-two used to be. The fastest computers ever built are, in the end, very carefully arranged to stay still.

Once the arithmetic was cheap and parallel, the remaining question was how much of it to buy. The Scaling post follows the discovery that the answer is predictable, and the Learning post shows why attention fits this hardware so well: it is matrix multiplies all the way down.
