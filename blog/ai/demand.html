The Demand: Who Asks, How Often?

In May 2026 Google said it was processing more than 3.2 quadrillion tokens a month across its products, seven times as many as a year earlier.1 Tokens are the unit of AI demand, and a few companies count them in public. This page adds up those counts, turns them into electricity and checks the total against the series’ top-down estimate of 55 TWh of inference in 2025. Then it follows the money: what four companies’ capital spending in 2026 buys in gigawatts.

The answer to the first sum is that published tokens explain only a small part of AI’s electricity, between a sixteenth and a seventh of it. The answer to the second is that the money now committed grows AI’s capacity faster than this series’ grid ceiling assumes, for as long as it keeps rising.

1Counting tokens

Three companies publish counts of the tokens they process. Google’s grew from 9.7 trillion a month in May 2024 to about 480 trillion a year later, more than 1.3 quadrillion by October 2025 and more than 3.2 quadrillion in May 2026, summed over Search, the Gemini app, its cloud customers and everything else.1 Its model APIs alone were handling nearly 32 billion tokens a minute by October 2026, four times as many as a year before.2 OpenAI’s API was serving 6 billion tokens a minute in October 2025, and ChatGPT was receiving about 2.5 billion prompts a day in July 2025.3 Microsoft processed 50 trillion tokens in March 2025.4 Nobody else publishes a number that can be added to these.

To turn tokens into energy the series uses one rate. Google’s median Gemini text prompt used 0.24 Wh at the data-centre meter in May 2025, idle machines and cooling included; taking that prompt as about 400 tokens, which is my conversion because Google gave no token count, makes 0.6 mWh a token. Epoch AI’s independent estimate for ChatGPT, about 0.3 Wh for a reply of 500 tokens, gives the same 0.6 mWh.5

Google’s count for calendar 2025 has to be pieced together. Growing smoothly at the rate between its two I/O figures, it sums to about 8.4 quadrillion tokens over the year, a little low if anything, since that path reaches only 1.1 quadrillion a month in October against Google’s reported 1.3. At 0.6 mWh that is 5.1 TWh. OpenAI’s API at its October rate for a whole year is 1.9 TWh, an upper bound because the rate was lower earlier in the year; ChatGPT’s prompts at OpenAI’s own 0.34 Wh each are 0.31 TWh; Microsoft at its March rate is 0.36 TWh. Together, about 7.6 TWh, a seventh of the top-down 55.

2Reading is cheaper than writing

Most of those tokens are read, not written. On OpenRouter, a marketplace that routes requests to hundreds of models, the average request in late 2025 carried about 6,000 tokens of prompt and got back about 400.6 A model reads its whole prompt in one parallel pass, which keeps the chip’s arithmetic busy; it writes one token at a time, and each one needs every weight fetched from memory again, as Scaling shows. Reading is therefore cheaper per token.

How much cheaper has not, as far as I can find, been measured for a production service. Epoch’s estimates give a working figure: a query with 10,000 tokens of input uses about 2.5 Wh against 0.3 Wh for a short one, so each token read adds about 0.22 mWh, a little over a third of a written token.5 That ratio is my arithmetic on a model, not a measurement. Count reads at that rate, with 94% of tokens read, and the published tokens come to about 3.3 TWh in 2025.

3What the count misses

A gap of more than six-sevenths is not an error to be closed. It is a list of what token counts leave out, and each item on it is large.

The largest is probably the AI nobody calls AI. At Meta, recommendation models were “up to 79%” of the AI inference cycles in its data centres when its engineers last published a share, and its 2025 paper on its own inference chip still calls them “the majority of inference workloads at Meta”.7 They rank feeds, videos and ads for billions of people, and they are not counted in tokens. Then come the providers that publish no counts at all: Anthropic, xAI, Meta’s assistant, and the Chinese and open-model services. Then images and video, which are billed in frames and denoising steps rather than tokens (§4). Last, capacity held in reserve: chips kept free for peaks and new launches, which the top-down figure counts and a token count does not.

The gap could also mean that the top-down figure is too high. It rests on two shares that nobody publishes directly, a 15% accelerated-server share of data-centre electricity and a 60% inference share, the second from a secondary source. Halve the inference share and the top-down figure halves to about 28 TWh; published tokens would still explain less than a third of it.

What the count settles is narrower but firm. Chatbot and API text, counted generously, was between a sixteenth and a seventh of AI inference’s electricity in 2025. It is the fastest-growing part: at its May 2026 rate Google’s count alone is 23 TWh a year counted as written and about 9 TWh with reads discounted, against about 77 TWh on Act 1’s Mid path for 2026. 4Tokens per task

The ladder below sets the scale of one task. Its bottom rung is Google’s 0.24 Wh median prompt.5 The IEA says reasoning, agents and video can use “hundreds or thousands of times” more energy per task;8 the three upper rungs are order-of-magnitude estimates that scale energy with the same multiple as tokens. That is the weak point: video and tool use break the link between tokens and energy. One measured rung, three labelled estimates. The ladder sets the demand sliders of the long-run meter on the Verdict page.

Why watt-hours are not tokens

The meter’s tokens-per-task slider is a multiple of today’s. The ladder’s upper rungs set it from a multiple of the chat rung’s token count, then show energy as 0.24 Wh times that multiple. That is a labelled approximation, not a measurement. A reasoning pass can spend more arithmetic per token on search and self-checking; a short video is made of frames and diffusion steps, not tokens; an agent spends energy on the tools it calls. When the IEA says “hundreds or thousands of times” more energy per task, it means energy, not tokens. Use the ladder for orders of magnitude, and treat its energy column on the estimated rungs as a sketch.

5From dollars to gigawatts

Tokens show the demand that exists. Money shows the capacity being built for the demand that is expected. In their latest guidance, given at the end of October 2026, the four largest cloud companies expect to spend about $730 billion of capital in calendar 2026: Alphabet $195–205 billion, Amazon about $220 billion, Microsoft about $170 billion and Meta $135–145 billion.9 Most of it is for AI. Microsoft says three-quarters of its latest quarter’s spending went on short-lived assets such as GPUs and CPUs; Alphabet says about 65% of its technical-infrastructure spending was servers and networking and the rest data-centre buildings.9

Epoch AI puts the up-front cost of a one-gigawatt AI data centre at about $38 billion: $21 billion of servers, $11 billion for the building with its power and cooling, $5 billion of networking, and under half a billion for land and the utility connection.10 The chain is then a division. $730 billion ÷ $38 billion per gigawatt ≈ 19 GW in one year, if all of it bought AI data centres. Epoch estimates the world’s AI data-centre capacity at 31 GW at the end of 2025, up from 13.5 GW a year earlier.11 Four companies’ 2026 spending alone would grow it by about 60%, more than twice the 26% a year that Act 2 assumes on its way to a grid ceiling of 316 GW in 2035.

The test also runs the other way. Held flat in dollars, the same spending adds the same 19 GW each year, so growth in percentage terms slows; by 2035 it reaches about 220 GW, short of Act 2’s ceiling. To reach 316 GW the four companies’ spending must keep rising by about 8.5% a year, to roughly $1.5 trillion a year by 2035. Alphabet already expects its capital spending to “increase significantly in 2027”, and Amazon expects it to rise too.9 Act 2’s ceiling is a statement about money as much as about the grid; What binds asks which runs out first.

6When cheaper means more

Google reported a 33× drop in energy per Gemini text prompt in a year (May 2024 to May 2025), while Google’s own data-centre electricity rose 27% in 2024 and its total electricity another 37% in 2025.12 Over roughly the same span its token count grew fiftyfold. That is consistent with the Jevons shape, cheaper answers inviting more of them, but it is not proof of it: the extra electricity also went to training, new products and everything else Google runs, and nobody has published the elasticity for the same service. The meter’s efficiency and tokens sliders pull opposite ways on purpose.

Electricity is a small part of the cost of a token. In Epoch’s cost model of a one-gigawatt data centre, energy is about 7% of the yearly cost of ownership and servers about 60%.10 So a fall in the price of electricity barely changes the price of a token, and a fall in the price of chips changes it a great deal. Very cheap tokens are tokens bought in bulk, increasingly by software rather than by people: human attention caps prompts per person, and growth past that cap needs agents that ask on our behalf, or it fails to find a use that pays.

For the cheap hours that both data centres and synthetic-fuel plants want to buy, see Cheap demand in the electricity series.

Final words

Count the tokens and AI’s best-known demand turns out to be a small part of its electricity: a few terawatt-hours in 2025 against an estimated 55 for all inference. Count the dollars and the capacity being built is growing faster than this series’ grid ceiling assumes, as long as the spending keeps rising. Neither count changes the long-run verdict on the Verdict page, where the bars are hundreds of times away. Both bear on the near-term question: whether the grid can connect what the money is buying. That is the subject of What binds.
