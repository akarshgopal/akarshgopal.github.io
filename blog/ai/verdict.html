This page holds the series’ long-run case. Act 1 asks whether AI inference becomes humanity’s largest use of energy; Act 2 asks whether orbital compute is the only way to supply a hundred times today’s AI. Here the meter is built up one lever at a time, three settings (Low, Mid and High) are frozen in a ledger, and the tripwires that would overturn the verdict are written down. It is a ledger, not a forecast. Every default is labelled.

1Act 1: the three rungs

Act 1’s question, will AI inference become humanity’s largest energy expenditure?, hides choices, so it is pinned down. “Inference” means serving trained models to people and software, measured at the data-centre meter. “Largest” is tested on three rungs, each with a bar that today’s numbers can check: R1, at least a tenth of the world’s electricity by 2035, about 3,800 TWh; R2, more than any other single use of electricity by 2050, a working bar of 6,000 TWh set by space cooling; R3, more than any other single use of energy at all, fuels included, by 2050, about 17,200 TWh set by heating buildings and water.12

The starting point is the series’ estimate of inference in 2025, 55 TWh (band 40–80). Nobody publishes it, so it is derived: about 15% of the IEA’s 415 TWh of data-centre electricity in 2024 went to accelerated servers; a year’s growth and the building’s overhead multiply that by about 1.5; and 60% of it, a share taken from a secondary source, was inference.3 Counting the tokens that Google, OpenAI and Microsoft publish gives only 3 to 8 TWh; the rest is recommendation and ads, unpublished providers, images and video, and reserve (The demand). Today’s 55 TWh is 70 times smaller than R1 and 300 times smaller than R3.

The model is one line. Inference energy in a year is 55 TWh × (growth in people and tasks × growth in tokens per task), raised to a power that climbs from 0 in 2025 to 1 in 2050, fast at first and then saturating; divided by the efficiency gained so far, where the yearly gain halves every twelve years; and capped by the power the world builds for inference, at 8.76 TWh a year per gigawatt. Below it is built one lever at a time.

Mid is calibrated to land near the IEA’s Base Case. The chain: accelerated servers were about 15% of the IEA’s 415 TWh in 2024, about 62 TWh; the IEA has them growing about 30% a year, so about 300 TWh by 2030 (roughly a third of its 950 TWh for all data centres); a 60% inference share gives about 180 TWh. Mid lands at about 220 TWh in 2030, a little above.4 That calibration is mine, not a forecast. The full meter moves all five levers at once; Low, Mid and High are presets.

2Act 2: 100× and beyond

Compute is power times efficiency, so a hundred times today’s AI is not a hundred times today’s power. If chips gain 40% a year for a decade, they do 1.410 ≈ 29 times more per watt, and 100× today’s compute needs 31.2 GW × 100 ÷ 29 ≈ 108 GW, about 3.5 times today’s AI data-centre capacity.5 The grid’s ceiling of 316 GW in 2035 is my extrapolation: Epoch’s capacity more than doubled in 2025, and I assume growth slows to 26% a year. Whether the money keeps up with that is tested on The demand; whether the grid does, on What binds. Under it, 100× needs no orbit. But 100× in ten years is only about 1.6× a year, itself a sharp slowdown: at 2025’s growth, 2035 would be a thousand to ten thousand times today.

At 100× by 2035 the answer is a plain no: the grid on its present trend gets there without orbit. The claim bites at a thousand or ten thousand times, and there orbit is limited in tonnes: a hundred gigawatts is over a million tonnes, and Forethought sums the FAA’s site approvals for Starship to 145 launches a year.7 Try the launches slider under More assumptions. What a launched kilowatt-hour costs, and why that is a floor rather than a price, is worked through on Orbital.

3Tripwires

Two observables keep the long-run case falsifiable without waiting for 2050: inference of at least 300 TWh by 2030, and at least 1,000 TWh by 2035. Both are mine. If the path misses them, R1 to R3 are effectively dead under this model. For scale, the IEA’s 2035 range for all data centres is 700 to 1,700 TWh.4 The sources to watch are the IEA’s Energy and AI updates, Ember, the Energy Institute’s Statistical Review and, for the near term, the token counts and capital spending tracked on The demand.

Final words

Under Mid assumptions the answer on Act 1 is NO on every rung, and on Act 2 the grid alone covers 100× by 2035. The interesting region is the joint tail: many levers moved together, or a much larger multiple. Mark the tripwires and return when the next IEA number lands. The near-term question, whether the grid can connect what AI is buying this decade, is on What binds; the case map on the front page shows which pages feed which slider.
