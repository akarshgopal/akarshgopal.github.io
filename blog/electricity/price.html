The Price of Electricity: Bills, Plants and Learning Curves

Essay 7 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

In 2025 a megawatt-hour cost about $100 on Germany’s power exchange. Delivered to a household, the same megawatt-hour cost more than four times as much.

Essay 4 priced electricity on the exchange, hour by hour. This one follows the money to the meter, then asks what it costs to build the plants that sell into that market, why solar got about five hundred times cheaper as more was built while nuclear got dearer, and what a clean grid costs as a whole.

1From the exchange to the meter

Between the exchange and the meter sit the same layers everywhere, in different proportions. Germany publishes every one of them, so start there, with the wholesale price:

For most of the 2010s a megawatt-hour cost $35–50. Then Europe’s gas crisis dragged the marginal gas plant, and with it every megawatt-hour, to a monthly peak of $489 in August 2022. By 2025 the average was back to about $100: still above the 2010s, because gas stayed dearer and carbon rose.2

A hundred dollars a megawatt-hour is ten cents a kilowatt-hour. A German household signing a new contract in 2026 was offered, on average, 43 cents.3 Where do the other 33 come from?

Three layers, each with its own logic.

Procurement starts from the wholesale price, but a retailer must buy power in the shape its customers use, heavy in winter evenings, and buys most of it in advance to shield them from spikes. So this layer lags the spot price: in 2023 (click its bar) spot had fallen, but households were still paying for power bought in the crisis. With sales costs and margin it came to 17.6 ¢ in 2026, about 41% of the price.

Network charges, 10.7 ¢ or a quarter of the bill, pay for the wires, including the redispatch and reserves of essay 4. They rose for years with the grid build-out; the 2026 drop is a federal subsidy, not a cheaper grid. The end of this section looks at why they vary from place to place.

Taxes and levies are the remaining third, led by 19% VAT (6.8 ¢) and the electricity tax (2.4 ¢). The famous EEG levy that funded feed-in tariffs, once nearly 8 ¢, was moved to the federal budget in July 2022. The fair summary is not that “renewables are expensive” but that the system transition costs money, and that money has migrated into networks, surcharges and the budget.

What “43 cents” is, and isn’t. It is the average offer for new household contracts at 3,500 kWh a year, fixed charges included. Customers on old or default tariffs can pay noticeably more or less, and network charges vary by region. It is the best single number available, but it is a construct.

The United States shows the same divergence from lower levels. In PJM, the largest US wholesale market, the wholesale price in 2024 was 3.4 ¢/kWh, about a quarter above 2019’s; the average residential price rose by a third, to 17.3 ¢ in 2025.4 Most of the gap is delivery: wires, poles, storm hardening and the returns that pay for them. Cheap wholesale power no longer means cheap power at the meter.

Across Europe the same three layers appear in very different proportions, and the ranking flips once prices are measured against what money buys locally.5

Worldwide the spread is wider still: households pay around forty US cents a kilowatt-hour in Italy and Germany, about eight in China and India, two in Egypt. Cheap fuel explains part of it; policy explains much of the rest, from light taxes to subsidies to industry cross-subsidising homes. Tariffs below cost are paid for in utility debt and, in places, power cuts.6

All of this assumes a meter on a wall. About 730 million people, eight in ten of them in sub-Saharan Africa, still had no electricity in 2024.8 Many more are on grids that fail often, like South Africa’s during the rolling cuts of 2023.9 Where grid power is dear or unreliable, people build their own: Pakistan imported about 17 GW of solar panels in 2024, mostly for rooftops.10

The network in your bill

Back to the network layer. Its operators are regulated monopolies: the regulator approves their costs and a return on investment, recovered through network charges. How that lands on bills differs by country, and Germany is an extreme case.

Counting the surcharges that also pay for grids, network costs made up about a third of a German household’s price on 1 April 2025: more than the energy itself.11 Of each network dollar, roughly 30 cents pays the four transmission operators and 70 cents the regional and local networks below them.12 What a household pays depends on where it lives and how much it uses: every operator sets a fixed yearly basic charge plus a price per kilowatt-hour, so a small user pays more for each kilowatt-hour than a large one.13

The spread between operators is not only about efficiency. Thinly populated regions spread their wires over fewer customers; regions where many wind and solar farms were connected carry the cost of connecting them; old, written-down networks are cheaper than new ones.13 In Germany, since 2025, the cost of connecting renewables is spread across regions rather than landing on rural areas where the wind farms are.14 In the United States the shift is the same: major utilities’ spending on transmission nearly tripled in real terms between 2003 and 2023, to $27.7 billion.15 Whether that is money well spent depends on what it buys: a line that lets cheap remote wind or hydro replace gas saves far more than it costs; one that sits half-used does not. Getting that right, line by line, is what transmission planning is for.

2What a power plant costs

The merit order runs on marginal cost, what one more megawatt-hour costs from a plant that already exists. But someone has to build the plant first, and that money comes from the infra-marginal rent of the merit order. So let’s build one and sell its output into that market.

On the left is what the plant costs, spread over every megawatt-hour it will ever make: build cost repaid with interest, plus upkeep, plus fuel. Call that total the levelised cost of electricity, LCOE. On the right is what essay 4’s model day pays it over a year.

Start with solar. Almost all its cost is capital, and it runs when every other panel does, so it earns less than the average price. Now drag the dot on the right: that is the cost of capital. From 4% to 10% the same panels cost about $50 against $84 per megawatt-hour, while what they earn does not move. When central banks raised rates in 2022–23, they raised the cost of the energy transition directly.

Switch to New nuclear: it runs almost every hour, but its capital alone, about $155/MWh, exceeds the $111 the market pays. Gas combined cycle is cheap to build and dear to run; its thin rent covers little more than a third of its fixed costs. Now choose the 2022-style gas shock. Gas runs in an eighth of the hours yet covers about half its costs, because gas sets the price; wind and nuclear, whose costs did not change, see their rent roughly double.

Lazard’s 2026 US midpoints are about $69/MWh for utility solar, $68 for onshore wind, $90 for gas combined cycle and $215 for new nuclear, with solar, wind and gas all dearer than a year earlier. Utility solar had fallen roughly tenfold from 2009 to 2021: a partial healing, then a partial relapse.1617

Two cautions. IRENA’s global averages, $34/MWh for onshore wind and $43 for solar in 2024, are dominated by large, cheap projects in China; they are not the cost of a wind farm in Brandenburg or Texas.

More importantly, LCOE is not value, as the two bars show. A megawatt-hour at noon in May is worth less than one at 7 pm in January. The cost of turning weather into reliable supply (storage, backup, wires) sits outside LCOE, and it is the cost that is growing: Lazard’s 2026 estimate for four-hour batteries rose to $210–292 per MWh discharged.

3Renewables, nuclear and learning

Lazard starts in 2009 and so hides the most important fact about solar: since 1975 the average price of a module has fallen about five-hundredfold, to about 27 cents a watt in 2024 (in 2025 dollars), while the world’s installed solar grew to some 1,900 GW. On logarithmic axes the points fall close to a straight line.18

Each doubling of the world’s installed solar has cut the module price by about the same fraction, roughly 24%, and closer to a third since 2010. That straight line is a learning curve, or Wright’s law, after the engineer who saw the same pattern in aircraft in 1936. Electricity from new solar farms followed, from about $408/MWh in 2010 to $43, and onshore wind more slowly.

The curve is not a law of nature; it is what happens when an industry makes the same thing in enormous numbers. It also gets harder to ride: the next doubling means another 1,900 GW, and modules are now a minority of a solar farm’s cost, while land, labour, grid connections and capital learn slowly. Switch the fit window to “recent” and drag the learning rate: small changes compound into very different futures. Batteries ride a similar curve: BloombergNEF’s annual survey reported an average of $108/kWh per pack in 2025.19

Cheap energy, expensive reliability

A cheap megawatt-hour is not a cheap system. LCOE already counts how often a plant runs. It does not count that every extra panel produces at the same moments as the rest, so its price falls as the fleet grows; nor that somebody must be there in the dark, windless week. Lazard puts that firming at roughly $8 to $62 per MWh, depending on the grid operator.16 That gap is where storage, transmission, flexible demand and firm generation compete.

Nuclear: the curve that bent the wrong way

Nuclear is the obvious firm, clean source, and its history is the mirror image of solar’s: in the West its costs rose with experience. Arnulf Grubler found French construction costs climbing over successive reactor generations even in the most standardised Western build-out, “negative learning by doing”.20 Recent projects show the problem starkly:

Vogtle 3 and 4 in Georgia were supposed to cost $14 billion and came to about $35 billion, seven years late. Flamanville 3 went from about $4 billion to about $27 billion with financing; Hinkley Point C from about $24 billion to about $45 billion, with its first unit expected in 2030 (in their own currencies, €3.3 billion to €23.7 billion and £18 billion to £35 billion).21

These are first-of-a-kind projects: new designs, rusty supply chains and schedules long enough for interest to pile up. The optimistic reading is that series build is cheaper, as South Korea’s near-identical reactors suggest.23 The pessimistic one is that the West has never paid for a series to find out.

Small reactors, big claims

Small modular reactors are the industry’s answer: build the reactor in a factory and learn through numbers rather than size. The evidence so far is thin. NuScale’s first project saw its target price rise from $58 to $89 per MWh and was cancelled in 2023.22 Ontario’s first BWRX-300s at Darlington are budgeted at about C$17,000 per kilowatt, in Vogtle’s range, and no US SMR has yet produced power.24

Small reactors give up economies of scale and must win them back through factory learning. That can work; solar shows how powerful repetition is. But that SMRs will beat large reactors built in series is an argument, not an observation, until somebody builds the first ten.

Build a clean grid

A grid is a portfolio, not one technology. Here is a toy for a German-sized system, about 58 GW of average demand, run hour by hour through a synthetic northern-European year. Choose the fleet; the model dispatches it and adds up the cost.

Wind, solar and batteries alone make plenty of energy over the year yet leave some 2,500 hours short, because the energy arrives in the wrong weeks: watch the red ticks under mid-January. A rarely-run gas fleet closes the gap cheaply. Nuclear covers the dark week without fuss but expensively; drag its cost to the $6,000–8,000/kW that series building would need and the picture changes. At the starting costs, the wind-and-solar-plus-gas fleet reaches 83% clean for about $152/MWh and the mostly-nuclear fleet 98% for $177; the blend the figure opens on gets 90% for $159: the last few percent are the expensive part.

Geography changes the answer more than any slider. Under Chilean or Indian sun a panel yields roughly twice as much; hydro systems like Norway’s carry their own seasonal storage. The trade-off has the same shape everywhere, but its numbers are local.
