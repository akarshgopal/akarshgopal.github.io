What a Kilowatt-Hour Costs: Plants, Capital and the Bill

Essay 8 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

On the world average, a new solar farm made electricity for about 4.3 cents a kilowatt-hour in 2024. American households paid 17.3 cents for a kilowatt-hour in 2025, a third more than in 2019. German households paid about 43.1,2,3

Essays 6 and 7 watched the old engine restart: panels, turbines and batteries getting cheaper the more of them are built, and worth less the larger their share. This essay counts the costs that engine does not touch. One sits inside every plant’s price: the interest on the money that builds it, which for wind, solar and nuclear is most of the cost. One belongs to the system as a whole: keeping the lights on through a dark, windless week. The rest sit between the plant and the meter: the wires, and the taxes, levies and cross-subsidies each country adds to the bill.

1What a power plant costs

The merit order of essay 5 runs on marginal cost: what one more megawatt-hour costs from a plant that already exists. But the plant has to be built first, and that money comes back only through the gap between the market price and the plant’s own running cost, its infra-marginal rent. So build one and sell its output into that market.

The bar marked what it costs spreads the plant’s cost over every megawatt-hour it will ever make: the build cost repaid with interest, plus upkeep, plus fuel. That total is the levelised cost of electricity, LCOE. The bar beside it, what it earns, is what essay 5’s model day pays the plant over a year.

Start with solar. Almost all its cost is the build cost, paid before the first kilowatt-hour and repaid over thirty years, and it runs when every other panel does, so it earns less than the average price. What that money costs is the cost of capital: a blend of the interest lenders charge and the return owners expect. The dot on the curve sets it. At 4 per cent the panels cost about $50/MWh; at 10 per cent, $84; what they earn does not move. When central banks raised rates in 2022–23, they raised the cost of the energy transition directly.

New nuclear runs almost every hour, but its capital alone, about $155/MWh, exceeds the $111 the market pays. Gas combined cycle is cheap to build and dear to run; its thin rent covers little more than a third of its fixed costs. In a 2022-style gas shock, gas runs in an eighth of the hours yet covers about half its costs, because gas sets the price; wind and nuclear, whose costs did not change, see their rent roughly double.

Real projects bear this out. Lazard’s 2026 US midpoints are about $69/MWh for utility solar, $68 for onshore wind, $90 for gas combined cycle and $215 for new nuclear, with solar, wind and gas all dearer than a year earlier. Utility solar fell roughly tenfold from 2009 to 2021 and has since roughly doubled.4 IRENA’s global averages, $34/MWh for onshore wind and $43 for solar in 2024, are lower because they are dominated by large, cheap projects in China.1

LCOE is not value, as the two bars show. A megawatt-hour at noon in May is worth less than one at 7 pm in January, which is the capture-rate problem of essay 7. And the cost of turning weather into reliable supply sits outside every plant’s LCOE. Section 3 puts a price on it.

2Why nuclear got dearer

Essay 6 followed solar down its learning curve: each doubling of the world’s installed panels has cut the price of a module by about a quarter. Nuclear is the obvious firm, clean source, and in the West its curve bent the other way. Arnulf Grubler found French construction costs climbing over successive reactor generations, in the most standardised build-out the West has had: “negative learning by doing”.5

In the United States, reactors ordered around 1970 took ever longer to finish, as designs changed during construction and safety rules tightened, above all after Three Mile Island in 1979. Those were also years of interest rates in double figures.6,7 The two problems multiply. A plant pays interest on every dollar from the day it is spent until the plant earns. Spend evenly over seven years at 7 per cent, and interest adds about 28 per cent to the cost by opening day; over fifteen years, about 73 per cent.8 Flamanville 3 in France cost about $15 billion to build and $27 billion with its financing.9

The recent Western projects show the problem plainly:

Vogtle 3 and 4 in Georgia were supposed to cost $14 billion and came to about $35 billion, seven years late. Flamanville 3 went from about $4 billion to $15 billion to build; Hinkley Point C in Britain from about $24 billion to about $45 billion, with its first unit expected in 2030 (in their own currencies, €3.3 billion to €13.2 billion, and £18 billion to £35 billion).9

These are first-of-a-kind projects: new designs, supply chains that had not built a reactor in a generation, and schedules long enough for interest to pile up. The optimistic reading is that building the same design again and again is cheaper, as South Korea’s near-identical reactors suggest.6 The pessimistic one is France’s experience: its standardised series got dearer, not cheaper. China, which is now building more reactors than any other country, has standardised on a few designs and builds them in series, but its costs are not published on a basis comparable with these projects, so they are left out here.11

Small reactors, big claims

Small modular reactors are the industry’s answer: build the reactor in a factory and learn through numbers rather than size. The evidence so far is thin. NuScale’s first project saw its target price rise from $58 to $89/MWh and was cancelled in 2023.10 Ontario’s first BWRX-300s at Darlington are budgeted at a cost per kilowatt close to Vogtle’s,10 and no US small reactor has yet produced power.12

Small reactors give up economies of scale and must win them back through factory learning. That can work; solar shows how powerful repetition is. But that they will beat large reactors built in series is an argument, not an observation, until somebody builds the first ten.

3The cost of a clean grid

A cheap megawatt-hour is not a cheap system. Wind and solar make energy. A grid also needs firm capacity: capacity that can be counted on to deliver its rated output whenever it is needed, for as long as it is needed, whatever the weather. A gas plant with fuel is firm; so is a nuclear plant, and a hydro dam with water behind it. A battery is firm for as many hours as it holds. A solar farm at night is not, and nor is a wind farm in a still week.

Firm capacity earns its keep in the few hours when nothing else will do, and must be paid for in all the others. That cost appears in no single plant’s LCOE.

Cheap energy, expensive reliability

Lazard puts the cost of firming wind and solar in the United States at roughly $8–62/MWh, depending on the grid; its 2026 estimate for four-hour batteries is $210–292 for every megawatt-hour they discharge.4 Storage, transmission, flexible demand and firm generation all compete to fill that gap.

To see how the cost rises as a grid gets cleaner, take a model grid with about 58 GW of average demand, roughly Germany’s, and run it hour by hour through a synthetic year of northern-European weather. Choose the fleet; the model dispatches it and adds up the cost.

Without anything firm, a fleet of wind, solar and four-hour batteries makes plenty of energy over the year yet leaves some 2,500 hours short, marked in red under the year, because the energy arrives in the wrong weeks. A rarely run gas fleet closes the gap cheaply; nuclear covers the dark week without fuss, but at a high price. At the starting costs, wind and solar with gas backup reach 83 per cent clean for about $152/MWh and the mostly-nuclear fleet 98 per cent for $177; the blend the figure opens on gets 90 per cent for $159. The last few per cent are the expensive part. At the $6,000–8,000/kW that building reactors in series would need, the mostly-nuclear fleet falls to $105–123, cheaper than any other clean option.

Geography changes the answer more than any choice of fleet. Under Indian or Chilean sun a panel yields roughly twice as much; hydro systems like Norway’s carry their own seasonal storage; China is building wind, solar, hydro, nuclear and coal at once and joining them with the long DC lines of essay 4. The trade-off has the same shape everywhere, but its numbers are local. Wherever it is struck, the firm capacity is paid for whether or not it runs, and in the United States that payment is now on the bill.

4From the exchange to the meter

Everything so far is the cost of making a megawatt-hour. A household pays for more: getting it to the house, and whatever the state adds or takes away on the way. The layers are the same everywhere: energy, networks, taxes and levies, and in many countries cross-subsidies between groups of customers. Their proportions are not. Four countries show the range.

Germany: cheap wholesale, dear bills

Germany publishes every layer, so start there, with the wholesale price:

For most of the 2010s a megawatt-hour cost $35–50. Then Europe’s gas crisis dragged the marginal gas plant, and with it every megawatt-hour, to a monthly peak of $488 in August 2022. By 2025 the average was back to about $100: still above the 2010s, because gas stayed dearer and carbon rose.14

A hundred dollars a megawatt-hour is ten cents a kilowatt-hour. So far in 2026 the exchange has averaged about 12.5 cents, and a German household signing a new contract was offered, on average, 43 cents.3 Where do the other 30 come from?

Three layers, each with its own logic.

Procurement starts from the wholesale price, but a retailer must buy power in the shape its customers use, heavy on winter evenings, and buys most of it in advance to shield them from spikes. So this layer lags the spot price: in 2023 spot had fallen, but households were still paying for power bought in the crisis. With sales costs and margin it came to 17.6¢ in 2026, about 41 per cent of the price.

Network charges, 10.7¢ or a quarter of the bill, pay for the wires, including the redispatch of essay 4 and the reserves of essay 5. They rose for years with the grid build-out; the 2026 drop is a federal subsidy, not a cheaper grid.

Taxes and levies are the remaining third, led by 19 per cent VAT (6.9¢) and the electricity tax (2.4¢). The EEG levy that funded feed-in tariffs, once nearly 8¢, was moved to the federal budget in July 2022. The fair summary is not that “renewables are expensive” but that the transition of the whole system costs money, and that money has moved into networks, surcharges and the budget.

What “43 cents” is, and isn’t. It is the average offer for new household contracts at 3,500 kWh a year, fixed charges included. Customers on old or default tariffs can pay noticeably more or less, and network charges vary by region. It is the best single number available, but it is a construct.

The network in a German bill

Network operators are regulated monopolies: the regulator approves their costs and a return on their investment, recovered through network charges. On the regulator’s data, and counting the surcharges that also pay for grids, network costs made up about a third of a German household’s price on 1 April 2025.15 Of each network dollar, roughly 30 cents pays the four transmission operators and 70 cents the regional and local networks below them.16 What a household pays depends on where it lives and how much it uses: every operator sets a fixed yearly basic charge plus a price per kilowatt-hour, so a small user pays more for each kilowatt-hour than a large one.17

The spread between operators is not only about efficiency. Thinly populated regions spread their wires over fewer customers; regions where many wind and solar farms were connected carry the cost of connecting them; old, written-down networks are cheaper than new ones.17 Since 2025 the cost of connecting renewables has been shared across regions rather than landing on the rural areas where the wind farms are.18

Germany is an extreme case even within Europe. The same three layers appear in very different proportions across the continent, and the ranking changes once prices are measured against what money buys locally.19

The United States: wires and capacity

The United States shows the same divergence from lower levels. In PJM, the largest US wholesale market, which runs from Chicago to Washington, the wholesale price in 2024 was 3.4¢/kWh, about a quarter above 2019’s. The average residential price rose by a third over the same years, to 17.3¢ in 2025.2

Most of the gap is delivery: wires, poles, storm hardening and the returns that pay for them. What major utilities spend on transmission nearly tripled in real terms between 2003 and 2023, to $27.7 billion a year.20 Whether that is money well spent depends on what it buys: a line that lets cheap remote wind or hydro replace gas saves far more than it costs; one that sits half-used does not. Getting that right, line by line, is what transmission planning is for.

The second layer is firm capacity, priced in the open. PJM holds an auction each year for the capacity it will need three years ahead, and retailers pass the cost on to every customer. For the year from June 2025 the price jumped from $28.92 to $269.92 per megawatt-day, and the auction for the year after cleared at its cap, $329.17, as forecasts of data-centre demand rose and older plants retired.21 Cheap wholesale energy no longer means cheap power at the meter.

India and China: tariffs as policy

In Germany and the United States, households pay more per kilowatt-hour than industry: they use less, at lower voltage, through more of the network. American industry pays about half the residential price. India and China turn that around.

Indian households paid about 7.6¢ a kilowatt-hour in the third quarter of 2026, businesses about 12.2¢.22 State regulators set the tariffs, and they deliberately charge industry and commerce more than it costs to supply them, to keep homes and above all farms cheap; in several states, farm pumps get their power free or nearly so. National tariff policy asks each state to keep every class of customer within 20 per cent of the average cost of supply.23

The state distribution companies sit in the middle, buying power on long contracts and selling it at those tariffs. Part of what they buy is never paid for: lost in the wires, stolen, or billed and not collected. These losses fell from about 21 per cent of the power bought in 2019–20 to about 16 per cent in 2023–24.23 A tariff set below cost is paid for in utility debt, state bailouts and, in places, power cuts. That is why the record-low solar auctions of essay 6 do not turn by themselves into cheap, reliable power for every home and farm.

Chinese households paid about 7.6¢ and businesses about 10.9¢.22 Here too the state sets the terms. Until 2021 the National Development and Reform Commission set a catalogue price for each class of user. Since October 2021, industrial and commercial users buy at market prices that may move up to 20 per cent either side of a coal benchmark, while household and farm tariffs stay regulated.24 Chinese businesses still pay less than half of what German ones do.

Worldwide the spread is wider still: households pay just over forty US cents a kilowatt-hour in Italy and Germany, about eight in China and India, two in Egypt. Cheap fuel explains part of it; policy explains much of the rest, from light taxes to subsidies to industry paying for homes.22

Add it up. Of the 43 cents a German household pays, less than a third is the energy traded on the exchange. In the United States the wires and the capacity auctions have pushed bills up while wholesale power stayed cheap. In India and China the tariff is an instrument of policy, and what each customer pays says more about the state than about the plant. The engine that made panels and turbines cheaper does not reach these layers. Capital, firm capacity, networks and policy now decide most of the bill.

The last essay puts the five pieces together: why electricity stopped getting cheaper, and what would have to be true for it to fall again.
