Why It Stopped, and Can It Fall Again?

Essay 9 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

For eighty years a kilowatt-hour got cheaper almost every year. Then, in the early 1970s, it stopped. The essays so far have built the pieces of an explanation. This one puts them together and asks what it would take for the price to fall again.

1Why the cheapening stopped

Essay 1 started with a curve. The real price an American household paid for electricity fell about 5 per cent a year from 1892 to 1973.1 Then it stopped falling, and in nominal terms it roughly doubled by 1980.2 The essays since have built four layers of an answer.

Physics. The machine followed steam through the turbine. For seventy years each new plant ran hotter and burned less fuel per kilowatt-hour than the last, until the steels of the 1960s set a ceiling near 40 per cent efficiency: a limit of materials, well short of what Carnot allows.

Scale. The same essay watched units grow from tens of megawatts to a thousand. Demand growing about 7¾ per cent a year let every new unit be the biggest yet, and bigger was cheaper per kilowatt. Past about 1,000 MW the units grew less reliable and the savings reversed, Richard Hirsh’s “technological stasis”. Then demand growth itself slowed.

Fuel. In essay 5 the price was set by the last plant needed. When crude went from about $3 to $12 a barrel between the autumn of 1973 and early 1974, that plant got dearer, fuel-adjustment clauses passed the cost straight through, and coal and gas followed oil upwards.

Institutions. Essay 8 priced a plant as capital repaid over decades. Pollution rules added scrubbers and cooling towers; US nuclear build times stretched from about seven years to twelve just as interest rates soared, so interest during construction came to dominate the bill. Why US nuclear costs rose so much is still argued over.3

Which layer mattered? A toy model separates them. It opens with the engine of improvement stopped in 1970 and every shock switched on, roughly what happened.

Stop the engine but switch the shocks off, and prices stop falling but barely rise. Run the engine with the shocks on, and prices still jump, but by 1990 they are back to about their 1970 level. Only stasis plus shocks makes prices jump and stay up. The plateau did not cause the 1970s spike; it removed the productivity growth that had quietly offset every rising cost.3

Other countries broke differently: France built standard nuclear plants, the Nordics leaned on hydro, Britain switched to gas. The lesson travels: electricity gets cheaper only when an engine of productivity runs faster than the costs rising around it.

A second engine started in the 2010s. Essay 6 followed solar down its learning curve, about a quarter cheaper for each doubling, with China’s factories doing most of the doubling; essay 7 showed that wind and solar earn less as their share rises. And essays 4 and 8 showed what the new engine does not touch: the wires, the firm capacity for the dark week, the cost of capital, and the taxes and levies that now make up most of a household bill in many countries. Whether power gets cheaper again depends on those.

Where the savings went

The utilities’ own accounts show the whole arc. Every investor-owned American utility reports its costs to the federal regulator each year, and summed, the reports show what an average household kilowatt-hour pays for. In 1951, in today’s money, it cost 37.0 cents, and by 1969 19.3. Over those years every part of the bill fell by about the same proportion, between 43 and 53 per cent, and each kept much the same share of revenue: fuel and plants fell no faster than wires, overheads or taxes. The whole business got cheaper together, and it was still getting cheaper in the late 1960s. Then fuel and bought power more than doubled, from 4.4 cents in 1969 to 10.3 by 1981, and the price climbed back to 23.3 cents in 1983. As the coal and nuclear plants of the 1970s came into the rate base, running and paying for plants rose too, from 3.8 cents to 5.8 by 1988. Then fuel got cheaper and the plants were paid down: by 2000 the kilowatt-hour cost 15.4 cents. Since then making the power has fallen further, from 10.3 cents to 7.4, while the wires that carry it have tripled, from 1.9 cents to 5.7, above their 1969 level.4

Cheaper energy was spent on the network. That is the shape of the problem now: the engine works on the part of the bill that is shrinking. In Germany the part it cannot touch is larger still: in 2026 taxes and levies were about a third of the household price and the network a quarter. China and India publish no comparable split of a household tariff; in both, tariffs are set by regulators, and in India some customers pay well below cost (essay 8).

First, though: why does any of this matter beyond the bill?

2Why the price of electricity moves everything else

Energy looks like a small part of the economy: Americans spent 5.2 per cent of GDP on it in 2024.5 But a small, unavoidable input can have a large effect when its price jumps, because nobody can stop using it quickly.

In 1981 the share was 13.3 per cent, and the energy spikes of 1974, 1980–81 and 2008 each sit next to a recession. James Hamilton found that ten of eleven post-war US recessions were preceded by a rise in the price of oil.6 When energy costs jump, everyone spends less on everything else.

A kilowatt-hour in a motor, heat pump or electric car often replaces two to four of fuel, so electrification spreads: electricity was about 20 per cent of the world’s final energy in 2023.7 Increasingly, the price of electricity is the price of energy.

And demand is growing again, unevenly. China used about 10,600 TWh in 2025, nearly eight times as much as in 2000, and now more per person than Germany. India’s use has more than tripled since 2000, yet per person it is still about a ninth of an American’s. US demand, flat for a decade, rose 11 per cent between 2020 and 2025; Germany’s has fallen.8 In 2000 four Indians in ten had no electricity at all; by 2024 almost everyone was connected.9

So industrial power prices have become national strategy. American industry paid 8.62¢/kWh on average in 2025; small German firms about 20¢ in 2026.10,11 Germany now subsidises industrial power towards a target of about $56/MWh.12 China sets industrial tariffs by regulation and makes well over half the world’s aluminium.13 India charges businesses more than households, so that industry helps pay for homes and farms.14 Each is a bet that the price of a megawatt-hour decides where industry can exist.

3What a megawatt-hour unlocks

For some products electricity is the main ingredient. The figure prices only the electricity in each. What differs is how much of the product’s cost that is: a large share for aluminium and electric hydrogen, a sliver of what a GPU-hour sells for.

Aluminium is the classic case: at 14 MWh a tonne, every $10/MWh adds $140 to the metal, so smelters follow cheap power to Iceland, Quebec, the Gulf and above all China.13 Steel made with hydrogen instead of coal turns a mill into one of the largest electricity customers in any country.

Hydrogen is where the arithmetic gets hard. At $60/MWh the power alone in a kilogram costs $3.30, more than three times the US target of $1 all-in.15 The synthetic fuels deep dive takes that line apart.

Computing sits at the other end. An hour of an H100 uses about ten cents of power at that price, tiny next to what the hour is worth. But data centres used about 1.5 per cent of the world’s electricity in 2024, heading for twice that by 2030, and in the US their binding constraint is how fast firm supply can be connected to them.7

Cheap electricity decides which industries are possible, and where. Can it get cheaper again?

4The surplus, the dark week and heat

Nobody knows what electricity will cost in 2040. But the generation engine is running again, and the problems it leaves behind can be played with. Three matter most.

Moving the surplus

The midday solar surplus is the cheapest energy ever produced and, unused, close to worthless: Germany’s day-ahead price was below zero in 573 hours of 2025.16 The direct fix is to move it in time, with batteries and with demand that shifts: cars that charge at noon, heat pumps that pre-heat, electrolysers that run when power is cheap.

A little of both only shortens the hours of negative prices; removing them takes a lot of flexible demand on top of the batteries. Batteries raise the price at noon and lower it in the evening, and if they flatten prices enough the arbitrage that pays for them disappears: storage, like solar, cannibalises its own value. And short batteries solve a daily problem. The harder one comes once or twice a year.

The dark week

Every grid has its bad week, and each country’s is different. In Germany it is the Dunkelflaute, still, grey winter days with little wind or sun. The figure opens on one, played on a model grid with plenty of wind and solar and little firm capacity.

Either side of the lull the wind carries most of the load. In the lull the batteries empty in one evening and demand goes unserved for days. Storage measured in days, or firm capacity that idles most of the year, closes the gap.

Texas failed differently in February 2021. Winter Storm Uri drove heating demand to a winter record just as gas wells, pipelines and power plants froze, and the grid operator ordered rotating outages that left millions of homes without power for days.17 In the Texas preset more batteries barely help; plants built to run in the cold do.

India’s hard hours come every evening of the hot season, when solar output falls away as air conditioners and lights come on, on a grid where coal still made 71 per cent of the electricity in 2025.8 That is a daily problem, and four-hour batteries solve it.

None of these costs appears in the cost of a kilowatt-hour from a wind farm or solar park. This is resource adequacy, and it is why in September 2026 the EU approved up to about $40 billion of German support for some 11–12 GW of new, mostly gas-fired capacity, and why markets like PJM in the eastern US pay plants simply to be available.18

Electrifying heat

In cold countries one of the biggest new demands will be heat. A heat pump is a refrigerator run backwards, moving heat from the outside air into the house: three to four units of heat per unit of electricity, its coefficient of performance (COP).

The physics always favours the heat pump: even at −15 °C, with radiators at 45 °C, it delivers more than twice the heat of the electricity it uses. What decides the bill is the ratio of the two prices. At German prices a heat pump needs a COP of about 2.8 just to break even; in Britain, where gas is cheap relative to power, about 3.8. A bill that loads levies onto each kilowatt-hour penalises the most efficient heating there is.

What would have to be true

For electricity at the meter to get cheaper in real terms over the next twenty years, these would have to be true. They are conditions, not predictions:

  • The generation engine keeps running. Solar, wind and batteries resume their cost declines after the 2021–26 relapse, China’s factories keep driving the learning curve, and capital stays affordable.
  • The surplus finds a use. Storage and flexible demand move midday energy into the evening, and old subsidies stop rewarding output at negative prices.
  • Firm capacity is bought cheaply. The dark week is covered by the cheapest mix of firm plants, long storage, interconnection and demand response, bought through markets rather than rescues, and built to run in the cold.
  • Wires get built faster. About 2,300 GW of US projects sat in interconnection queues at the end of 2024, and the median wait to connect has stretched to over four years.20
  • Network costs grow slower than the energy they carry. New loads, from heat pumps to data centres, spread fixed costs over more kilowatt-hours if the grid is used more intensively.
  • Someone can pay. Tariffs cover what power costs to deliver, so that utilities can buy the cheap new energy on offer: in India, record-low solar auctions sit beside distribution companies that sell power below cost.
  • Bills tell the truth. Electricity is taxed less relative to fossil fuels, and tariffs reflect time and place.

Each can fail. The 1970s suggest the most dangerous outcome is not one shock but a stalled engine meeting a run of bad luck.

5Back to the curve

The curve fell for eighty years because the whole business got cheaper together. Hotter steam in bigger machines cut the cost of making power, and a market that doubled about every decade spread the wires and overheads over ever more kilowatt-hours. It flattened when the machines stopped improving, growth slowed and the shocks arrived together.

A second engine is running now, in solar factories and battery lines, most of them in China. It cuts the cost of energy at the plant. Whether the price at the meter follows depends on everything between the plant and the meter: wires built in years rather than decades, firm capacity bought cheaply for the dark week, capital that does not cost a fortune, and bills that charge for what the system costs. None of those is set by physics. All of them can be chosen.

There is a longer way to see the same question. In 1964 the Soviet astronomer Nikolai Kardashev sorted possible civilisations by the power they command: Type I uses energy on the scale of a planet, Type II of a star, Type III of a galaxy. Carl Sagan later turned the types into a continuous scale with Type I at 1016 watts. Averaged over 2024, humanity used about 20 terawatts, which puts it at 0.73.22

Each step of 0.1 on the scale is ten times more power. At the 5.5 per cent a year the world grew in 1965–73, Type I would be about 120 years away; at the 1.9 per cent a year since, over 300. The Henry Adams curve of essay 1 went flat in the rich countries, and the world kept climbing mostly because China and India did. Physics leaves room: Type I is about a seventeenth of the sunlight that reaches the Earth. How fast anyone climbs depends on whether energy gets cheaper again, which is the question these essays set out to answer.
