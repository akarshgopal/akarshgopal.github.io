Transmission: The Network That Decides What Gets Built

An interactive essay · reactance, heat, steel towers and politics · written for Akarsh Gopal, October 2026

A transmission line looks like the simplest thing in the power system: aluminium hung from steel towers. It is also the part that now decides how fast everything else can change. China has strung a second, ultra-high-voltage grid across the country in fifteen years; India has tripled the capacity between its regions since 2014. The United States, which built about 4,000 miles of high-voltage line in 2013, now manages a few hundred a year, and Germany spends around three billion euros a year working around lines it has not yet built.

The main essay covered the first reason lines run at high voltage: losses fall with the square of the current. Its four-node network showed that power flows wherever physics sends it and that a full line splits prices in two. This part goes further: what limits a line, why long AC lines carry less than short ones, why direct current wins over long distances and under the sea, why new lines take a decade or more in some countries and a few years in others, the tricks that get more out of existing wires, and what it all adds to your bill.

1Why high voltage: losses, corona and corridors

A current I through a resistance R turns I²R watts into heat, so for a given power, doubling the voltage quarters the loss. That is why the largest lines rose from tens of kilovolts a century ago to 380–400 kV in Europe, 500 and 765 kV in North America and India, and 1,000 kV AC in China. Each step needed bigger transformers at both ends, and brought new problems.

The first is corona. At very high voltage, the field at a thin conductor’s surface ionises the air: the line crackles, glows faintly at night, interferes with radio and wastes power, most in rain and fog. The cure is a fatter conductor, built as a bundle of two, four or six wires held apart by spacers. American Electric Power, which runs the largest 765 kV network in the US, publishes what this buys: at 1,000 MW its six-wire 765 kV lines lose 3.4 MW per 100 miles to resistance and 2.3 MW to corona, 0.6% in all; a typical 345 kV line loses 42.5 MW, 4.2%.1

The second argument is land. A 765 kV line needs a right-of-way about 61 m wide and a 345 kV line about 46 m, but over long distances one 765 kV circuit carries as much as six at 345 kV.1 Try it below.

The figure opens at 2 GW over 400 km, and the gap is stark: five 345 kV circuits losing about 4.5%, or one 765 kV circuit losing about 2% on a corridor almost four times narrower. The higher voltage costs more per mile ($2.6–4.0 million against $1.1–2.0 million in AEP’s 2008 figures) but much less per megawatt.1 Why does the number of circuits climb so steeply with distance? Not resistance: reactance.

2Resistance, reactance and the power angle

A line is not only a resistor. Its current makes a magnetic field that, in an AC system, builds and collapses a hundred times a second (120 at 60 Hz), inducing a voltage that opposes the change, as in a transformer’s core. The line is a long, thin inductor, and its opposition to AC is its reactance, X: about a quarter of an ohm per kilometre on a 380 kV line, ten times the resistance. Reactance wastes no energy, but it decides how much power a line can carry at all.

Watch the two traces. The pushback is largest not when the current is largest but when it is changing fastest, as it swings through zero, so voltage and current end up a quarter-cycle apart. That is why reactance moves no energy: what the field takes on the way up it returns on the way down. Every kilometre adds the same field, so reactance grows with length, and with DC it disappears.

Because of it, the voltage at the sending end of a loaded line runs slightly ahead of the receiving end, by an angle δ. Ignoring resistance, the power carried is

P = V1 V2 sin δ / X

Power flows from the leading end to the lagging one, and more power means a larger angle: the AC grid’s version of water flowing downhill, with the angle as the height difference and reactance as the pipe’s narrowness. And sin δ can never exceed 1. Drag on the curve.

At 90° the line carries the most it ever can. Push further and the power falls; the sending generators speed up, the angle grows, and they fall out of step with the grid. Real systems keep a wide margin, often no more than 30–45° across a path, to survive losing a line. The ceiling falls with length: double the length, double the reactance, half the ceiling. So short lines are limited by heat, long ones by the angle.

A subtler effect. The line is also a long capacitor, conductors and ground separated by air, and charging it every cycle generates reactive power; its inductance absorbs reactive power, more as the current rises. Lightly loaded, the line makes more than it uses and the far-end voltage rises; heavily loaded, it uses more and the voltage sags. At one load in between the two exactly cancel and the line looks like a pure resistance. That balance point is its surge impedance loading, or SIL. It depends on voltage, not length: about 400 MW at 345 kV, 900 MW at 500 kV and 2,200–2,400 MW at 765 kV.1 Tap changers and compensation only stretch it so far.

In 1953 H. P. St. Clair summarised decades of experience in a curve every transmission planner still knows: a line’s practical limit, as a multiple of its SIL, against its length. Short lines are limited by heat, medium ones by voltage drop, long ones by stability: three times the SIL or more at 50 miles, once the SIL at 300.

This is the physics behind China’s 1,000 kV AC lines and India’s 765 kV backbone. To move a lot of power far over AC you raise the voltage (SIL grows with its square), add compensation along the route, or give up on AC. But on most lines in Europe and much of the US the limit that binds first is simpler: heat.

3Heat, sag and the weather

An overhead conductor is a heater: current and sunshine warm it, wind and radiation cool it. The aluminium of a standard steel-reinforced conductor (ACSR) is usually kept below about 80 °C, partly to save its strength but mainly because metal expands. A 400 m span a few tens of centimetres longer hangs metres lower, and a line that sags too close to a tree can flash over. In 2003 an overloaded line sagging into trees in Ohio started the cascade that blacked out 50 million people in North America.5

So a line’s rating is the current that keeps it below its maximum temperature. Operators traditionally set a static rating from pessimistic weather: a hot day, full sun, barely any wind. Most hours are kinder, and the line could safely carry far more.

Wind matters far more than temperature: a breeze of a few metres per second across the conductor can add half again to its capacity. And wind is what wind farms need, so a line out of a windy region is often best cooled when it is most loaded. Dynamic line rating (DLR) uses sensors or weather data to set the rating in real time. A 2019 US Department of Energy report collected a decade of trials: 10–20% more capacity in Northern Ireland, 30–44% at the New York Power Authority, 30–70% on average at Oncor in Texas.8

A simpler step, the ambient-adjusted rating, updates for air temperature and day or night but not wind; US regulators made it mandatory in Order 881 of December 2021, though several operators have had extensions.5 German operators have used weather-based ratings on parts of their network for years.9 The carbon-core conductor is the other way past the heat limit; section 9 returns to it.

4AC power flow: loop flows and phase angles

A pipeline operator can choose which pipe the gas goes down. A grid operator mostly cannot. In a meshed AC network, power flows along every path from source to sink, split by the paths’ reactances like current through parallel resistors. The main essay’s network showed what that does to prices; here is what it does to geography.

Central Europe is the textbook case. Germany’s wind is in the north and the North Sea; its heaviest demand is in the industrial south and west. Power sent from Hamburg to Munich does not stay in Germany: part loops west through the Netherlands, Belgium and France, part east through Poland and the Czech Republic. These loop flows use up neighbours’ lines without their say.

With 14 GW of northern surplus, over a third takes the long way round, and the direct German lines overload before the neighbours’ do. The only physical levers are in the figure. A phase-shifting transformer adds an angle to one line, making that path look “uphill”; Poland installed four at Mikułowa on its German border in 2016.11 But it only moves flow between paths. An HVDC link carries exactly the power it is told to, regardless of the AC network around it, which is why Germany’s new north–south corridors are DC.

The same physics, with prices: the four-node market in the main essay lets you switch between nodal and zonal pricing and watch congestion split prices.

5Congestion: when cheap power gets stuck

Europe’s markets are zonal: Germany and Luxembourg are one bidding zone with one price, as if the network inside were a copper plate. The market clears ignoring internal lines; then the transmission operators check the flows, and when they do not fit, which in Germany is most days, they redispatch, paying generators north of the bottleneck (often wind farms) to produce less and those south of it to produce more.

The bill rose from about €1.5 billion in 2017 to €4.2 billion in 2022, when southern gas plants burned crisis-priced fuel, and has stayed around €3 billion since: €2.95 billion in 2024, €3.08 billion in 2025. In 2024 the measures moved some 30 TWh, about 6% of German consumption.1213 The loop-flow figure above is this process in miniature: every gigawatt it says “must be redispatched” is one of those payments. Developers see the other side as curtailment, charted in Part 2.

The same pattern, cheap power far from the cities, appears almost everywhere. In 2016 China curtailed 17% of its wind output, 43% in Gansu and 38% in Xinjiang, before new ultra-high-voltage lines and local reforms brought the national rate down.14 In 2013–14 about 16% of the power traded on India’s exchanges was constrained by congestion between regions.15 Texas solved its version by building first: the CREZ programme strung about 3,600 miles of 345 kV line for $6.9 billion, finished in January 2014, to carry 18.5 GW from the western wind zones to its cities.16

Most large US markets use nodal pricing instead of zones: every substation has its own price, including congestion, so trapped cheap power is priced cheaply where it is, an honest signal for where to put a factory, a battery or an electrolyser. It is also politically explosive: splitting Germany into a cheap north and dear south has been studied in the EU’s bidding-zone reviews and opposed by successive German governments, so every consumer shares the bill through network charges. The structural fix is more transmission, above all long DC links.

6HVDC: converters, break-even distance and case studies

Direct current has no reactance and no angle. A DC line carries whatever power its converters command, up to its conductors’ thermal limit, at any length, with no charging current and two conductors instead of three. Its problem was always the ends: converting AC to DC and back took room-sized mercury-arc valves for most of the twentieth century.

Two converter technologies dominate today. Line-commutated converters (LCC) use thyristors, switches that turn on at will but off only when their current falls to zero. They are robust, cheap per megawatt and efficient, losing about 0.6–0.8% per station, and carry the world’s largest links,17 but need a strong AC grid at both ends and absorb a lot of reactive power.

Voltage-source converters (VSC) use transistors (IGBTs) that switch on and off at will, building a near-perfect sine wave from hundreds of small steps. They lose a little more, about 1% per station,17 but can control reactive power, restart a dead grid, connect offshore wind farms with no grid of their own, and use plastic-insulated cables. They are large-scale relatives of Part 2’s grid-forming inverters.

The economics follow from the cost shape. DC has an expensive fixed part, the two converter stations, and a cheap variable part: fewer conductors, lighter towers, lower losses, no compensation. AC is the reverse. Somewhere the lines cross.

Overhead, DC wins over hundreds of kilometres, which is why China’s ultra-high-voltage DC lines carry south-western hydro and north-western coal, wind and solar to the coast, and why Brazil carries Amazon hydropower south on DC. Under the sea it wins after a few dozen kilometres, so almost every subsea interconnector and far-offshore wind farm uses it. And because DC links are fully controllable, short back-to-back ones join grids that are not synchronised, such as the three US interconnections or the 50 and 60 Hz halves of Japan.

The extremes are Chinese. The Changji–Guquan line, energised in 2019, runs 3,293 km from Xinjiang to Anhui at ±1,100 kV and carries 12 GW, about a dozen large power stations’ worth, for CNY 40.7 billion.20 Brazil’s Belo Monte dam on the Xingu feeds two ±800 kV, 4 GW bipoles: 2,092 km to Estreito from 2017, and 2,518 km to Rio de Janeiro from 2019, both built by State Grid’s Brazilian arm after competitive concession auctions.24

Europe’s links are mostly subsea: NordLink trades Norwegian hydro against German wind over 623 km from 2021, and Viking Link joined Britain and Denmark in December 2023.21 In the US, where most big DC lines date from the 1970s and 80s, SunZia’s developer announced in 2026 that the line had come online, carrying up to 3 GW of New Mexico wind to Arizona more than eighteen years after it was proposed.22

7Underground versus overhead

Nobody wants a pylon in their view, and in Germany that objection changed the grid. SuedLink, two 2 GW DC circuits over about 700 km from Schleswig-Holstein to the south, was written into law in 2013 as an overhead project. After years of protest the Bundestag decided in December 2015 that new DC lines should go underground, and the route was replanned from scratch. The last section was approved in October 2025; operation is due in 2028, at a total cost of about €10 billion.19

Cables cost more because everything is harder. The insulation is centimetres of extruded plastic that must shed heat into the soil, so cables are thick, laid in trenches with special backfill and jointed every kilometre or so in clean-room conditions. A 2016 German government study put underground routes at €4.5–12 million per kilometre, three to ten times overhead.19 Repairs take weeks, not days. And an AC cable is a long capacitor: beyond about 100 km its charging current alone fills its rating (the break-even figure’s cable view). So long cables are DC, and Germany’s underground DC choice was coherent, if expensive and slow.

The trade-off cuts both ways. Cables are invisible, nearly immune to storms and wildfire, and win consent faster; overhead lines are cheaper and quicker to repair and upgrade. Most countries mix them: overhead in open country, cable near towns and in sensitive landscapes.

8Planning, permitting and who pays

Planning starts from one rule: the grid must survive losing any single element, one line, transformer or generator, without overloads or dropped customers. This “N−1” criterion is why lines normally run well below their thermal rating.

The physics is a century old; what varies is governance. In the United States a line crossing three states needs three state approvals, federal permits and a cost-sharing deal, and every party can stall it.

Germany shows the other half of the problem. It cut its approval times hard: the kilometres of line approved each year rose sevenfold between 2021 and 2025. But a line still takes years to build once approved, and the kilometres put into operation each year have barely moved.25 Seen that way, finishing the grid is a question of rate. The work left is a fixed number of kilometres, so the faster you build, the sooner you finish.

The early-2010s US boom came from plans made years before, such as Texas’s CREZ lines. Since then the pipeline has thinned: the Department of Energy’s 2024 study implies about 5,000 miles of high-capacity line a year, and the US averaged about 540 in 2020–2024.26 Germany’s pipeline is now full: more than a third of its legislated kilometres are under construction, and all four big north–south DC lines had their permits by 2025. Ultranet is due in 2026, A-Nord and SuedOstLink in 2027 and SuedLink in 2028.25

The federal regulator’s Order 1920 of May 2024 makes every region plan at least 20 years ahead across several scenarios and weigh the full range of benefits, including getting more from existing lines.28 Compliance filings run into 2026, so steel in the ground is still years away.

Hardest are interregional lines across planning seams, the most valuable kind. They have no natural owner, so most are merchant bets: SunZia succeeded; Grain Belt Express lost its federal loan guarantee in 2025.22

Other countries settle need centrally. In Germany the Bundestag writes the network plan into law; of about 16,900 km legislated since 2009, under a fifth was operating by mid-2026, though permitting is easing.9 China builds its ultra-high-voltage lines as national projects; Brazil auctions 30-year concessions to build and run them.24 Transformers are a bottleneck for all of them.

How the slow pace of transmission shows up as years-long interconnection queues for new wind, solar and battery projects is the subject of Part 2’s project anatomy.

9Getting more out of existing wires

If new lines take a decade, squeeze more from the old ones. The toolbox is called grid-enhancing technologies: dynamic line ratings (section 3); advanced power-flow control, compact power electronics that act like adjustable phase shifters; and topology optimisation, software that finds which breakers to switch to reroute flows. A 2021 Brattle Group study for the Southwest Power Pool found the three together could more than double the wind and solar Kansas and Oklahoma could connect without new lines, from 2,580 MW to 5,250 MW.29

The bigger lever is reconductoring: keep the towers, replace the wire. Modern conductors have a carbon-fibre or composite core and trapezoidal aluminium strands that pack about 30% more metal into the same diameter, and run at 180 °C or more without extra sag. A 2024 UC Berkeley and GridLab study found reconductoring roughly doubles a line’s capacity within its right-of-way, usually in 18–36 months, at less than half the cost of a new line, and could supply most of the new inter-zonal capacity the US needs by 2035.30

None of these replaces new lines. Dynamic ratings help most where wind and loading rise together; power-flow control only moves capacity around; reconductoring stops at the limits of towers, substations and the angle and voltage limits of long lines. But they are fast, cheap and available now, and Order 1920 requires planners to consider them.28

10Interconnection as a resource

A line between regions also shares reserves, peaks and above all weather. When the wind drops over Germany it may still blow over Britain or the Baltic; when solar floods the midday market, Norwegian and Alpine reservoirs can hold back water. ACER, the EU regulators’ agency, put the benefit of cross-border trade at about €34 billion in 2021 alone.32 The EU’s target is for each country to be able to import at least 15% of its generating capacity by 2030.33

Even a modest link shaves the extremes, and each extra gigawatt saves a little less. The same logic drives proposals for long DC links across the North Sea and under the Mediterranean. It also shows how an interconnector competes with storage: a large hydro neighbour is, for Germany, a battery measured in terawatt-hours, and a 1.4 GW cable is its narrow door. The main essay and Part 2 show storage and flexible demand doing the same job inside a country.

Grids built across countries and continents

Europe is only one way to share. China has built a separate tier of ultra-high-voltage lines on top of its 500 kV grid: by October 2025, 45 projects were operating, 21 at 1,000 kV AC, 23 at ±800 kV DC and one at ±1,100 kV DC, most carrying power from the west and north to the eastern coast.35 India joined its five regional grids into one synchronous system on 31 December 2013, when the 765 kV Raichur–Solapur line tied in the south; inter-regional capacity grew from 36 GW in 2014 to about 120 GW in 2025.36

Where countries are poorer and smaller, they pool. The Southern African Power Pool, founded in 1995, links twelve countries’ utilities from the Cape to the Congo, letting hydro-rich Zambia, Mozambique and the DR Congo trade with coal-heavy South Africa.37 But Angola, Malawi and Tanzania are still not connected, and only about 7% of the energy traded in 2024–25 went through its competitive markets; the rest moved under bilateral deals limited by thin cross-border lines.38

11The network in your bill

Somebody pays for all this. Network operators are regulated monopolies: the regulator approves their costs and a return on investment, recovered through network charges. How that lands on bills differs by country; Germany, whose bill the main essay takes apart, is an extreme case.

On 1 April 2025, about a quarter of a German household’s price was the network tariff, and about a third with the surcharges that also pay for grids: more than the energy itself.39 Of each network euro, roughly 30 cents pays the four transmission operators and 70 cents the regional and local networks below them.40 What a household pays depends on where it lives and how much it uses: every operator sets a fixed yearly basic charge plus a price per kilowatt-hour, so a small user pays more for each kilowatt-hour than a large one.41

The spread between operators is not only about efficiency. Thinly populated regions spread their wires over fewer customers; regions where many wind and solar farms were connected carry the cost of connecting them; old, written-down networks are cheaper than new ones.41 German network charges also carry congestion management, and since 2025 the cost of connecting renewables is spread across regions rather than landing on rural areas where the wind farms are.42 In the United States the shift is the same: major utilities’ spending on transmission nearly tripled in real terms between 2003 and 2023, to $27.7 billion.43 As grids expand, the share will grow. Whether that is money well spent depends on what it buys: a line that lets cheap remote wind or hydro replace gas saves far more than it costs; one that sits half-used does not. Getting that right, line by line, is what transmission planning is for.

The final part follows electricity to its strangest customers, the electrolysers and fuel plants whose economics depend on how cheap a megawatt-hour can get, and so on the grid that delivers it.
