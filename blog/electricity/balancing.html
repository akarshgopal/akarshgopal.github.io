Balancing the Grid: Fifty Hertz and the Markets That Keep It

Essay 4 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

The grid stores almost no energy. Every second, the power going in must match the power coming out, across a whole continent, and the only buffer is the spin of the generators themselves.

This essay follows how that balance is kept: by physics in the first seconds and reserves over minutes, by inverters that must now do what spinning machines did for free, and by markets that decide which plants run each hour, each day and in each place.

1Fifty hertz

Continental Europe, from Portugal to Turkey, is a single synchronous machine, and so is the eastern half of North America. Thousands of generators like the one in the first essay turn in lockstep, their magnetic fields locked to each other through the wires.

At every instant, generation must equal consumption plus losses. When they differ, the difference comes, in the first seconds, from the only store there is: the kinetic energy of all those spinning rotors. If consumption wins, the rotors slow and the frequency falls. Frequency is therefore a live, continent-wide measurement of balance, reported by every socket: 50 Hz across Europe, most of Asia, Africa and Australia, 60 Hz across most of the Americas.

The figure opens as one large power station, a gigawatt, drops off the grid without warning. The rotors slip, the frequency dips, and the reserves catch it well clear of trouble. Several things happen, on different clocks:

In the first instant, nothing but physics responds. The missing power is drawn from the rotating mass of every machine on the grid, and the frequency starts to fall at a rate set by how much rotating mass there is — the inertia. Then some load relief appears on its own: many motors draw slightly less power when they turn slightly slower.

Within seconds the primary reserve wakes up. Selected plants and batteries measure frequency locally and raise output in proportion to the deviation, with no instruction from anyone. The fall stops at the nadir, about 49.92 Hz in this model (49.76 Hz for the 3 GW design incident); Europe’s rules allow at most 800 mHz of instantaneous deviation. By design, primary reserve only holds the frequency at a new, slightly low level.

Over minutes, the secondary reserve takes over. A central controller at the grid operator sees the persistent error and dispatches more generation until the frequency is back at exactly 50.000 Hz, freeing the primary reserve for the next incident. After fifteen minutes or so, slower tertiary reserves and the market replace the lost plant for good.

Continental Europe is designed to survive a bigger blow: the sudden loss of three gigawatts, roughly two large nuclear units. Losing fifteen is far beyond the design case. Primary reserve is overwhelmed, the frequency falls through 49.0 Hz, and automatic relays disconnect slices of customers so the rest can survive. That is how Europe survived its split in November 2006, when a line switching in northern Germany cascaded into three islands.

Large spinning machines provide inertia for free. With less of it, the same trip makes the frequency fall faster and deeper, giving the reserves less time.

2Grid-forming inverters

Wind, solar and batteries connect through power electronics, and most of those inverters today are grid-following: they measure the grid’s voltage and frequency and inject current to match, like a dancer following a partner. They add no inertia, so as they replace coal and gas, a trip like the one in section 1 makes the frequency fall faster and deeper. A grid-forming inverter sets its own voltage and frequency and pushes back at once when the grid moves, much like a synchronous machine, provided it has stored energy to draw on, typically a battery.

As the inverter share rises with no grid-forming plant, the fall steepens and deepens; past a point the frequency hits the load-shedding threshold before reserves respond. Add grid-forming capacity, or batteries that inject power within a second, and stability returns, mostly without fuel. That is why Britain and Australia, with high wind and solar shares on smallish grids, buy inertia (some of it from synchronous condensers, spinning machines that generate nothing) and grid-forming capability, and why grid codes increasingly demand it.

Spinning machines also held voltage steady for free, by absorbing and supplying reactive power, the back-and-forth energy that keeps voltage where it should be. On 28 April 2025, mainland Spain and Portugal blacked out within seconds. ENTSO-E’s expert panel found no single cause: oscillations earlier that day, too little dynamic voltage control and renewable plants set not to respond to voltage, among other weaknesses, let voltage climb until plants tripped in a cascade, Europe’s first overvoltage blackout. Its main recommendation is to require new generators, wind and solar included, to control voltage actively.3 The inverters can do it; until recently the rules did not ask them to.

3The merit order

Frequency tells the grid, second by second, whether supply matches demand. Most of that matching is arranged a day ahead. How does the market decide which plants run, and at what price? In most liberalised markets, from Europe’s day-ahead auction to Texas and Australia, every generator submits, for every hour (or quarter-hour) of tomorrow, how much it is willing to produce at what price. Every buyer submits how much it wants to buy. An algorithm then stacks all the offers from cheapest to most expensive — the merit order — and finds where supply meets demand.4

What does a plant offer? In a competitive market, roughly its short-run marginal cost: what one more megawatt-hour costs it to make right now. For wind and solar that is close to zero, since the fuel is free (below zero for plants paid a subsidy whatever the price); for nuclear it is low; for a fossil plant it is mostly fuel, plus carbon permits where carbon is priced. A country’s offers for one hour, in the order they arrived:

Sorted cheapest first, the offers form a staircase: the supply curve. Demand is a vertical line, how much the country needs this hour. Every plant to the left of it runs; every plant to the right waits. And everyone who runs is paid the same price, the offer of the last plant needed. This is uniform pricing, or pay-as-clear, and it is the most misunderstood feature of electricity markets. When demand crosses the edge of a block, the price jumps, not just for the extra megawatt-hours but for all of them.

Why not pay each plant what it bid? Because then nobody would bid their real cost. A wind farm that expects gas to set the price would bid just under it, and so would everyone else: roughly the same price, but with everyone guessing and dearer plants sometimes running ahead of cheaper ones. Pay-as-clear makes bidding your true cost the sensible strategy.

Now let the wind blow. A wind farm’s offer sits at the bottom of the stack, so more wind does not add a block at the top: it widens a block at the bottom and pushes every other plant to the right:

The demand line now crosses the staircase lower down, at a cheaper plant. This is the merit-order effect: wind and solar lower the price in the hours they produce, including for the coal and gas plants that still run, and, as the next section shows, for themselves.

Last, the fuel. A fossil plant’s height is its fuel price divided by its efficiency, plus the carbon price times its emissions. Lignite burns cheap fuel but emits about twice as much CO₂ per unit of heat as gas, so its block climbs steeply with the carbon price. The full market, with both prices:

At low carbon prices lignite and coal undercut gas; as carbon rises, gas slides ahead of coal, while lignite climbs fastest. This fuel switch is how a carbon price changes what burns, hour by hour, and why Europe’s wholesale price is so often the price of gas plus carbon. With gas at around $170 per megawatt-hour of heat, where it stood for much of 2022, the marginal plant in most hours is gas-fired, and its cost sets the price for everyone, including wind farms whose costs didn’t change at all.

Every plant below the marginal one is paid the clearing price but spends only its own running cost. The gap is what it keeps. That keep is called infra-marginal rent, and it is normally how plants with low running costs pay back the cost of building them. In 2022 the rents were extraordinary, and governments taxed them.

4A day on the grid

Zoom out from one hour to a whole day and the balancing problem changes. Demand has a shape: low at night, a morning ramp, a working-day plateau, an early-evening peak. Solar is a hump at noon and wind wanders over hours and days. Whatever they don’t cover, something else must: the residual load.

The figure opens at 13:00 on a spring Sunday, with about the solar Germany now has installed, some 110 GW. The residual load has all but vanished, the price sits below zero, and some wind and solar is switched off because nobody can use it. As the sun sets, the residual load ramps steeply back up and the evening price climbs. With less solar installed, the noon price recovers.

California named this shape. Its grid operator, CAISO, drew the duck curve in 2013: a midday belly of net demand that deepens every year, then a steep evening neck. In 2024 it curtailed 3.4 TWh of wind and solar, almost all of it solar and most of it in spring, when sunshine is strong and air conditioners are off.6

Negative prices are not a glitch. Germany’s day-ahead market had 573 negative hours in 2025, up from 457 the year before.7 Some producers are paid per kilowatt-hour whatever the market price (many older solar panels are on fixed feed-in tariffs), and some plants are expensive to stop and restart. Both will pay to keep producing.

This has an awkward consequence for solar itself. Every panel produces in the same hours as every other panel, so the more there are, the lower the price in exactly those hours. What solar earns per megawatt-hour, divided by the average price of all hours, is its capture rate, and it falls as the fleet grows.

That fall is called cannibalisation. It doesn’t mean solar is bad; it means the value of the next panel depends on what is already on the grid, and that the cheapest kilowatt-hours are cheap partly because they arrive when nobody needs them. Remember this when we get to the cost of building power plants in essay 7: a cost per kilowatt-hour is only half of the story; the other half is when those kilowatt-hours arrive.

5Markets in time

The merit order clears once a day, at noon, for all of tomorrow. But tomorrow is uncertain. A wind portfolio that sold 9 GW for 18:00 may learn by morning that the wind will be weaker than forecast.

So electricity is traded in a sequence of markets, each closer to real time.8 The day-ahead auction sets most of tomorrow’s schedule and the price quoted in the news. The intraday market then lets traders adjust as forecasts improve, until shortly before delivery. American markets organise the same physics differently. Most US regions have a day-ahead market and then a real-time market that clears every five minutes, and the real-time price serves as the final settlement for deviations.9 Same problem, different labels.

Two extremes bracket everything else. On a sunny, windy holiday the market is oversupplied and prices go negative, in Europe down to the auction floor of about −$570/MWh at worst. In a cold, dark, windless winter week almost every plant is needed and the price is set by the dearest peakers, or by buyers’ willingness to stop consuming. Both kinds of hours are becoming more frequent, and a market of mostly zero-cost wind and solar is increasingly decided by them. We return to the dark week in the last essay.

6Markets in space

The merit order treats the grid as a copper plate; essay 3 showed it isn’t. Cheap power gets stuck behind a full line, and operators pay to work around it. This section is about what that does to prices: once a line fills, one price no longer fits the whole network.

The figure opens in nodal mode, how most large US markets work, with the northern wind just high enough to fill the West–South line, so the four prices have already split. One notch less wind and they merge; more, and they fork further: power is trapped in the north, where wind is curtailed and the price can fall to zero, and scarce in the south, where a local peaker must run. The small chart traces that fork across every wind level. These locational marginal prices show where new generation, storage or demand is worth most; what loads pay beyond what generators receive is the operator’s congestion rent.

Switch to zonal, which is how Europe, Australia’s NEM and Japan work: Germany and Luxembourg, for example, are one bidding zone with one price. The market clears as if the grid were a copper plate, the resulting flows break the line limits, and the operators fix them afterwards by redispatch. Its cost never shows up in the wholesale price; it is socialised into network charges, one of the ways the cost of the energy transition migrated into the regulated part of the bill.10

Neither system is free. Nodal pricing is efficient but makes customers in some places pay more; zonal pricing keeps one price but hides the cost of the grid in a socialised charge and blunts the signal for where to build. Splitting Germany into a northern and a southern zone is one of the live debates of European market design; for now Berlin has chosen one zone and more lines.

Nodal and zonal markets are only two arrangements. Most of the world’s electricity is not sold on a spot exchange at all. China’s grid is run by two state-owned companies, with plants paid regulated or negotiated prices, though provincial spot markets are spreading. India’s state distribution companies buy mainly under long-term contracts. Much of Africa and the Gulf has a single state utility or buyer. Texas and Australia run “energy-only” markets with very high price caps; PJM and Britain add capacity auctions.11

Whatever the design, each has to fit in more solar every year. The next essay follows it from a photon striking a cell to the grid, and comes back to the noon problem: Germany’s real solar capture rate for every year since 2015.
