What a Kilowatt-Hour Is: Charge, Voltage and Heat

Essay 2 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

Every electricity bill is a count of kilowatt-hours. To ask why they stopped getting cheaper, it helps to know what one is: what moves in the wire, what pushes it, and where the energy goes on the way.

This essay starts with a single electron in a copper wire and ends with the meter. Three ideas from here run through the rest of the series. Voltage is a height, an amount of energy per unit of charge. Resistance turns some of that energy into heat wherever current flows. And power, the rate, is not the same thing as energy, the amount you pay for.

1Charge and current

Every atom in a copper wire has electrons, and in a metal some of those electrons are not attached to any particular atom. They wander through the crystal lattice like a gas, bumping into the vibrating ions, moving in every direction at once at more than a thousand kilometres a second.1 Because they move in every direction equally, nothing flows. There is no current.

Each electron carries a tiny, fixed amount of negative charge. Charge is counted in coulombs; one coulomb is the charge of about six billion billion electrons. A current is charge flowing past a point, and one ampere is one coulomb a second.

Now connect the wire to a battery. The battery pushes a little extra negative charge onto one end of the wire and pulls some away from the other, and this sets up an electric field along the whole length of the wire. The field gives each electron a very gentle shove in one direction between collisions. Their random motion is still there, but now it has a tiny drift superimposed on it:

The drift is very slow: in a kettle cable the electrons creep along at a fraction of a millimetre per second, slower than a snail; with alternating current they merely shuffle back and forth by a few micrometres. Yet the lamp lights at once, because what travels fast is not the electrons but the field. Close the switch and the push runs along the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light, like water in a pipe that is already full.

So electricity is not a substance that gets delivered. The electrons in your wall were there before you plugged anything in and they will be there afterwards. What is delivered is energy. The rest of this essay follows it: how the battery gives it to the charges, and how the charges give it up.

2Voltage is height

A ball at the top of a hill has energy it can give up by rolling down. Charge in a circuit is similar. A battery, or a generator, is a pump: inside it, chemistry or magnetism pushes each charge “uphill”, against the field, and hands it energy. Outside, the charge runs back down through the circuit and gives that energy away.

The height of the hill is the voltage. It is measured in energy per unit of charge: one volt means each coulomb carries one joule. A 12-volt battery lifts every coulomb that passes through it by 12 joules. The push a battery gives is often called its electromotive force, or EMF, though it is not a force at all; it is this height.

The figure lays a simple circuit, a battery, two wires and a lamp, flat on the floor, and raises each point of it by the energy a charge carries there. The colour of each charge shows the same thing.2

Two things in the picture matter later. First, the field is the steepness of the hill: the volts lost per metre. Where the track is steep, as through the lamp, the field is strong and the charges give up energy quickly. Where it is nearly flat, as along the copper wires, they give up very little. Second, a taller hill pushes harder, and more charge flows each second: double the voltage and, through the same lamp, the current doubles too.

Why does the lamp take nearly all of the height, and the wires almost none? That is a question about what slows charge down.

3Resistance is collisions

Go back into the copper. Between collisions the field speeds each electron up a little; then it hits an ion of the lattice, gives up what it gained, and starts again. Every collision hands energy to the lattice, which vibrates a little more. A lattice vibrating more is, by definition, hotter. This is what a wire’s resistance is: how hard the charge must be pushed to keep it moving through the collisions. And it is why every current makes heat.

Three things make a conductor resist more. A longer one has more lattice to cross, so it needs more push for the same current. A thinner one has fewer lanes for the same flow, so each electron must drift faster and is stopped more often. A hotter one has ions vibrating harder, so collisions come sooner. The material matters as well: tungsten, the metal of old lamp filaments, resists about three times as much as copper of the same size.1

A lamp circuit puts these side by side. The figure shows one: a metre of household copper wire, a filament of fine tungsten and another metre of wire, on a 12-volt battery. Above it is the same height profile as before.

The same current flows through every part, because charge cannot pile up anywhere in the loop: every electron that enters the filament leaves it. In the thin filament the electrons crowd into a narrow channel and drift much faster, and the collisions there are many and hard. That is where the height goes. The filament takes almost all of the 12 volts and glows; the copper takes a few hundredths of a volt and stays cool.

This is Ohm’s law, seen as a landscape. The height lost across any piece of the circuit equals the current through it times its resistance, V = I × R. With one current for the whole loop, the piece with the most resistance loses the most height. Resistance is measured in ohms: one ohm loses one volt for every ampere. A shorter, thicker filament has less resistance, so more current flows; make it thick enough and the copper starts to take a share of the height, and warms.

The filament also shows why temperature matters. Cold, it has less than a tenth of the resistance it has white-hot, so a lamp draws a surge of current at the instant it is switched on, until the filament heats up. Filaments usually fail at that moment.

4Power and energy

The energy a charge loses in a piece of the circuit is the height it falls, and the charge passing each second is the current. Multiply them and you have the rate at which energy turns into heat or light there, the power: P = V × I, in watts. One watt is one joule a second. In the lamp figure, 12 volts times about one ampere is the filament’s 12 watts.

The two words that cause the most confusion in everything that follows are power and energy. Power is a rate, measured in watts. Energy is an amount, measured in watt-hours or joules: a watt-hour is one watt kept up for an hour, 3,600 joules. A kettle draws about two kilowatts, but only for a couple of minutes; a fridge draws a twentieth of that, but all day long. Switch on the kettle and watch the meter:

Your bill charges for energy, in kilowatt-hours. Grids and markets also care about power: how many watts must be available at the worst moment of the year. A household using 3,500 kWh a year averages about 400 W, but occasionally asks for ten times that. Much of the cost of an electricity system is being ready for the moments when everyone asks at once; that thread comes back in essay 5 and essay 8.

5Heat in the wires

In the lamp, the heat is the point. In the wires it is a loss, and Ohm’s law says how big. The height lost in a wire is I × R, so the power lost is I × R × I: it grows with the square of the current. Double the current through the same wire and it gets four times as hot.

This one fact shapes the grid. Suppose a village needs 10 kW at the end of a line with 1 Ω of resistance. Sent at 230 volts, the line carries about 43 amperes and loses about 1.9 kW, almost a fifth as much again as it delivers. Sent at 2,300 volts, the same power needs a tenth of the current and the line loses a hundredth as much, about 19 W. That is why power travels across a country at hundreds of thousands of volts and is stepped down near the people who use it. How it is stepped, and what limits a line, is the subject of the Wires essay.

Heat is also where nearly all the energy ends up. The kettle’s heat is the goal; the motor’s and the laptop’s are what is left after the work is done. Heat is easy to make from electricity, completely and at any scale. Getting electricity back out of heat is the hard direction, and most of the world’s electricity is made that way. Essay 3 is about what that costs.

6What a kilowatt-hour is worth

A kilowatt-hour is a thousand watts for an hour: 3.6 million joules. It is a useful amount of energy. It would boil about eleven litres of water from the tap. It would lift a one-tonne car about 370 metres straight up. A fit person turning a crank can keep up about 100 watts of useful work, so a kilowatt-hour is a long day of hard labour.3

In the United States in 2025 a household paid on average 17.3¢ for one. In Germany it was about 43.7¢, two and a half times as much, for reasons that are mostly not the power station.4 Either way, a day of labour costs less than a cup of coffee.

What it took to deliver is less tiny. If it came from a coal plant converting a third of its fuel’s heat, as a typical one does, the plant burned about a third of a kilogram of coal and sent two thirds of the heat up a cooling tower or into a river.5 Then a generator turned at a fixed speed for it, transformers stepped it up and down, and the wires turned a few per cent of it into heat on the way, every loss following I × I × R.

All of that is in the 17.3¢: the fuel, the machine, the wires and the losses. Each part can get cheaper or dearer on its own, and the century of falling prices in essay 1 was mostly one of them, the machine, getting better. Where the push in every circuit comes from, and why turning heat into electricity has a limit, is essay 3: the machine.
