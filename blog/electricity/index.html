How the World’s Electricity Works

An interactive essay by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

Flip a switch and a lamp lights instantly. Somewhere, hundreds of kilometres away, a machine the size of a house turns very slightly harder. In between are wires that hum at exactly fifty (or, in the Americas and western Japan, sixty) cycles a second, an auction that ran yesterday at noon, a bill built up in layers, and more than a century of engineering, money and politics.

This essay follows that chain from one end to the other: from a single electron in a copper wire, through the machines that push it and the grid that holds them in step, to the markets that decide which machines run and the bill at the end of the month. 1Charge, fields and current

Every atom in a copper wire has electrons, and in a metal some of those electrons are not attached to any particular atom. They wander through the crystal lattice like a gas, bumping into the vibrating ions, moving in every direction at once at more than a thousand kilometres a second. Because they move in every direction equally, nothing flows. There is no current.

Now connect the wire to a battery. The battery pushes a little extra negative charge onto one end of the wire and pulls some away from the other, and this sets up an electric field along the whole length of the wire. The field gives each electron a very gentle shove in one direction between collisions. Their random motion is still there, but now it has a tiny drift superimposed on it:

The drift is very slow: in a kettle cable the electrons creep along at a fraction of a millimetre per second, slower than a snail; with alternating current they merely shuffle back and forth by a few micrometres. Yet the lamp lights instantly, because what travels fast is not the electrons but the field. Close the switch and the push runs along the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light, like water in a pipe that is already full.

So electricity is not a substance that gets delivered. The electrons in your wall were there before you plugged anything in and they will be there afterwards. What is delivered is energy, carried by the fields around the wires.

Voltage is height

Picture a circuit as a landscape. The battery or generator is a pump that lifts charge up a hill; the voltage is the height of the hill (one volt is one joule per coulomb). Charge rolls down through a heater, motor or lamp and gives up its energy as heat, motion or light. The copper wires are nearly flat.

The rate at which energy is delivered is power, and it is simply the height of the hill multiplied by how much charge flows down it each second: P = V × I. Double the voltage at the same current and you deliver twice the power. This little equation will come back, in a very consequential way, when we try to move electricity across a country.

Power and energy

The two words that cause the most confusion in everything that follows are power and energy. Power is a rate, measured in watts. Energy is an amount, measured in watt-hours or joules. A kettle draws about two kilowatts, but only for a couple of minutes; a fridge draws a twentieth of that, but all day long. Switch on the kettle and watch the meter:

Your bill charges for energy, in kilowatt-hours. Grids and markets also care about power: how many watts must be available at the worst moment of the year. A household using 3,500 kWh a year averages about 400 W, but occasionally asks for ten times that. Much of the cost of an electricity system is being ready for the moments when everyone asks at once.

2Making electricity

Batteries make electricity from chemistry, and solar panels make it from light, but nearly all of the world’s electricity has, for more than a century, been made by the effect Michael Faraday discovered in 1831: a changing magnetic field pushes charges around a loop of wire. Move a magnet into a coil and a current flows one way; pull it out and the current flows the other way; hold it still and nothing happens at all.

What matters is the rate of change. A faster movement gives a bigger voltage. The direction of the voltage depends on whether the flux through the coil is increasing or decreasing, which is why pushing the magnet in and pulling it out give opposite kicks.

Pushing a magnet in and out by hand is not a practical way to light a city. Instead, we turn it. Put the same magnet on a shaft inside a coil, seen here end-on, and turn the shaft. As the north pole swings past one side of the coil and then the other, the flux through the coil rises, falls and reverses, over and over. Grab the magnet to turn it yourself, slowly and then fast:

Turn it slowly and the voltage is small; turn it fast and it grows, because what counts is how fast the flux changes. Turn it steadily and the voltage traces a smooth wave, a sine, simply because the coil sees the magnet’s pole from a steadily turning angle. One revolution of a two-pole rotor gives one cycle of voltage, so a two-pole machine must spin at exactly 3,000 rpm for 50 Hz and 3,600 rpm for 60 Hz. A slow hydro turbine with many poles turns at a few hundred rpm and makes the same frequency. Grid frequency is, quite literally, the rotational speed of its generators.

Three phases

A single coil’s power pulses: full, nothing, full, nothing, a hundred times a second. Large machines and motors dislike pulsing torque. The fix, standard since the 1890s, is to add two more coils to the same machine, a third of a turn round the stator. The magnet reaches each one a third of a turn later, so each makes the same sine wave, 120° behind the last: phase A, phase B and phase C.

Switch to Add them up, then Power. Two things follow. First, the three voltages add to exactly zero at every instant, so the three wires can serve as each other’s return path and no fourth heavy conductor is needed. Second, the three pulsing power flows add up to a perfectly steady total. A three-phase generator feels a smooth, constant load on its shaft. This is why every high-voltage line you have ever seen carries its conductors in threes.

Put the pieces together and you have the machine at the heart of almost every power station: a magnet turning inside three pairs of coils. A real one is the same arrangement scaled up to hundreds of megawatts, with an electromagnet in place of the permanent magnet.

3Heat, wind and light

A generator converts rotation into electricity at better than 98% efficiency. The hard part is getting the rotation, and the world gets it in very different ways.

The world’s electricity in one picture

The world generated about 31,900 TWh of electricity in 2025, a third of it in China. Coal is still the largest single source, at a third of the total; solar, a rounding error in 2015, now matches nuclear at about 9%.1

The mixes differ enormously. India runs mostly on coal, the Middle East on gas, Brazil on hydro and France on nuclear; Australia gets a fifth of its power from the sun. Germany appears often in this essay because its data is unusually open, but it is a small grid by world standards, and where a mechanism plays out differently in Texas, Delhi or São Paulo the figures say so.

Heat

For most of the twentieth century, and still for the majority of the world’s electricity today, the rotation has come from heat: burn coal or gas, or split uranium, boil water into high-pressure steam, and let the steam expand through a turbine.

Heat engines have a ceiling that no engineering can raise. Sadi Carnot showed in 1824 that an engine working between a hot source at absolute temperature Thot and a cold sink at Tcold can convert at most a fraction 1 − Tcold / Thot of the heat into work. The rest must be dumped into the cold side — the river, the sea, or the plume above a cooling tower. Hotter steam means a higher ceiling:

This chart holds a story we will come back to. From the 1900s to the 1960s engineers pushed steam from a few hundred degrees to about 540–600 °C, and every step saved coal. Then, around 1960, the climb stopped. The limit was metallurgy, not Carnot: hotter steels crept, cracked and corroded. A typical American coal plant sat near 35% efficiency for decades, a plateau we return to in section 13.

Wind

A wind turbine skips the heat. It takes kinetic energy directly from moving air. The power carried by wind through a rotor of area A is ½ ρ A v³, where ρ is the density of air and v its speed. That cube is the most important fact about wind power: double the wind speed and the available power rises eightfold.

Why can’t a turbine take all of it? If it stopped the air completely, the air would pile up and nothing more could flow through. Slow it too little and you extract little; too much and the flow goes around the rotor. In 1920 Albert Betz found the optimum: slow the wind by a third and capture 16/27, about 59%, of the power in the stream.

Sunlight

Solar panels have no moving parts at all: a photon knocks an electron loose in a silicon junction and the junction’s built-in field sweeps it out into the circuit. The physics is elegant; the economics are dominated by a simpler fact — the sun is only up half the time, and it is low in the sky in winter.

Move the day slider to December and the output collapses to a short, shallow hump. Over a year, a panel in northern Germany delivers on average around a tenth of its nameplate power. This number — the capacity factor — is what turns the price of a panel into the price of a kilowatt-hour, and it is why the same panel produces cheaper electricity in Spain than in Hamburg.

4Moving it

Power stations are rarely where the people are. China’s coal, wind and sun are in the far west, its factories on the east coast. Brazil’s great dams are in the Amazon, its demand in São Paulo and Rio. Germany’s wind blows on the North Sea coast, far from its southern industry. Moving electricity hundreds or thousands of kilometres runs into the formula from section 1. A wire with resistance R carrying current I turns I²R watts into heat. For a fixed amount of power P = V × I, raising the voltage lowers the current — and the losses fall with the square of the current:

At household voltage a few hundred megawatts would melt the wires. At 380 kV, the backbone voltage of much of Europe, the figure’s gigawatt crosses 300 km on one circuit losing about 6%; spread over two circuits, as German grid operators usually build such lines, about 3%.3 China goes further: since 2019 a ±1,100 kV direct-current line has carried up to 12 GW about 3,300 km from Xinjiang to Anhui, and Brazil’s ±800 kV lines bring Belo Monte’s Amazon hydropower more than 2,000 km south.3

High voltage is dangerous in a home and expensive in a generator, so the voltage has to change along the way. This is why alternating current beat Edison’s direct current a century ago: AC voltage is easy to change, with a transformer. A transformer is Faraday’s experiment with nothing moving: alternating current in one coil makes an alternating flux in an iron core, which induces a voltage in a second coil. Twenty times the turns gives twenty times the voltage and a twentieth of the current.

A generator’s 21 kV is stepped up to 380 kV for the journey, then down in stages to 230 V at the socket.

Part 3 opens the box, and Part 4 follows the power along the lines.

The modern exception is high-voltage direct current. Converters made of power electronics can now turn AC into DC and back efficiently, and for very long distances or undersea cables DC wins. Germany’s planned north–south corridors are DC lines for exactly this reason. Unlike AC lines, whose flows are decided by physics, a DC link’s flow can be set precisely, which matters in section 9.

5Fifty hertz

Continental Europe, from Portugal to Turkey, is a single synchronous machine, and so is the eastern half of North America. Thousands of generators like the one above turn in lockstep, their magnetic fields locked to each other through the wires.

And the grid stores almost no energy. At every instant, generation must equal consumption plus losses. When they differ, the difference comes, in the first seconds, from the only store there is: the kinetic energy of all those spinning rotors. If consumption wins, the rotors slow and the frequency falls. Frequency is therefore a live, continent-wide measurement of balance, reported by every socket: 50 Hz across Europe, most of Asia, Africa and Australia, 60 Hz across most of the Americas.

The figure opens as one large power station, a gigawatt, drops off the grid without warning. The rotors slip, the frequency dips, and the reserves catch it well clear of trouble. Several things happen, on different clocks:

In the first instant, nothing but physics responds. The missing power is drawn from the rotating mass of every machine on the grid, and the frequency starts to fall at a rate set by how much rotating mass there is — the inertia. Then some load relief appears on its own: many motors draw slightly less power when they turn slightly slower.

Within seconds the primary reserve wakes up. Selected plants and batteries measure frequency locally and raise output in proportion to the deviation, with no instruction from anyone. The fall stops at the nadir, about 49.75 Hz in this model; Europe’s rules allow at most 800 mHz of instantaneous deviation. By design, primary reserve only holds the frequency at a new, slightly low level.

Over minutes, the secondary reserve takes over. A central controller at the grid operator sees the persistent error and dispatches more generation until the frequency is back at exactly 50.000 Hz, freeing the primary reserve for the next incident. After fifteen minutes or so, slower tertiary reserves and the market replace the lost plant for good.

Continental Europe is designed to survive a bigger blow: the sudden loss of three gigawatts, roughly two large nuclear units (Trip 3 GW). Losing fifteen is far beyond the design case. Primary reserve is overwhelmed, the frequency falls through 49.0 Hz, and automatic relays disconnect slices of customers so the rest can survive. That is how Europe survived its split in November 2006, when a line switching in northern Germany cascaded into three islands.

Large spinning machines provide inertia for free; wind turbines and solar panels behind power electronics do not, unless their inverters are programmed to imitate it. Turn the inertia down (under More) and the same trip makes the frequency fall faster and deeper, giving the reserves less time. That is why fast batteries, synchronous condensers and “grid-forming” inverters are joining the toolkit, and why holding reserves costs money that ends up on the network side of the bill.

6The merit order

Frequency tells the grid, second by second, whether supply matches demand. Most of that matching is arranged a day ahead. How does the market decide which plants run, and at what price? In most liberalised markets, from Europe’s day-ahead auction to Texas and Australia, every generator submits, for every hour (or quarter-hour) of tomorrow, how much it is willing to produce at what price. Every buyer submits how much it wants to buy. An algorithm then stacks all the offers from cheapest to most expensive — the merit order — and finds where supply meets demand.8

What does a plant offer? In a competitive market, roughly its short-run marginal cost: what one more megawatt-hour costs it to make right now. For wind and solar that is close to zero, since the fuel is free; for nuclear it is low; for a fossil plant it is mostly fuel, plus carbon permits where carbon is priced. Here are a country’s offers for one hour, in the order they happened to arrive. Sort them:

Sorted cheapest first, the offers form a staircase: the supply curve. Demand is a vertical line, how much the country needs this hour. Every plant to the left of it runs; every plant to the right waits. And everyone who runs is paid the same price, the offer of the last plant needed. This is uniform pricing, or pay-as-clear, and it is the most misunderstood feature of electricity markets. Drag the demand line across the edge of a block and the price jumps, not just for the extra megawatt-hours but for all of them.

Why not pay each plant what it bid? Because then nobody would bid their real cost. A wind farm that expects gas to set the price would bid just under it, and so would everyone else: roughly the same price, but with everyone guessing and dearer plants sometimes running ahead of cheaper ones. Pay-as-clear makes bidding your true cost the sensible strategy.

Now let the wind blow. A wind farm’s offer sits at the bottom of the stack, so more wind does not add a block at the top: it widens a block at the bottom and pushes every other plant to the right. Drag the wind handle:

The demand line now crosses the staircase lower down, at a cheaper plant. This is the merit-order effect: wind and solar lower the price in the hours they produce, including for the coal and gas plants that still run, and, as the next section shows, for themselves.

Last, the fuel. A fossil plant’s height is its fuel price divided by its efficiency, plus the carbon price times its emissions. Lignite burns cheap fuel but emits about twice as much CO₂ per unit of heat as gas, so its block climbs steeply with the carbon price. Here is the full market, with both prices to play with:

Move the gas and carbon sliders. At low carbon prices lignite and coal undercut gas; push carbon up and gas slides ahead of coal, while lignite climbs fastest. This fuel switch is how a carbon price changes what burns, hour by hour, and why Europe’s wholesale price is so often the price of gas plus carbon. Then push gas to around $170 per megawatt-hour of heat, roughly where it stood in 2022. The marginal plant in most hours is gas-fired, and its cost sets the price for everyone, including wind farms whose costs didn’t change at all.

Every plant below the marginal one is paid the clearing price but spends only its own running cost. The gap, shaded under Who profits?, is what it keeps. That keep is called infra-marginal rent, and it is normally how plants with low running costs pay back the cost of building them. In 2022 the rents were extraordinary, and governments taxed them.

7A day on the grid

Zoom out from one hour to a whole day and the balancing problem changes. Demand has a shape: low at night, a morning ramp, a working-day plateau, an early-evening peak. Solar is a hump at noon and wind wanders over hours and days. Whatever they don’t cover, something else must: the residual load.

The figure opens at 13:00 on a spring Sunday, with about the solar Germany now has installed, some 110 GW. The residual load has all but vanished, the price sits below zero, and some wind and solar is switched off because nobody can use it. As the sun sets, the residual load ramps steeply back up and the evening price climbs. Drag across the chart to follow the day, and slide the solar down: the noon price recovers.

California named this shape. Its grid operator, CAISO, drew the duck curve in 2013: a midday belly of net demand that deepens every year, then a steep evening neck. In 2024 it curtailed 3.4 TWh of wind and solar, almost all of it solar and most of it in spring, when sunshine is strong and air conditioners are off.5

Negative prices are not a glitch. Germany’s day-ahead market had 573 negative hours in 2025, up from 457 the year before.6 Some producers are paid per kilowatt-hour whatever the market price (many older solar panels are on fixed feed-in tariffs), and some plants are expensive to stop and restart. Both will pay to keep producing.

This has an awkward consequence for solar itself. Every panel produces in the same hours as every other panel, so the more there are, the lower the price in exactly those hours. A panel ends up selling at a discount to the day’s average, and the discount deepens as the fleet grows. That ratio, what solar earns per megawatt-hour over the average price of all hours, is its capture rate:

This is called cannibalisation. It doesn’t mean solar is bad; it means the value of the next panel depends on what is already on the grid, and that the cheapest kilowatt-hours are cheap partly because they arrive when nobody needs them. Remember this when we get to the cost of building power plants in section 11: a cost per kilowatt-hour is only half of the story; the other half is when those kilowatt-hours arrive.

8Markets in time

The merit order clears once a day, at noon, for all of tomorrow. But tomorrow is uncertain. A wind farm that sold 10 GW for 18:00 may learn by morning that the wind will be weaker than forecast.

So electricity is traded in a sequence of markets, each closer to real time.10 The day-ahead auction sets most of tomorrow’s schedule and the price quoted in the news. The intraday market then lets traders adjust as forecasts improve, until shortly before delivery. After that, the grid operator activates balancing reserves and charges each party an imbalance price for its error. Press Play to follow one wind farm’s hour through them:

American markets organise the same physics differently. Most US regions have a day-ahead market and then a real-time market that clears every five minutes, and the real-time price serves as the final settlement for deviations.11 Same problem, different labels.

Two extremes bracket everything else. On a sunny, windy holiday the market is oversupplied and prices go negative, in Europe down to the auction floor of about −$570/MWh at worst. In a cold, dark, windless winter week almost every plant is needed and the price is set by the dearest peakers, or by buyers’ willingness to stop consuming. Both kinds of hours are becoming more frequent, and a market of mostly zero-cost wind and solar is increasingly decided by them. We return to the dark week in section 15.

9Markets in space

So far we have pretended the grid is a copper plate. It isn’t. Every line has a thermal limit, and you cannot choose which route the power takes: in an AC network it flows along every path between source and sink, split by impedance. Send power north to south and some goes the long way round, through your neighbour’s country if the physics says so.

The figure opens in nodal mode, how most large US markets work, with the northern wind set at the exact moment the West–South line fills. Drag the wind down a notch and all four nodes share one price. Push it up and the prices split: power is trapped in the north, where wind is curtailed and the price can fall to zero, and scarce in the south, where a local peaker must run. The small chart traces that fork across every wind level. These locational marginal prices show where new generation, storage or demand is worth most; the price gap across a full line, times its flow, is the operator’s congestion rent.

Now switch to zonal, which is how Europe, Australia’s NEM and Japan work: Germany and Luxembourg, for example, are one bidding zone with one price. The market clears as if the grid were a copper plate, and the resulting flows break the line limits. So the operators intervene afterwards, paying northern wind farms to curtail and southern gas plants to start. This redispatch never shows up in the wholesale price; it is socialised into network charges, one of the ways the cost of the energy transition migrated into the regulated part of the bill.12

Neither system is free. Nodal pricing is efficient but makes customers in some places pay more; zonal pricing keeps one price but hides the cost of the grid in a socialised charge and blunts the signal for where to build. Splitting Germany into a northern and a southern zone is one of the live debates of European market design; for now Berlin has chosen one zone and more lines.

Part 4 follows the same physics onto a map of central Europe, where it costs Germany about $3.4 billion a year.

Nodal and zonal markets are only two arrangements. Most of the world’s electricity is not sold on a spot exchange at all. China’s grid is run by two state-owned companies, with plants paid regulated or negotiated prices, though provincial spot markets are spreading. India’s state distribution companies buy mainly under long-term contracts. Much of Africa and the Gulf has a single state utility or buyer. Texas and Australia run “energy-only” markets with very high price caps; PJM and Britain add capacity auctions.13

10From the exchange to the meter

Everything so far has been about the wholesale price, the price of a megawatt-hour on the exchange. Between the exchange and the meter sit the same layers everywhere, in different proportions. Germany publishes every one of them, so start there:

For most of the 2010s a megawatt-hour cost $35–50. Then Europe’s gas crisis dragged the marginal gas plant, and with it every megawatt-hour, to a monthly peak of $489 in August 2022. By 2025 the average was back to about $100: still above the 2010s, because gas stayed dearer and carbon rose.6

A hundred dollars a megawatt-hour is ten cents a kilowatt-hour. A German household signing a new contract in 2026 was offered, on average, 43 cents.15 Where do the other 33 come from?

Three layers, each with its own logic.

Procurement starts from the wholesale price, but a retailer must buy power in the shape its customers use, heavy in winter evenings, and buys most of it in advance to shield them from spikes. So this layer lags the spot price: in 2023 (click its bar) spot had fallen, but households were still paying for power bought in the crisis. With sales costs and margin it came to 17.6 ¢ in 2026, about 41% of the price.

Network charges, 10.7 ¢ or a quarter of the bill, pay for the wires, including the redispatch and reserves of sections 5 and 9. They rose for years with the grid build-out; the 2026 drop is a federal subsidy, not a cheaper grid. Part 4’s network costs follows the money.

Taxes and levies are the remaining third, led by 19% VAT (6.8 ¢) and the electricity tax (2.4 ¢). The famous EEG levy that funded feed-in tariffs, once nearly 8 ¢, was moved to the federal budget in July 2022. The fair summary is not that “renewables are expensive” but that the system transition costs money, and that money has migrated into networks, surcharges and the budget.

What “43 cents” is, and isn’t. It is the average offer for new household contracts at 3,500 kWh a year, fixed charges included. Customers on old or default tariffs can pay noticeably more or less, and network charges vary by region. It is the best single number available, but it is a construct.

The United States shows the same divergence from lower levels. In PJM, the largest US wholesale market, the wholesale price in 2024 was 3.4 ¢/kWh, about a quarter above 2019’s; the average residential price rose by a third, to 17.3 ¢ in 2025.16 Most of the gap is delivery: wires, poles, storm hardening and the returns that pay for them. Cheap wholesale power no longer means cheap power at the meter.

Across Europe the same three layers appear in very different proportions, and the ranking flips once prices are measured against what money buys locally.17

Worldwide the spread is wider still: households pay around forty US cents a kilowatt-hour in Italy and Germany, about eight in China and India, two in Egypt. Cheap fuel explains part of it; policy explains much of the rest, from light taxes to subsidies to industry cross-subsidising homes. Tariffs below cost are paid for in utility debt and, in places, power cuts.18

All of this assumes a meter on a wall. About 730 million people, eight in ten of them in sub-Saharan Africa, still had no electricity in 2024.20 Many more are on grids that fail often, like South Africa’s during the rolling cuts of 2023.21 Where grid power is dear or unreliable, people build their own: Pakistan imported about 17 GW of solar panels in 2024, mostly for rooftops.22

11What a power plant costs

The merit order runs on marginal cost, what one more megawatt-hour costs from a plant that already exists. But someone has to build the plant first, and that money comes from the rent of section 6. So let’s build one and sell its output into that market.

On the left is what the plant costs, spread over every megawatt-hour it will ever make: build cost repaid with interest, plus upkeep, plus fuel. Call that total the levelised cost of electricity, LCOE. On the right is what the market of sections 6 and 7 pays it over a year.

Start with solar. Almost all its cost is capital, and it runs when every other panel does, so it earns less than the average price. Now drag the dot on the right: that is the cost of capital. From 4% to 10% the same panels cost about $50 against $84 per megawatt-hour, while what they earn does not move. When central banks raised rates in 2022–23, they raised the cost of the energy transition directly.

Switch to New nuclear: it runs almost every hour, but its capital alone, about $155/MWh, exceeds the $111 the market pays. Gas combined cycle is cheap to build and dear to run; its thin rent covers little more than a third of its fixed costs. Now choose the 2022-style gas shock. Gas runs in an eighth of the hours yet covers about half its costs, because gas sets the price; wind and nuclear, whose costs did not change, see their rent roughly double.

Lazard’s 2026 US midpoints are about $69/MWh for utility solar, $68 for onshore wind, $90 for gas combined cycle and $215 for new nuclear, with solar, wind and gas all dearer than a year earlier. Utility solar had fallen roughly tenfold from 2009 to 2021: a partial healing, then a partial relapse.2324

Two cautions. IRENA’s global averages, $34/MWh for onshore wind and $43 for solar in 2024, are dominated by large, cheap projects in China; they are not the cost of a wind farm in Brandenburg or Texas.

12Renewables, nuclear and learning

Lazard starts in 2009 and so hides the most important fact about solar: since 1975 the average price of a module has fallen about five-hundredfold, to about 27 cents a watt in 2024 (in 2025 dollars), while the world’s installed solar grew to some 1,900 GW. On logarithmic axes the points fall close to a straight line.25

Each doubling of the world’s installed solar has cut the module price by about the same fraction, roughly 24%, and closer to a third since 2010. That straight line is a learning curve, or Wright’s law, after the engineer who saw the same pattern in aircraft in 1936. Electricity from new solar farms followed, from about $408/MWh in 2010 to $43, and onshore wind more slowly.

Part 1 visits the factories behind this curve.

The curve is not a law of nature; it is what happens when an industry makes the same thing in enormous numbers. It also gets harder to ride: the next doubling means another 1,900 GW, and modules are now a minority of a solar farm’s cost, while land, labour, grid connections and capital learn slowly. Switch the fit window to “recent” and drag the learning rate: small changes compound into very different futures. Batteries ride a similar curve: BloombergNEF’s annual survey reported an average of $108/kWh per pack in 2025.26

Cheap energy, expensive reliability

A cheap megawatt-hour is not a cheap system. LCOE already counts how often a plant runs. It does not count that every extra panel produces at the same moments as the rest, so its price falls as the fleet grows; nor that somebody must be there in the dark, windless week. Lazard puts that firming at roughly $8 to $62 per MWh, depending on the grid operator.23 That gap is where storage, transmission, flexible demand and firm generation compete.

Nuclear: the curve that bent the wrong way

Nuclear is the obvious firm, clean source, and its history is the mirror image of solar’s: in the West its costs rose with experience. Arnulf Grubler found French construction costs climbing over successive reactor generations even in the most standardised Western build-out, “negative learning by doing”.27 Recent projects show the problem starkly:

Vogtle 3 and 4 in Georgia were supposed to cost $14 billion and came to about $35 billion, seven years late. Flamanville 3 went from about $4 billion to about $27 billion with financing; Hinkley Point C from about $24 billion to about $45 billion, with its first unit expected in 2030 (in their own currencies, €3.3 billion to €23.7 billion and £18 billion to £35 billion).28

These are first-of-a-kind projects: new designs, rusty supply chains and schedules long enough for interest to pile up. The optimistic reading is that series build is cheaper, as South Korea’s near-identical reactors suggest.30 The pessimistic one is that the West has never paid for a series to find out.

Small reactors, big claims

Small modular reactors are the industry’s answer: build the reactor in a factory and learn through numbers rather than size. The evidence so far is thin. NuScale’s first project saw its target price rise from $58 to $89 per MWh and was cancelled in 2023.29 Ontario’s first BWRX-300s at Darlington are budgeted at about C$17,000 per kilowatt, in Vogtle’s range, and no US SMR has yet produced power.31

Small reactors give up economies of scale and must win them back through factory learning. That can work; solar shows how powerful repetition is. But that SMRs will beat large reactors built in series is an argument, not an observation, until somebody builds the first ten.

Geography changes the answer more than any slider. Under Chilean or Indian sun a panel yields roughly twice as much; hydro systems like Norway’s carry their own seasonal storage. The trade-off has the same shape everywhere, but its numbers are local.

13A century of cheapening

From the 1890s to the early 1970s, electricity got cheaper almost every year: the real US residential price fell about 5% a year from 1892 to 1973.32 Then it stopped, and in nominal terms doubled between 1973 and 1980.33

Why did the cheapening stop? Four layers broke at the same time.

Physics. As section 3 showed, steam temperatures, and with them efficiency, hit a materials ceiling in the 1960s. For seventy years every new plant had burned less fuel per kilowatt-hour than the last. Now they didn’t.

Scale. A 500 MW unit cost far less per kilowatt than a 50 MW one, and demand growing about 7¾% a year let every new unit be bigger. Past about 1,000 MW the units grew unreliable and the economies reversed, Richard Hirsh’s “technological stasis”. Then demand growth itself broke.

Fuel. Crude went from about $3 to $12 a barrel in four months of 1973–74, and utilities passed it straight to customers. Coal and gas followed oil upwards.

Institutions. Pollution rules added scrubbers and cooling towers; nuclear build times stretched from seven years to twelve just as interest rates soared. Why US nuclear costs rose so much is still contested.30

The relative weight of these layers matters, and a toy shows it:

Switch the shocks off but stop the engine of improvement, and prices stop falling but barely rise. Run the engine with the shocks on, and they are mostly absorbed. Only stasis plus shocks makes prices jump and stay up. The plateau did not cause the 1970s spike; it removed the productivity growth that had quietly offset every rising cost.30

Other countries broke differently: France built standard nuclear, the Nordics leaned on hydro, Britain switched to gas. But the lesson travels: electricity gets cheaper only when an engine of productivity runs faster than the costs rising around it. Wind, solar and batteries restarted that engine in the 2010s. The question is whether wires, firming, permitting and capital can keep up.

Elsewhere the arc was electrification itself. Soviet Russia made a national grid a founding project of the state in 1920; Japan is still split between 50 and 60 Hz because Tokyo and Osaka bought German and American generators in the 1890s. China connected its last households in 2015, and India went from about half its people to nearly all in thirty years.35

Why does any of this matter beyond the bill?

14Why the price of electricity moves everything else

Energy looks like a small part of the economy: Americans spent 5.2% of GDP on it in 2024.36 But a small, unavoidable input can have a large effect when its price jumps, because nobody can stop using it quickly.

By 1981 that share had climbed to 13.3%, and the energy spikes of 1974, 1980–81 and 2008 each sit next to a recession. James Hamilton found that ten of eleven post-war US recessions were preceded by an oil price rise.37 The mechanism is simple: when energy costs jump, everyone spends less on everything else.

A kilowatt-hour in a motor, heat pump or electric car often replaces two to four of fuel, so electrification spreads fast: electricity was about 20% of the world’s final energy in 2023.38 Increasingly, the price of electricity is the price of energy.

What a megawatt-hour unlocks

For some products electricity is the main ingredient. Drag the price: the rows where power is most of the cost swing wildly, the rest barely twitch.

Aluminium is the classic case: at 14 MWh a tonne, every $10/MWh adds $140 to the metal, so smelters follow cheap power to Iceland, Quebec, the Gulf and above all China, which makes well over half the world’s supply.41 Steel made with hydrogen instead of coal turns a mill into one of the largest electricity customers in any country.

Part 5 adds the missing input, the electrolyser itself, and tests the cheap-hours idea against a year of real prices.

So industrial power prices have become national strategy. American industry paid 8.62 ¢/kWh in 2025; small German firms about 20 ¢ in 2026.1516 From 2026 Germany’s Industriestrompreis refunds energy-intensive firms the gap between a reference price and a floor of about $56/MWh on half their use.40 China does it through tariffs, the Gulf through cheap gas: each an admission that the price of a megawatt-hour decides where industry can exist.

Hydrogen is where the arithmetic gets hard. At about 55 kWh a kilogram, $50/MWh power alone costs $2.75, nearly three times the US target of $1 all-in.39 Every green-hydrogen plan is a bet on power well under $20/MWh for many hours a year.

Computing is the other extreme: an hour of an H100 GPU uses about ten cents of power, tiny next to its worth. But data centres used about 1.5% of the world’s electricity in 2024, heading for twice that by 2030, and their binding constraint is how fast firm supply can be connected.38

Cheap electricity decides which industries are possible, and where. Can it get cheaper again?

15Futures

Nobody knows what electricity will cost in 2040. What we can do is play with the mechanisms that would make useful electricity cheaper.

Moving the surplus

The midday solar surplus is the cheapest energy in history and, raw, close to worthless. The direct fix is to move it in time, with batteries and with demand that shifts: cars that charge at noon, heat pumps that pre-heat. Slide both up and watch the negative prices disappear:

Batteries raise the price at noon and lower it in the evening. Flatten prices enough and the arbitrage that pays for them disappears: storage, like solar, cannibalises its own value. And short batteries solve a daily problem. The harder one is weekly.

Part 2 runs a battery at a real solar farm near Seville.

The dark week

Every grid has its bad week. Germany has the Dunkelflaute, the still, grey winter days; Texas had Winter Storm Uri in 2021; Brazil fears drought emptying its reservoirs. The figure plays the German kind.

Over the week there is plenty of renewable energy, but in the lull the batteries empty in one evening and demand goes unserved. The ways out are storage measured in days, or firm capacity that idles most of the year. Neither appears in any LCOE. This is resource adequacy, and it is why in September 2026 the EU approved up to about $40 billion of German support for some 11–12 GW of new, mostly gas-fired capacity, and why markets like PJM pay plants simply to be available.42

Electrifying heat

In cold countries one of the biggest new demands will be heat. A heat pump is a refrigerator run backwards, moving heat from the outside air into the house: three to four units of heat per unit of electricity, its coefficient of performance (COP).

The physics favours the heat pump on almost any day. What decides it is the ratio of the two prices. At German prices a heat pump needs a COP of about 2.8 just to break even; in Britain, where gas is cheap relative to power, about 3.8. A bill that loads levies onto each kilowatt-hour penalises the most efficient heating there is. Rate design may decide the transition.

What would have to be true

For electricity at the meter to get cheaper in real terms over the next twenty years, these would have to be true. They are conditions, not predictions:

  • The generation engine keeps running. Solar, wind and batteries resume their cost declines after the 2021–26 relapse, and capital stays affordable.
  • The surplus finds a use. Storage and flexible demand move midday energy into the evening, and legacy subsidies stop rewarding output at negative prices.
  • Firm capacity is bought cheaply. The dark week is covered by the cheapest mix of firm plants, long storage, interconnection and demand response, bought through markets rather than rescues.
  • Wires get built faster. About 2,300 GW of US projects sat in interconnection queues at the end of 2024, and the median wait to connect has stretched to over four years.43
  • Network costs grow slower than the energy they carry. New loads, from heat pumps to data centres (US data centres alone may use 325–580 TWh by 202844), spread fixed costs over more kilowatt-hours if the grid is used more intensively.
  • Bills tell the truth. Electricity is taxed less relative to fossil fuels, and tariffs reflect time and place.

Each can fail. The 1970s suggest the most dangerous outcome is not one shock but a stalled engine meeting a run of bad luck.

Still, look at where we started: an electron creeping along a wire, a magnet turning in a coil, continents of machines spinning in lockstep, held there by the reflexes of a few thousand power plants and an auction at noon. Its price is a sum of physics, engineering, markets and political choices, and all four are still moving.
