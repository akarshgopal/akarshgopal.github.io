How the World’s Electricity Works

An interactive essay by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

Flip a switch and a lamp lights instantly. Somewhere, hundreds of kilometres away, a machine the size of a house turns very slightly harder. In between are wires that hum at exactly fifty (or, in the Americas and western Japan, sixty) cycles a second, an auction that ran yesterday at noon, a bill built up in layers, and more than a century of engineering, money and politics.

This essay follows that chain from one end to the other: from a single electron in a copper wire, through the machines that push it and the grid that holds them in step, to the markets that decide which machines run and the bill at the end of the month. 1Charge, fields and current

Every atom in a copper wire has electrons, and in a metal some of those electrons are not attached to any particular atom. They wander through the crystal lattice like a gas, bumping into the vibrating ions, moving in every direction at once at more than a thousand kilometres a second. Because they move in every direction equally, nothing flows. There is no current.

Now connect the wire to a battery. The battery pushes a little extra negative charge onto one end of the wire and pulls some away from the other, and this sets up an electric field along the whole length of the wire. The field gives each electron a very gentle shove in one direction between collisions. Their random motion is still there, but now it has a tiny drift superimposed on it:

The drift is very slow: in a kettle cable the electrons creep along at a fraction of a millimetre per second, slower than a snail; with alternating current they merely shuffle back and forth by a few micrometres. Yet the lamp lights instantly, because what travels fast is not the electrons but the field. Close the switch and the push runs along the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light, like water in a pipe that is already full.

So electricity is not a substance that gets delivered. The electrons in your wall were there before you plugged anything in and they will be there afterwards. What is delivered is energy, carried by the fields around the wires.

Voltage is height

Picture a circuit as a landscape. The battery or generator is a pump that lifts charge up a hill; the voltage is the height of the hill (one volt is one joule per coulomb). Charge rolls down through a heater, motor or lamp and gives up its energy as heat, motion or light. The copper wires are nearly flat.

The rate at which energy is delivered is power, and it is simply the height of the hill multiplied by how much charge flows down it each second: P = V × I. Double the voltage at the same current and you deliver twice the power. This little equation will come back, in a very consequential way, when we try to move electricity across a country.

Power and energy

The two words that cause the most confusion in everything that follows are power and energy. Power is a rate, measured in watts. Energy is an amount, measured in watt-hours or joules. A kettle draws about two kilowatts, but only for a couple of minutes; a fridge draws a twentieth of that, but all day long. Switch on the kettle and watch the meter:

Your bill charges for energy, in kilowatt-hours. Grids and markets also care about power: how many watts must be available at the worst moment of the year. A household using 3,500 kWh a year averages about 400 W, but occasionally asks for ten times that. Much of the cost of an electricity system is being ready for the moments when everyone asks at once.

2Making electricity

Batteries make electricity from chemistry, and solar panels make it from light, but nearly all of the world’s electricity has, for more than a century, been made by the effect Michael Faraday discovered in 1831: a changing magnetic field pushes charges around a loop of wire. Move a magnet into a coil and a current flows one way; pull it out and the current flows the other way; hold it still and nothing happens at all.

What matters is the rate of change. A faster movement gives a bigger voltage. The direction of the voltage depends on whether the flux through the coil is increasing or decreasing, which is why pushing the magnet in and pulling it out give opposite kicks.

Pushing a magnet in and out by hand is not a practical way to light a city. Instead, we turn it. Put the same magnet on a shaft inside a coil, seen here end-on, and turn the shaft. As the north pole swings past one side of the coil and then the other, the flux through the coil rises, falls and reverses, over and over. Grab the magnet to turn it yourself, slowly and then fast:

Turn it slowly and the voltage is small; turn it fast and it grows, because what counts is how fast the flux changes. Turn it steadily and the voltage traces a smooth wave, a sine, simply because the coil sees the magnet’s pole from a steadily turning angle. One revolution of a two-pole rotor gives one cycle of voltage, so a two-pole machine must spin at exactly 3,000 rpm for 50 Hz and 3,600 rpm for 60 Hz. A slow hydro turbine with many poles turns at a few hundred rpm and makes the same frequency. Grid frequency is, quite literally, the rotational speed of its generators.

Three phases

A single coil’s power pulses: full, nothing, full, nothing, a hundred times a second. Large machines and motors dislike pulsing torque. The fix, standard since the 1890s, is to add two more coils to the same machine, a third of a turn round the stator. The magnet reaches each one a third of a turn later, so each makes the same sine wave, 120° behind the last: phase A, phase B and phase C.

Switch to Add them up, then Power. Two things follow. First, the three voltages add to exactly zero at every instant, so the three wires can serve as each other’s return path and no fourth heavy conductor is needed. Second, the three pulsing power flows add up to a perfectly steady total. A three-phase generator feels a smooth, constant load on its shaft. This is why every high-voltage line you have ever seen carries its conductors in threes.

Put the pieces together and you have the machine at the heart of almost every power station: a magnet turning inside three pairs of coils. A real one is the same arrangement scaled up to hundreds of megawatts, with an electromagnet in place of the permanent magnet.

3Heat, wind and light

A generator converts rotation into electricity at better than 98% efficiency. The hard part is getting the rotation, and the world gets it in very different ways.

The world’s electricity in one picture

The world generated about 31,900 TWh of electricity in 2025, a third of it in China. Coal is still the largest single source, at a third of the total; solar, a rounding error in 2015, now matches nuclear at about 9%.1

The mixes differ enormously. India runs mostly on coal, the Middle East on gas, Brazil on hydro and France on nuclear; Australia gets a fifth of its power from the sun. Germany appears often in this essay because its data is unusually open, but it is a small grid by world standards, and where a mechanism plays out differently in Texas, Delhi or São Paulo the figures say so.

Heat

For most of the twentieth century, and still for the majority of the world’s electricity today, the rotation has come from heat: burn coal or gas, or split uranium, boil water into high-pressure steam, and let the steam expand through a turbine.

Heat engines have a ceiling that no engineering can raise. Sadi Carnot showed in 1824 that an engine working between a hot source at absolute temperature Thot and a cold sink at Tcold can convert at most a fraction 1 − Tcold / Thot of the heat into work. The rest must be dumped into the cold side — the river, the sea, or the plume above a cooling tower. Hotter steam means a higher ceiling:

This chart holds a story we will come back to. From the 1900s to the 1960s engineers pushed steam from a few hundred degrees to about 540–600 °C, and every step saved coal. Then, around 1960, the climb stopped. The limit was metallurgy, not Carnot: hotter steels crept, cracked and corroded. A typical American coal plant sat near 35% efficiency for decades, a plateau we return to in section 13.

Wind

A wind turbine skips the heat. It takes kinetic energy directly from moving air. The power carried by wind through a rotor of area A is ½ ρ A v³, where ρ is the density of air and v its speed. That cube is the most important fact about wind power: double the wind speed and the available power rises eightfold.

Why can’t a turbine take all of it? If it stopped the air completely, the air would pile up and nothing more could flow through. Slow it too little and you extract little; too much and the flow goes around the rotor. In 1920 Albert Betz found the optimum: slow the wind by a third and capture 16/27, about 59%, of the power in the stream.

Sunlight

Solar panels have no moving parts at all: a photon knocks an electron loose in a silicon junction and the junction’s built-in field sweeps it out into the circuit. The physics is elegant; the economics are dominated by a simpler fact — the sun is only up half the time, and it is low in the sky in winter.

Move the day slider to December and the output collapses to a short, shallow hump. Over a year, a panel in northern Germany delivers on average around a tenth of its nameplate power. This number — the capacity factor — is what turns the price of a panel into the price of a kilowatt-hour, and it is why the same panel produces cheaper electricity in Spain than in Hamburg.

4Moving it

Power stations are rarely where the people are. China’s coal, wind and sun are in the far west, its factories on the east coast. Brazil’s great dams are in the Amazon, its demand in São Paulo and Rio. Germany’s wind blows on the North Sea coast, far from its southern industry. Moving electricity hundreds or thousands of kilometres runs into the formula from section 1. A wire with resistance R carrying current I turns I²R watts into heat. For a fixed amount of power P = V × I, raising the voltage lowers the current — and the losses fall with the square of the current:

At household voltage a few hundred megawatts would melt the wires. At 380 kV, the backbone voltage of much of Europe, the figure’s gigawatt crosses 300 km on one circuit losing about 6%; spread over two circuits, as German grid operators usually build such lines, about 3%.3 China goes further: since 2019 a ±1,100 kV direct-current line has carried up to 12 GW about 3,300 km from Xinjiang to Anhui, and Brazil’s ±800 kV lines bring Belo Monte’s Amazon hydropower more than 2,000 km south.3

High voltage is dangerous in a home and expensive in a generator, so the voltage has to change along the way. This is why alternating current beat Edison’s direct current a century ago: AC voltage is easy to change, with a transformer. A transformer is Faraday’s experiment with nothing moving: alternating current in one coil makes an alternating flux in an iron core, which induces a voltage in a second coil. Twenty times the turns gives twenty times the voltage and a twentieth of the current.

A generator’s 21 kV is stepped up to 380 kV for the journey, then down in stages to 230 V at the socket.

The modern exception is high-voltage direct current. Converters made of power electronics can now turn AC into DC and back efficiently, and for very long distances or undersea cables DC wins. Germany’s planned north–south corridors are DC lines for exactly this reason. Unlike AC lines, whose flows are decided by physics, a DC link’s flow can be set precisely, which matters in section 9.
