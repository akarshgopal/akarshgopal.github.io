Solar PV: From Photon to Grid

Essay 5 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

In 2025 the world’s factories shipped about 706 gigawatts of solar modules, more than half the generating capacity of the United States, in a single year. Each module is a sheet of glass over a slice of purified sand, with no moving parts. At the end of 2025 it sold on the spot market for about nine US cents a watt at the factory gate.1

This essay follows the energy from a photon after its eight-minute trip from the sun to a junction in a crystal, a cable, an inverter and the grid, and back to the market of the last essay, where electricity made at noon is increasingly worth almost nothing. On the way: a hard physical limit, a supply chain concentrated in one country, and a price that fell further than almost anyone predicted.

1Light and the band gap

Sunlight arrives as photons, each carrying a fixed parcel of energy set by its colour: about 2.7 electronvolts for blue, 1.8 for red, and less for the infrared that makes up about half of the sun’s energy at the ground. On a clear day a surface square to the sun receives roughly a thousand watts per square metre, spread across the spectrum below.2

A semiconductor’s electrons sit on a floor they cannot leave unless something lifts them over a step of fixed height. A photon smaller than the step does nothing and passes straight through, as if the crystal were glass. A larger one lifts an electron free, but the surplus above the step is lost as heat within a trillionth of a second. That step is the material’s band gap: 1.12 eV in silicon.3 Every absorbed photon, whatever its colour, delivers at most one gap’s worth of energy.

Move the gap and the trade-off is immediate. A low gap, like germanium’s, catches almost every photon but keeps a small slice of each; a high gap keeps a big slice but lets most light through. For silicon, about 19% of the sunlight’s energy is below the gap and 32% is lost above it as heat. What remains is not yet electricity: electrons have been knocked loose but not yet sent anywhere.

2The p–n junction

A free electron in silicon wanders until it finds a hole, an empty slot to fall back into, and gives up its energy as heat or a faint glow. To get work out, the cell needs a one-way street that pushes electrons one way and holes the other before they meet.

That street is a p–n junction. One side of the silicon is doped with phosphorus, which has one electron too many (n-type); the other with boron, which has one too few (p-type). Where they meet, electrons spill across and leave a thin layer of fixed charge and a built-in electric field: the cell’s engine. In the band diagram below, “downhill” for an electron is towards the n side, and for a hole towards the p side:

Open the circuit and charge piles up: electrons on the n side, holes on the p side, making a voltage that opposes the junction’s field. The bands flatten until carriers recombine as fast as light creates them. That balance point, the open-circuit voltage, is about 0.7 V for a good silicon cell, around 60% of the gap. A cell is a light-powered pump that can lift charge by at most that much.

Close the circuit and the electrons have an easier way home, through the wire and the lamp, so less voltage builds up. A short circuit gives the most current and no voltage; an open circuit the most voltage and no current. Neither delivers power. Everything useful happens in between.

3The curve every panel lives on

Plot current against voltage for a module and you get its IV curve: flat at the short-circuit current, a sharp knee, then a cliff to the open-circuit voltage. Power is P = V × I, so it is zero at both ends and peaks at the knee, the maximum power point.

Light and heat move the curve differently. Light scales the current almost exactly: half the sun, half the current, nearly the same voltage. Heat lowers the voltage, by a few tenths of a per cent per degree, because warm silicon recombines carriers faster. A module on a still July afternoon at 60–70 °C can lose a tenth of its rated power, which is why panels have their best minutes on cold, bright spring days.

Datasheet ratings assume “standard test conditions”: 1,000 W/m² of reference light and cells at 25 °C. Real modules rarely see that, so nothing in the field delivers its label for long. The maximum power point tracker that keeps a module near the moving peak lives in the inverter (section 10).

4The Shockley–Queisser limit

In 1961 William Shockley and Hans-Joachim Queisser asked what the best possible single-junction cell could do, with perfect materials and only the losses physics insists on.5 The answer adds one loss to the spectrum split of section 1. Anything that absorbs light also emits it, so a perfect cell glows faintly back at the sky, and that radiative recombination steals some current. The voltage can never reach the band gap.

The peak is broad: anything from 1.1 to 1.5 eV comes within a couple of points. Silicon sits on the left shoulder at 33.4%, but with a handicap. Its gap is indirect, so it absorbs weakly and wafers must be thick, and in thick silicon electrons collide and recombine without emitting light (Auger recombination). That cuts the practical limit to about 29.4%.7

The best laboratory silicon cell, a LONGi back-contact cell certified in 2026, reaches 28.3%, 96% of that limit.6 The way past it is a tandem: a wide-gap cell on top takes the blue and green photons and passes the red and infrared to a silicon cell below. LONGi has announced a certified 35.5% for a perovskite–silicon tandem in 2026, against a theoretical limit of about 43%.6

5Cells, modules and strings

A silicon cell makes about 0.6 V at its maximum power point and, at today’s wafer sizes, well over ten amps. So cells are wired in series: same current, voltages add. A utility module of 144 half-cells gives 40–45 V. About twenty-five modules in series make a string of up to 1,500 V DC, the limit for most inverters and safety rules, and strings are combined in parallel at the inverter.

Series wiring has a weakness: every element must carry the same current. Shade one cell and the rest of the string still pushes full current through it. The shaded cell is forced to run backwards, absorbing power and heating up. So modules contain bypass diodes, usually three, each across a third of the module. When a group of cells cannot carry the string current, its diode lets the current go around, at the cost of that third’s output:

With one cell shaded, a module with diodes loses about a third of its power and its curve grows a second hump, one peak where the diode bypasses the group and another where the shaded cell limits everything. A naive power-point tracker can get stuck on the wrong hump, so modern inverters sweep the whole curve now and then. Remove the diodes and most of the output goes, while the shaded cell burns its neighbours’ power: the origin of the “hot spots” that crack glass.

6PERC, TOPCon, heterojunction, tandem

The junction itself is near-perfect, so a decade of progress in commercial cells has mostly attacked one problem: carriers recombining at surfaces and metal contacts, where broken bonds trap them. Each new cell generation is a more careful way of touching silicon with metal without killing carriers at the contact.

PERC added a passivating layer, which ties up the broken bonds, to the back of the cell; a laser opens it only where the contact must touch. PERC dominated production from the late 2010s. TOPCon replaces that rear with an oxide about 1.5 nanometres thick, capped with doped polysilicon, so carriers tunnel through while the surface stays passivated. Because PERC lines could be upgraded to it, TOPCon went from about 30% of production in 2023 to about 70% in 2024.10

Heterojunction cells wrap the wafer in thin amorphous silicon instead: excellent voltages, but low-temperature processing and more silver or indium. Back-contact cells move all the metal to the rear, so nothing shades the front. They hold the silicon records and, per ITRPV, made the most efficient modules of 2025: 24.1%, against 23.5% for TOPCon and heterojunction.9

These gaps matter more than they look. Most of a solar farm’s cost (glass, frames, steel, land, cabling, labour) scales with area, so a point of efficiency cheapens all of it per watt. That is why the industry retools every few years, and why perovskite tandems are being pushed towards mass production, which ITRPV expects after 2027, despite open questions about their outdoor stability.9

7From sand to module

Arc furnaces reduce quartz to metallurgical silicon, which the energy-hungry Siemens process refines into polysilicon of six or more nines purity. That is pulled into ingots, sliced into wafers a fraction of a millimetre thick, made into cells by doping, coating and printing contacts, and laminated under glass into modules.

Few industries this large are so concentrated. In 2024 China made between 86% and 96.5% of the world’s output at every stage, from polysilicon to modules.10 Industrial policy, cheap coal power, scale and fierce competition between Chinese firms all played a part, and so did repeated overbuilding: polysilicon fell from $460/kg in the 2008 shortage to below $6 in September 2026, and makers are now cutting output to stem losses.11 The world can make about twice as many modules a year as it installs.9

The long-run result is a learning curve, which essay 7 plots: each doubling of cumulative output has cut the module price by about a quarter.9

8The sun’s geometry

How often a module sees 1,000 W/m² depends on where the sun is, which is geometry: latitude, day of the year (the sun’s declination swings 23.4° either side of the equator) and hour. In a high-latitude summer the sun rises in the north-east, climbs high and sets in the north-west; in winter it makes a short, low arc in the south. A module turned away from the sun loses the cosine of the angle.

At high latitudes the seasons swamp everything: in Berlin, at 52.5°N, a clear June day gives a panel tilted at 40° over three times the energy of a clear December day, and a flat panel nearly ten times. Tilting towards the equator, by well under the latitude angle in the north (about 40° in Berlin), catches far more of the low winter sun.

A tracker that turns the panels east to west fattens the day into a plateau, gaining most in the morning and evening. That shape is worth more than its energy, because it puts output into the pricier shoulder hours (section 12). In the United States, 99% of utility-scale solar built in 2024 was on single-axis trackers.13 Two-axis trackers rarely repay their extra steel and motors.

9A kilowatt around the world

Clear-sky curves flatter. Real yields depend on clouds, haze and temperature, so the European Commission’s PVGIS models them from years of satellite weather; the figure shows its output for 1 kWp, panels rated at one kilowatt under standard test conditions.14

Fixed panels yield three times as much in the Atacama as in the Arctic, and a tracker in the Atacama over four times as much as a fixed panel in Tromsø. A kilowatt in Berlin makes about 1,050–1,100 kWh a year; in Seville or Phoenix, over 2,000 on a tracker; in Chile’s Atacama, about 3,000. The right-hand scale gives the same yields as a capacity factor: a plant’s average output, night included, as a share of its nameplate.

The monthly bars matter as much as the totals. A kilowatt in Berlin makes about four and a half times as much in June as in December, the root of the dark week of northern winters. Near the equator the bars are flat, but cloudy tropical skies keep totals modest.

A solar plant’s cost is almost all paid up front, so its cost per kilowatt-hour is roughly capital divided by lifetime output: double the yield, halve the cost. That is why the same panel makes power far more cheaply in Spain than in Hamburg.

Where the panels go

Yield explains where solar is cheapest, not where it is built. In 2025 solar made 8.8% of the world’s electricity, 42% of it in China.15 Growth is now fastest in India, the Middle East and Africa, and panels are cheap enough to spread without any government scheme: Pakistan imported 17 GW in 2024, mostly for rooftops escaping high tariffs and outages.16

10Inverters and clipping

Modules make direct current; the grid runs on alternating current. The inverter in between tracks the maximum power point, turns DC into a sine wave locked to the grid, and increasingly helps run the grid: riding through faults, supporting voltage and, in “grid-forming” designs, providing some of the frequency stability that spinning generators gave.

Modules rarely reach nameplate, so it pays to install more DC panel capacity than AC inverter capacity and let the inverter cap output in the brightest hours. That ratio is the inverter loading ratio (DC/AC). The US benchmark utility plant uses 1.34.17

Raising the ratio loses little at first. A kilowatt of panels on a Seville tracker never delivers more than about 810 W in any hour of 2020, so at 1.34 only 0.1% of the energy is clipped. Yet each kilowatt of inverter, and of the scarce grid connection behind it, now delivers 34% more energy.

Clipping only bites above about 1.5; at 1.8 a tenth of the energy is thrown away. Oversizing flattens output into a plateau, and storing what would be clipped in a battery is the logic of hybrid solar-and-storage plants.

11Rooftop versus field

The module is now a minor part of most systems’ cost. US Department of Energy benchmarks for early 2025 put a utility plant at about $1.12 a watt and a home rooftop system at about $2.95, though both use modules at roughly 30 cents a watt (the US delivered price, well above the factory-gate spot price).17

The difference is mostly “soft” cost. NREL itemises it only for the systems with batteries: for the home system, office work (permits, inspection, interconnection paperwork, sales) and the installer’s overhead and margin come to about $2.20 a watt, more than all the hardware, battery included; a utility plant spreads such costs over a hundred megawatts.

The United States is an expensive extreme. IRENA puts the average utility-scale plant commissioned in 2024 at about 53 cents a watt in India and 59 cents in China, against $1.06 in the United States.18 Fraunhofer ISE puts German rooftops at about $1.10–2.20 a watt,19 and Australia, with standard installs and light permitting, has driven rooftop costs much lower.

Rooftop solar still pays for many households, because it saves the retail price, with all the network charges, levies and taxes of a household bill, while a field plant earns only wholesale. That gap attracts owners, and it is why tariffs that let them skip paying for the grid they still use are increasingly contested.

12The noon problem

Every panel in a country sees roughly the same sun, so, as essay 4 showed, the more panels there are, the lower the price in exactly the hours they produce, and the lower their capture rate. Germany has been running that experiment for real:

Through the 2010s solar earned close to the average. Then the midday dip deepened: in 2025 solar earned about $52/MWh against an average of $101, a capture rate of 52%, and prices were negative in 573 hours, most of them sunny middays, many on weekends and holidays. The 2022 spike shows that the capture rate is set by the market, not the panels: when gas set the price in every hour, solar earned 94% of the average. Hour by hour, 2015’s nearly flat summer day, with a shallow midday dip, has become 2025’s noon canyon.

Panels can do a little about it themselves: trackers and east–west layouts move output out of noon and into the shoulder hours (section 8). The rest depends on the system around them: batteries that move noon energy to the evening, demand that shifts to noon, interconnectors that export the surplus. Since February 2025 new German systems get no market premium in negative-price hours, which nudges them to curtail or store.21

13Thirty years in the weather

A module must survive decades of ultraviolet light, frost-to-70 °C cycling, hail, sand and damp heat. It degrades slowly: a large review of field data found a median loss of about 0.5% a year,22 and warranties now commonly run 25 to 30 years. Newer cells bring new failure modes (some TOPCon cells corrode in damp-heat tests), so today’s long-term record is still being written.

Over thirty years, 0.5% a year costs about 7% of lifetime output, small next to the factor of four between sites. The bigger questions are economic: whether a plant keeps its grid connection, what noon power is worth in 2050, and whether glass, aluminium, silver and silicon can be recycled when the 2,844 GW (DC) installed by 20259 reaches the end of its life.

Within a working lifetime, solar went from a space-programme curiosity to the cheapest new electricity in much of the world. Physics sets a ceiling that silicon has nearly reached; factories have made that physics a commodity; geography sets the yield; markets now set the value. The next essay turns to wind and the fleet as a whole: how wind and solar farms are designed, financed, connected and paid.
