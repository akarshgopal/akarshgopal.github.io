Solar: The Engine Restarts

Essay 6 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

In 2025 the world’s factories shipped about 706 gigawatts of solar modules, more than half the generating capacity of the United States, in a single year. Each module is a sheet of glass over thin slices of purified sand, with no moving parts. At the end of 2025 it sold on the spot market for about nine US cents a watt at the factory gate.1

The first essays of this series told how electricity got cheaper for seventy years and then stopped. Steam plants burned less fuel and grew bigger with each generation, until the materials gave out near 40% efficiency and the biggest units grew unreliable. Essay 5 showed why, after that, a fuel shock passed straight through to every kilowatt-hour: the price is set by the last plant needed.

Solar is the first new engine of cheapening since then, and it runs on something other than heat. This essay follows it from a photon to a factory, to the four countries that build and install it, and to the limit it runs into at noon.

1From light to current

Sunlight arrives as photons, each carrying a fixed parcel of energy set by its colour: about 2.7 electronvolts for blue, 1.8 for red, and less for the infrared that makes up about half of the sun’s energy at the ground. On a clear day a surface square to the sun receives roughly a thousand watts per square metre.2

In a semiconductor, the electrons sit on a floor they cannot leave unless something lifts them over a step of fixed height. A photon smaller than the step passes straight through, as if the crystal were glass. A larger one lifts an electron free, but the surplus above the step is lost as heat within a trillionth of a second. That step is the material’s band gap: 1.12 eV in silicon.3 Every absorbed photon, whatever its colour, delivers at most one gap’s worth of energy.

The gap is a trade-off. A low gap, like germanium’s, catches almost every photon but keeps a small slice of each; a high gap keeps a big slice but lets most light through. For silicon, about 19% of the sunlight’s energy is below the gap and 32% is lost above it as heat.

A loose electron is not yet a current: left alone it wanders until it falls back into a gap in the crystal and gives up its energy as heat. A solar cell adds a one-way street. One side of the silicon is doped with phosphorus, which brings a spare electron; the other with boron, which leaves one missing. Where the two layers meet, a p–n junction, a built-in electric field pushes freed electrons one way and the gaps they left the other, and they can only meet again by going round through the outside circuit.

A good cell pushes charge with about 0.6 volts at full output, so cells are wired in series: a utility module of 144 half-cells gives 40–45 volts, and about twenty-five modules in series make a string of up to 1,500 V. That is the whole machine: layers of doped silicon, a few lines of printed silver, glass, an aluminium frame. Nothing spins and nothing burns.

2A ceiling silicon has nearly reached

Steam plants met a ceiling set by temperature, and stopped well short of it (essay 3). Solar cells have one too. In 1961 William Shockley and Hans-Joachim Queisser asked what the best possible single-junction cell could do, with perfect materials and only the losses physics insists on.4 Their answer adds one loss to the split above: anything that absorbs light also emits it, so even a perfect cell glows faintly back at the sky and loses some current.

The peak is broad, and silicon sits on its left shoulder at 33.4%. Silicon absorbs weakly, so its wafers must be thick, and in thick silicon some electrons are lost in collisions with each other, which cuts the practical limit to about 29.4%.6 The best laboratory silicon cell, certified in 2026, reaches 28.3%, 96% of that limit.5 Unlike steam, silicon has come close to its ceiling.

The progress came from a series of small changes to the same cell, each about one problem: electrons lost where the silicon touches its surfaces and metal contacts. Factories moved from PERC cells to TOPCon, which slips an oxide layer about 1.5 nanometres thick under the rear contact; TOPCon went from about 30% of production in 2023 to about 70% in 2024, largely because existing lines could be upgraded rather than replaced.7 The average mass-produced module of 2025 converted 23.5–24.1% of the light.8

A point of efficiency matters more than it looks. Most of a solar farm’s cost (glass, frames, steel, land, cabling, labour) scales with area, so a more efficient cell cheapens all of it per watt. The next step is a tandem: a wide-gap perovskite cell on top takes the blue and green light and passes the red to silicon below. A certified 35.5% has been announced for one, against a theoretical limit of about 43%; mass production is expected after 2027.58

3From sand to module

Arc furnaces reduce quartz to metallurgical silicon, which is refined into polysilicon of six or more nines purity. That is grown into ingots, sliced into wafers thinner than a fingernail, made into cells by doping, coating and printing contacts, and laminated under glass into modules. Each step is a production line making the same object millions of times a day.

That is the difference from steam. A thermal plant is a one-off, built on site over years, and the old engine made it cheaper by making each one bigger. A 1,000 MW solar farm is well over a million copies of the same module. None of them gets bigger; the factory that makes them gets better. A failed module costs a millionth of the plant’s output, not all of it.

Few industries this large are so concentrated in one country. In 2024 China made between 86% and 96.5% of the world’s output at every step, from polysilicon to modules:7

Industrial policy, cheap coal power, scale and fierce competition between Chinese firms all played a part, and so did repeated overbuilding. Polysilicon fell from $460/kg in the 2008 shortage to below $6 in September 2026, and makers are now cutting output to stem their losses.9 The world can make about twice as many modules a year as it installs.8 For buyers, that glut is the engine running flat out.

4The learning curve

Since 1975 the average price of a module has fallen about five-hundredfold, to about 27 cents a watt as a 2024 yearly average, in 2025 dollars (the spot price had fallen to 9 cents by the end of 2025). Over the same years the world’s installed solar grew to some 1,900 GW by 2024. Plot price against cumulative capacity, both on logarithmic axes, and the points fall close to a straight line.10

A straight line on these axes means each doubling of the world’s installed solar has cut the module price by about the same fraction: roughly 24% over the whole record, and closer to a third since 2010. This is the learning curve, also called Wright’s law, after the engineer who saw the same pattern in aircraft in 1936: the more of a thing is made, the cheaper the next one, by a steady fraction per doubling.

Electricity from new solar farms followed, from about $408/MWh in 2010 to $44 in 2025, and onshore wind more slowly.10 Batteries ride a similar curve: BloombergNEF’s annual survey found an average of $108 per kilowatt-hour for a lithium-ion pack in 2025.11 These are the engines that restarted.

The curve is not a law of nature; it is what happens when an industry makes the same thing in enormous numbers, and it gets harder to ride. The next doubling means building again all the 2,400 GW or so installed by 2025.12 And modules are now a minority of a solar farm’s cost, while land, labour, grid connections and capital learn slowly. Two more doublings at the long-run 24% would take modules to about 16 cents a watt; at the recent third, to 12 cents.

5Where the panels go

Fifteen years ago solar was a rounding error in all four of this series’ countries; Germany, the only one above 1%, got about 2% of its electricity from it. By 2025 it made 18% of Germany’s electricity, 11% of China’s, 9% of India’s and 9% of the United States’, and 8.8% of the world’s.12

Germany paid for much of the early curve. From 2000 its feed-in tariffs guaranteed a fixed price for every kilowatt-hour from new panels for twenty years, when modules still cost several dollars a watt.13 Much of its solar is on roofs: small systems, close to the load, that save their owners the retail price rather than earn the wholesale one.

China now installs as fast as it manufactures. Its solar fleet grew by about 315 GW in 2025 alone, more than all the solar in the United States, and reached half the world’s total.12 The country that runs the factories is also the biggest customer, which keeps the engine turning.

India buys solar through auctions: the state offers a block of capacity, often with land and a grid connection in a large solar park, and developers bid the price at which they will sell for 25 years. In December 2020 the winning bid in one auction was 2.69¢/kWh,14 among the cheapest electricity ever sold. Many of the parks are in the Thar desert of Rajasthan, around Bhadla near Jodhpur, and in the salt flats of Kutch in Gujarat.15 India’s fleet grew by more than a third in 2025, to 135 GW.12

The United States builds mostly large, utility-scale plants, nearly all on trackers that turn the panels to follow the sun.16 It has also built a tariff wall: duties on Chinese panels since 2012, global safeguard tariffs from 2018, and duties on factories in Southeast Asia since 2025. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 added tax credits for making modules and cells at home; in 2025 Congress cut back the credits for new wind and solar farms but kept most of those for factories.17 American buyers paid about 30 cents a watt for modules in early 2025, over three times the world spot price.181

Panels are now cheap enough to spread without any scheme at all: Pakistan imported 17 GW in 2024, mostly for rooftops escaping high tariffs and outages.19

Rooftop versus field

The module is now a minor part of most systems’ cost. US Department of Energy benchmarks for early 2025 put a utility plant at about $1.12 a watt and a home rooftop system at about $2.95, though both use the same modules at roughly 30 cents a watt:18

The rest is mostly “soft” cost. For the American home system with a battery, office work (permits, inspection, interconnection paperwork, sales) and the installer’s overhead and margin come to about $2.20 a watt, more than all the hardware; a utility plant spreads such costs over a hundred megawatts. These are the parts of the bill that do not ride the learning curve.

They also differ most between countries. IRENA puts the average utility-scale plant commissioned in 2024 at about 53 cents a watt in India and 59 cents in China, against $1.06 in the United States.20 Fraunhofer ISE puts German rooftops at about $1.10–2.20 a watt.21 Rooftop solar still pays for many households because it saves the retail price, with all the network charges, levies and taxes of a household bill, while a field plant earns only the wholesale price.

6Sun, site and capacity factor

A panel’s rating says what it makes under 1,000 W/m² of light with its cells at 25 °C. Real panels see that rarely: the sun is low in the morning, evening and winter, clouds and haze come and go, and at night there is nothing. The European Commission’s PVGIS models real output from years of satellite weather; the figure shows its estimates for 1 kWp, panels rated at one kilowatt, in 28 places.22

A fixed kilowatt near Jodhpur, in India’s solar-park country, makes about 1,670 kWh a year; the same panels in Berlin make about 1,060, with four and a half times as much in June as in December. Jodhpur’s bars are much flatter, with a dip from June to September, when the monsoon clouds arrive.

The right-hand scale gives the same yields as a capacity factor: a plant’s average output, night included, as a share of its rated power. Fixed panels near Jodhpur run at about 19%, in Berlin about 12%. A nuclear plant can run at over 90%; solar never comes close, because the sun sets.

A tracker that turns the panels east to west through the day lifts the Jodhpur yield to about 2,060 kWh, a capacity factor near 24%. It gains most in the morning and evening, which matters later in this essay. Two-axis trackers rarely repay their extra steel and motors.

Panels make direct current; the inverter turns it into alternating current locked to the grid, and increasingly helps run the grid, as essay 5 described. Because panels rarely reach their rating, developers install more panels than inverter: the American benchmark plant has 1.34 watts of panels per watt of inverter. That throws away little: for a tracker in Seville, about 0.1% of a year’s energy is clipped at the inverter, and each kilowatt of inverter and grid connection carries a third more energy.1822

Because nearly all of a solar plant’s cost is paid up front, its cost per kilowatt-hour is roughly capital divided by lifetime output: double the yield and the cost halves. Panels age slowly, losing about half a per cent of their output a year.23 This is why the same panel makes power far more cheaply in Rajasthan than in Hamburg, and why the cost of borrowing the capital, which essay 8 takes up, matters so much for solar.

7The noon problem

Every panel in a country sees roughly the same sun. Essay 5 showed what follows in a market cleared by the merit order: the more panels there are, the lower the price in exactly the hours they produce, and the lower solar’s capture rate. Cheap energy, arriving all at once, is worth less.

Germany, with the highest solar share of the four countries and open hourly data, shows the effect most clearly:

Through the 2010s solar earned close to the average price. Then the midday dip deepened: in 2025 solar earned about $52/MWh against an average of $101, a capture rate of 52%, and prices were negative in 573 hours, most of them sunny middays. The 2022 spike shows that the capture rate is set by the market, not the panels: when gas set the price in almost every hour, solar earned 94% of the average. Hour by hour, 2015’s nearly flat summer day has become 2025’s noon canyon.

This is the limit of the restarted engine. The learning curve makes each kilowatt-hour cheaper to produce; the noon problem makes each extra kilowatt-hour of solar worth less. Where demand peaks after sunset, as India’s does, noon power does nothing for the hardest hour of the day.

Panels can do a little themselves: trackers and east–west layouts move output out of noon and into the shoulder hours. The rest depends on the system around them: batteries that carry noon energy into the evening, demand that moves to noon, lines that export the surplus. Since February 2025 new German systems get no payment in negative-price hours, which nudges them to switch off or store.25

So the engine has restarted, for energy: a product made by the million that gets about a quarter cheaper with each doubling, and already the cheapest new electricity in much of the world. What it cannot do alone is make that energy arrive when it is needed. Essay 7 adds wind, the other engine, and asks what the whole fleet is worth once it is built.
