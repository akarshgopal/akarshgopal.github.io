Wind and the Fleet: Building It, Running It, and What It Is Worth

Essay 7 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

In 2025 the world’s wind turbines made some 2,700 terawatt-hours, about one unit of electricity in twelve. Half of the world’s wind capacity now stands in China, which added more in that one year than the whole of Germany’s fleet.1

Essay 6 showed the engine of the twentieth century running again: solar panels getting cheaper with every doubling of the fleet, built in Chinese factories and sold in Indian auctions. Wind is the other half of that restart. If both keep falling in cost, why has electricity not started getting cheaper again?

This essay gives two reasons. Building the fleet runs into things the learning curve does not touch: the queue to connect, the supply chain and the cost of borrowing. And running it shows that a megawatt-hour from wind or sun is worth less the more of them there are. First, the machine itself.

1Power in the wind

Unlike the steam plants of essay 3, a wind turbine needs no heat, so no Carnot limit applies. It takes kinetic energy directly from moving air. The power carried by wind through a rotor of area A is ½ ρ A v³, where ρ is the density of air and v its speed. That cube is the most important fact about wind power: double the wind speed and the power rises eightfold.

A turbine cannot take all of it. If it stopped the air completely, the air would pile up and nothing more could flow through. Slow it too little and it extracts little; too much and the flow goes around the rotor. In 1920 Albert Betz found the optimum: slow the wind by a third and capture 16/27, about 59 per cent, of the power in the stream. Modern three-bladed turbines reach 45–50 per cent at their best.

The formula rewards size twice. Swept area grows with the square of blade length, so doubling the rotor quadruples the energy. And wind speeds up with height, away from the drag of the ground; because of the cube, a few per cent more speed matters. So turbines have kept growing, the same economy of scale that drove the steam plants of essay 3:

By 2024 the average new American onshore turbine stood on a 106 m tower, had a rotor 137 m across and was rated at 3.5 MW, against 48 m and 0.72 MW in 1998–99.2 At sea, with no roads to carry blades and no neighbours to object, the largest machines have rotors of 220–236 m and ratings of 13–15 MW.

Unlike the steam plants, turbines did not grow into one giant unit: a wind farm is many of them, and they steal wind from each other. A rotor leaves behind a wake, a tube of slower, more turbulent air that recovers only over many rotor diameters, and a turbine standing in it loses much more power than speed. The classic engineering model, by Niels Otto Jensen at Risø in 1983, treats the wake as a cone widening at a fixed rate.4 It is crude, but gets the essentials right:

With the wind straight down a row, the second turbine makes far less than the first, and those behind settle lower still. At Horns Rev, an early Danish offshore farm, the second turbine in such a row made only about 60 per cent as much as the first;6 the model gives 59 per cent.5 That is the worst case. Turn the wind a few degrees and the wakes slide between the turbines, hence the sharp dips in the lower curve; averaged over every direction, the array loses about 12 per cent of its output.

Spacing turbines further apart costs cable and land or seabed, so every farm trades one loss against the other. Smooth sea air keeps wakes longer than rough air over land, and large offshore farms lose of the order of 10–20 per cent of their output to them.6

2Capacity factor is a design choice

Essay 6 introduced the capacity factor, a plant’s average output as a share of its rating. For a wind turbine it is often quoted as a property of the site. It is largely the designer’s choice, made through one ratio: the size of the generator against the area its rotor sweeps, in watts per square metre. This is the specific power. That of new American turbines fell from 393 W/m² in 1998–99 to 237 W/m² in 2023, because rotors grew much faster than generators.2

Keep the rating fixed and enlarge the rotor: the turbine reaches full power at lower winds, the sorted hours fill out and the capacity factor climbs. Shrink the generator under the same rotor and it rises too, but by throwing away the strongest winds. Low specific power gives steadier output, and that is worth more on a grid that already has plenty of power in storms.

Site still matters. Dividing each country’s wind output by its fleet, US wind ran at about 34 per cent in 2025, helped by the windy Great Plains and Texas; the fleets of China, Germany and India ran at 21–23 per cent.1 US projects built in 2023 averaged 37.5 per cent in 2024.2 What designers optimise, though, is value rather than energy, which is why much of what follows is about money.

3The queue to connect

A wind or solar farm starts life as a land option, a measurement mast and a spreadsheet. The developer secures land, measures the resource, applies for permits and asks the grid operator to connect; only with all that, and a buyer, can it be financed. Construction is often the shortest phase.

The request to connect has become the bottleneck, and the United States shows it most clearly. The grid operator must study the plant’s effect on every nearby line and transformer, and decide which upgrades it needs and who pays. Those studies happen in a public queue, which Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory tallies each year:

At the end of 2024, 2,290 gigawatts of projects were waiting, against about 1,320 gigawatts of power plants in operation. Of requests made in 2000–2019, only 13 per cent were operating by 2024, and the median wait has stretched from under two years to over four.8 Entering was cheap, so a developer with ten possible sites filed all ten and let the studies sort them, slowing everyone else. Since 2023, reforms study projects in batches and demand bigger deposits, and in 2024 the queue shrank for the first time.

Behind the paperwork, the constraint is the upgrades: transformers in short supply and lines that take a decade. Texas shows what building the lines first does. The state planned about $7 billion of new lines into its windy west, finished in the early 2010s, before most of the farms that would use them.9 In 2025 wind supplied about 24 per cent of the Texas grid’s demand, and in its windiest hours two-thirds.10

India and China organise it differently. In India the state runs auctions: it decides how much capacity it wants, often supplies the land and the grid connection, and developers bid the price at which they will sell for 25 years, which is how solar reached the record prices of essay 6. India’s wind fleet sits mostly in two windy states, Gujarat and Tamil Nadu.11 China plans its wind and solar in large bases in the north and west, and builds the ultra-high-voltage lines to carry their output east. Either way, the state takes the risks of land, permits and grid, and in exchange buys some of the cheapest electricity ever sold.

4Supply chains and the cost of capital

Offshore wind has some of the best wind resources in the world, strong, steady and close to coastal cities, and the hardest engineering. Turbines stand on steel piles driven into the seabed, and the power reaches shore through export cables and an offshore substation. Only a handful of vessels in the world can lift the largest turbines. The clearest public record of its cost comes from outside the four countries, in Britain, whose auctions guarantee developers the price they bid for fifteen years:

In real terms, winning bids fell by about two-thirds between the first round in 2015 and 2022, a learning curve like solar’s. Then the curve turned. Interest rates rose, steel and copper jumped, and turbine makers who had sold at a loss raised prices. In the fifth round in 2023 the price cap was below what developers could build for, and not one offshore project bid. Later rounds cleared higher; the latest, in January 2026, came in about three-quarters above the 2022 low in real terms.12

The United States felt it too: Ørsted cancelled its 2.2 GW Ocean Wind projects off New Jersey in October 2023, recognising impairments of 28.4 billion Danish kroner.13

Two lessons reach beyond offshore wind. The first is the cost of capital, the return lenders and owners demand on the money tied up in a plant. A wind farm has no fuel bill; nearly all of its cost is paid before it makes a single megawatt-hour, so its price is mostly repayment of that money. Raise interest rates from 2 to 5 per cent and the cost of each megawatt-hour rises by roughly 30 per cent. Essay 8 works through that arithmetic.

The second is the supply chain. Western turbine makers, a few firms on thin margins, could not absorb the price rises of 2022–23. Chinese makers, selling mostly into the largest market in the world, now lead the global rankings: in 2024 the four largest turbine makers by installations were Chinese.14 As with solar modules, the learning curve runs fastest where the most is built.

5Who carries the risk

A wind farm’s costs are known the day it is built; its income is not. It depends on how windy the year is, on the price in each hour, and on how far that price falls when the wind blows everywhere at once: volume, price and shape. Lenders charge less for predictable income, so whoever takes those risks also sets the cost of capital, and so the cost of the electricity.

Germany has eleven years of open hourly prices and output, and a history of trying most kinds of contract, so it makes the clearest case. The figure runs one German onshore wind farm through 2015–2025, in which wind’s market value swung from about $28/MWh to $190,15 and asks what it would have received under each kind of contract:

A merchant plant sells into the market and takes what comes, ruinous years and windfall years. A feed-in tariff, which built Germany’s first fleet, pays a fixed price per megawatt-hour and passes the price risk to consumers. Germany now uses a sliding premium: the plant sells in the market and gets a top-up whenever wind’s market value falls below its auction strike price.

In 2022, under a sliding premium, the farm kept the whole windfall. Let the top-up also work in reverse, so that when the market pays more than the strike price the farm hands the excess back to consumers, and it becomes a two-sided contract for difference (CfD), as in Britain. The EU’s 2024 reform makes it the default for new state-supported plants from 2027.18 India’s auctions, with a fixed price for 25 years, put the price and shape risk on the buyer in the same way.

Increasingly the buyer is a company: in a power purchase agreement (PPA), a data centre or steelmaker buys a farm’s output at a fixed price for ten to fifteen years. A pay-as-produced PPA leaves the buyer with the shape risk; a baseload PPA promises a flat block, so the generator must buy the shortfall in the market when the wind is weak.

6Capture rates and curtailment

The shape risk is the one that grows with the fleet. Essay 5 defined the capture rate, the price a source earns divided by the average price of all hours, and essay 6 followed German solar’s down from 93 per cent in 2019 to 52 per cent in 2025. Wind faces the same arithmetic, more slowly, because it blows at more varied hours. Onshore wind’s share of German demand rose from 14 to 23 per cent between 2015 and 2025, while its capture rate stayed mostly between 80 and 87 per cent; offshore wind, which blows more at night and in winter, held at 96 per cent in 2025.15 Solar is the warning. At double today’s solar, with nothing else changed, solar earns about an eighth of the average price, with prices below zero for about 1,900 hours.20

Nothing else changing is the catch: batteries, exports and flexible demand grow where power is cheap, and 40 GW of two-hour batteries would lift that eighth to about 30 per cent. The same slide is under way wherever solar grows fast; in California, negative prices fall almost only in sunny late-spring hours.21

A low price is the market’s signal. The physical version is curtailment: farms told to turn down while the wind blows, because nobody will take the power or the lines cannot carry it. In Germany most curtailment has the second cause, the one essay 4 traced: wind in the north, demand in the south and west, full lines between.

Offshore wind, landing on the north coast, is curtailed most. Congestion management cost Germany about $3.5 billion in 2025, a cost that lands on network charges and so on the bill.22 The other countries have their versions. In the United States the Southwest Power Pool, the windiest market, curtailed 10.5 per cent of its wind in 2024.2 China curtailed about 17 per cent of its wind in 2016, a third or more in parts of the north-west, before new long-distance lines and dispatch rules brought the national figure down to a few per cent.23 A turbine that is cheap to build but turned off when it could run is less cheap than it looks.

7Hybrid plants and batteries

If some hours are worth little and others a lot, store energy from the first for the second. Batteries are now cheap enough, and the best place for them is often a solar or wind farm itself. A hybrid plant shares one grid connection, the scarce asset of section 3, between generators and batteries. India now auctions such plants directly: wind–solar hybrids, whose output is steadier than either alone, and contracts that require power at set hours, which only storage can promise.11

The figure opens with a 0.4 MW, four-hour battery behind a connection of 0.75 MW per MW of panels, the US benchmark ratio from essay 6. Little is lost to the connection; the bigger cost is the low noon price. The battery moves a slice of noon into the evening peak and lifts the plant’s average price from about $82 to $101/MWh, and its revenue by about a sixth, though its losses mean fewer megawatt-hours sold.

Each extra hour of storage adds less, because most days have only a few dear hours to fill. Shrink the connection and the battery recovers output that would otherwise be lost, which is why developers now design generation, storage and connection together: in the US queue, nearly half of all solar is proposed as part of a hybrid plant.8 The figure leaves out the battery’s own cost, which decides whether the extra revenue is worth it. Batteries move energy across hours, not seasons.

8Seasons in four countries

One farm can be made more valuable with a bigger rotor, a battery and a better contract. A whole system must match output that follows the weather to demand that follows people, every month of the year, and the four countries face that in different ways:

In Germany the two fit: in 2025 wind supplied about a third of demand from September to January and solar 31 per cent in June, and demand peaks in winter, when the wind blows most. In India they fit too, differently: the monsoon brings the strongest winds in June and July, just when clouds dim the panels. In the United States and China wind is weakest in late summer, exactly when air conditioning drives demand to its peak, and solar has to carry the season.

Months hide the hours. In Germany’s stillest week of 2025, in mid-January, wind and solar together averaged under 9 GW, while in the windiest, sunniest hour of the year they reached 64 GW.15 Batteries move energy across hours; transmission moves it across places; flexible demand, such as heat pumps, electric cars and electrolysers, can soak up surplus. None removes the need, in a dark, still week, for firm capacity: plants that can run whenever asked. Essay 9 returns to that week.

So the engine has restarted, for energy. Wind and solar make megawatt-hours more cheaply than ever, but each one is worth less as the fleet grows, and building them runs into queues, supply chains and the cost of capital. Essay 8 adds up what a kilowatt-hour costs: the plants, the capital, and everything else on the bill.
