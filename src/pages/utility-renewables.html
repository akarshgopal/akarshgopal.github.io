Utility-Scale Renewables: Building and Running the Fleet

Essay 6 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

In 1998–99 the average new wind turbine in the United States had a rotor 48 metres across. By 2024 it was 137 metres, and the largest machines at sea now sweep circles 236 metres wide.12

This essay is about building such machines, and the solar farms beside them, by the hundred, and making them earn a living: the rotor and its wakes, the queue to connect, the auctions and contracts that share the risk, the falling prices a growing fleet earns, and the batteries, wires and flexible demand that help it fit into a system. The engineering is only half of it. A modern wind or solar farm is also a grid connection and a contract, and each has become, in turn, the thing that decides whether it gets built.

1Wind physics at scale

Unlike the steam plants of essay 1, a wind turbine needs no heat. It takes kinetic energy directly from moving air. The power carried by wind through a rotor of area A is ½ ρ A v³, where ρ is the density of air and v its speed: proportional to the area the rotor sweeps and to the cube of wind speed. That cube is the most important fact about wind power: double the wind speed and the available power rises eightfold.

Why can’t a turbine take all of it? If it stopped the air completely, the air would pile up and nothing more could flow through. Slow it too little and you extract little; too much and the flow goes around the rotor. In 1920 Albert Betz found the optimum: slow the wind by a third and capture 16/27, about 59%, of the power in the stream. No rotor can do better; modern three-bladed turbines reach 45–50% at their best.

The formula rewards size twice. Swept area grows with the square of blade length, so doubling the rotor quadruples the energy. And wind speeds up with height, away from the drag of the ground; because of the cube, a few per cent more speed matters. So turbines have kept growing:

By 2024 the average new American onshore turbine stood on a 106 m tower and was rated at 3.5 MW, against 0.72 MW in 1998–99.1 At sea, with no roads to carry blades and no neighbours to object, the largest machines have rotors of 220–236 m, each blade about the length of a football pitch, and ratings of 13–15 MW.

A blade turns because each slice of it is a small wing. The slice meets the wind coming at the rotor, but it is also moving sideways through the air, and near the tip far faster than the wind itself. What it feels is the sum of the two, an apparent wind arriving almost edge-on. Lift acts at right angles to that apparent wind, so it leans slightly into the direction of rotation, and that small lean is the torque that drives the generator:

Move the slice from root to tip and the apparent wind swings from about 30° off the rotor plane to about 5°, because the slice speeds up while the wind does not. A flat blade would meet it at the right angle at only one radius, and near the root it would stall. So blades are twisted, steep at the root and nearly flat at the tip. Raise the wind with the rotor speed fixed and the angle of attack grows everywhere; real turbines then pitch the whole blade, or let the outer sections stall, to cap the power.

On a wind farm the largest losses come from turbines stealing wind from each other. A rotor leaves behind a wake, a tube of slower, more turbulent air that recovers only over many rotor diameters, and a turbine standing in it loses much more power than speed. The classic engineering model, by Niels Otto Jensen at Risø in 1983, treats the wake as a cone widening at a fixed rate.3 It is crude, but gets the essentials right:

With the wind straight down a row, the second turbine makes far less than the first, and those behind settle lower still, each in the merged wakes upwind. At Horns Rev, an early Danish offshore farm, the second turbine in such a row made only about 60% as much as the first;5 the model gives 59%.4 That is the worst case. Turn the wind a few degrees and the wakes slide between the turbines and the losses largely vanish, hence the sharp dips in the figure’s lower curve; averaged over every direction, the array loses about 12% of its output.

Over land, rougher air mixes wakes in faster. Smooth sea air keeps them longer, so offshore turbines are spaced further apart, and in a crowded North Sea one farm’s wake can cut the output of a neighbour tens of kilometres downwind. Spacing costs cable and seabed; large offshore farms lose of the order of 10–20% of their output to wakes.5

2Capacity factor is a design choice

A turbine’s capacity factor, its average output as a share of its rating, is often quoted as a property of the site. It is largely the designer’s choice, made through one ratio: the size of the generator against the area its rotor sweeps, in watts per square metre. This is the specific power. That of new American turbines fell from 393 W/m² in 1998–99 to 237 W/m² in 2023, because rotors grew much faster than generators.1

Keep the rating fixed and enlarge the rotor: the turbine reaches full power at lower winds, the duration curve fills out and the capacity factor climbs. Shrink the generator under the same rotor and it rises too, but by throwing away the strongest winds. Low specific power gives steadier output, worth more on a grid that already has plenty in storms, and suits weaker-wind sites closer to cities.

The fleet shows it: US wind projects built in 2023 averaged a 37.5% capacity factor in 2024, against 34% for the fleet built since 1998.1 Solar developers make the same choice with the inverter loading ratio, accepting some clipping at noon for more output in the shoulders. What is being optimised is value, not energy, which is why much of what follows is about money.

3Project anatomy and the queue

At the end of 2024 the United States had about 1,320 gigawatts of power plants in operation, and 2,290 gigawatts of new projects, mostly solar farms, batteries and wind farms, waiting in line to plug into the grid. Most will never be built.7

A wind or solar farm starts life as a land option, a measurement mast and a spreadsheet. The developer secures land, measures the resource, applies for permits and asks the grid operator to connect; only with all that, and a buyer, can it be financed. Construction is often the shortest phase: a year for a large solar farm.

The request to connect has become the bottleneck. The grid operator must study the plant’s effect on every nearby line and transformer, and decide which upgrades it needs and who pays. In the United States those studies happen in a public queue, which Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory tallies each year:

Of requests made in 2000–2019, only 13% were operating by 2024, and the median wait has stretched from under two years to over four.7

Entering was cheap, so a developer with ten possible sites filed all ten and let the studies sort them, slowing everyone else. Since 2023 US reforms study projects in batches and demand bigger deposits; in 2024 the queue shrank for the first time.7 Behind the paperwork, the constraint is the upgrades: transformers in short supply and lines that take a decade.

Most of the world runs auctions instead: the state decides how much capacity it wants and where, often supplies land and grid connection, and developers bid the price at which they will sell for 20–25 years. South Africa’s renewables auction programme cut its solar tariff by 91% in real terms between 2011 and 2021.8 Indian solar bids reached 2.7¢/kWh in 2020,9 and Saudi Arabia’s Shuaibah 1 set the record at 1.04¢.10 Auctions put the risks of land, permits and grid on the state, and in exchange deliver some of the cheapest electricity ever sold.

4Offshore, supply chains and the cost reversal

Offshore wind has some of the best wind resources in the world, strong, steady and close to coastal cities, and the hardest engineering. Turbines stand on steel monopiles driven into the seabed, or on lattice jackets in deeper water, and the power reaches shore through export cables and an offshore substation, or for distant farms through high-voltage direct-current links. Only a handful of vessels in the world can lift the largest turbines.

For a decade its cost fell faster than almost anyone forecast. The clearest record is Britain’s auctions, in which developers bid the price they will be guaranteed for fifteen years:

In real terms, winning bids fell by about two-thirds between the first round in 2015 and 2022. Then the curve turned. Interest rates rose, steel and copper jumped, and turbine makers who had sold at a loss raised prices. In the fifth round in 2023 the price cap was below what developers could build for, and not one offshore project bid.

Ørsted cancelled its 2.2 GW Ocean Wind projects off New Jersey in October 2023, and in May 2025 dropped the 2.4 GW Hornsea 4, eight months after it won a contract.12 Later UK rounds cleared higher; the latest, in January 2026, came in about three-quarters above the 2022 low in real terms.11

The lesson reaches beyond offshore wind. Wind and solar have almost no fuel cost, so they are acutely sensitive to the cost of capital (interest rates rising from 2% to 5% raise the cost of a megawatt-hour by roughly 30%) and to the price of steel, copper and magnets. Solar largely escaped, because Chinese module overcapacity kept prices falling. Wind turbines, made by a few Western firms with thin margins, did not. Every technology has its own bottleneck: Chinese wafers and magnets for solar; vessels and cable factories for offshore wind; and large transformers for everything.

5Who carries the risk

A wind farm’s costs are known the day it is built; its income is not. It depends on how windy the year is, on the price in each hour, and on how far that price falls when the wind blows everywhere at once: volume, price and shape. Lenders charge less for predictable income, so whoever takes those risks also sets the cost of the electricity.

The figure runs one German onshore wind farm through eleven real years, in which wind’s market value swung from about $28/MWh to $190,13 and asks what it would have received under each kind of contract:

A merchant plant sells into the market and takes what comes, ruinous years and windfall years. A feed-in tariff, which built Germany’s and Spain’s first fleets, pays a fixed price per megawatt-hour and passes the price risk to consumers. Germany now uses a sliding premium: the plant sells in the market and gets a top-up whenever wind’s market value falls below its auction strike price.

Look at 2022. Under a sliding premium the farm kept the whole windfall. Now let the top-up also work in reverse: when the market pays more than the strike price, the farm hands the excess back to consumers. That is a two-sided contract for difference (CfD), as in Britain; pick it in the figure and the payback shows in red. The EU’s 2024 reform makes it the default for new state-supported plants from 2027.16

Increasingly the buyer is a company: in a power purchase agreement (PPA), a data centre or steelmaker buys a farm’s output at a fixed price for ten to fifteen years. A pay-as-produced PPA leaves the buyer with the shape risk; a baseload PPA promises a flat block, so the generator must buy the shortfall in the market when the wind is weak, and carries the price risk on it.

6Capture rates and curtailment

Essay 5 traced solar’s falling capture rate; wind faces the same arithmetic, more slowly. The dots below are Germany’s real years; the dashed curve re-prices 2025’s hours as if the country had had more or less solar.

In 2025 German solar earned 52% of the average price, down from 93% in 2019; onshore wind held at 87%, and offshore wind, which blows more at night and in winter, at 96%.13

The re-priced 2025 tracks solar’s real slide closely.18 At double today’s solar, with nothing else changed, solar earns about an eighth of the average price, with prices below zero for about 1,900 hours. Nothing else changing is the catch: batteries, exports and flexible demand grow where midday power is cheap, and 40 GW of two-hour batteries would lift that eighth to about 30%. The same slide is under way wherever solar grows fast. In California negative prices fall almost only in sunny late-spring hours; in South Australia the price was zero or below in 30% of the hours of 2025.19

A low price is the market’s signal. The physical version is curtailment: farms told to turn down while the wind blows. Some happens because nobody will take more power, so prices go negative and plants stop. In Germany most has another cause, the one essay 3 traced: wind in the north, demand in the south and west, full lines between.

Offshore wind, landing on the north coast, is curtailed most; solar curtailment almost doubled in 2025, increasingly because local distribution grids are full. Congestion management cost about $3.5 billion in 2025.20 In the United States, the Southwest Power Pool, the windiest market, curtailed 10.5% of its wind in 2024.1

7Hybrid plants and batteries

If noon is worth little and the evening a lot, store some noon output. Batteries are now cheap enough, and the best place for them is often the solar farm itself. A hybrid plant shares one grid connection, the scarce asset of section 3, between panels and batteries: it charges from output the connection would otherwise clip and discharges in the evening when the panels are idle.

The figure builds one near Seville from a real year of sunshine and prices.

The figure opens with a 0.4 MW, four-hour battery behind a connection of 0.75 MW per MW of panels, the US benchmark ratio from essay 5. Little is lost to the connection; the bigger cost is the low noon price. The battery moves a slice of noon into the evening peak and lifts the plant’s average price from about $82 to $101/MWh, slightly above the year’s average, and revenue by about a sixth, though its losses mean fewer megawatt-hours sold.

Each extra hour of storage adds less, because most days have only a few dear hours to fill. Shrink the connection and the battery recovers output that would otherwise be lost, which is why developers now design panels, storage and connection together: in the US queue, nearly half of all solar is proposed as part of a hybrid plant.7

The figure leaves out the battery’s own cost, which decides whether the extra revenue is worth it, and the other markets a battery can serve, frequency reserves above all. But the result is general: storage lifts solar’s value most where there is a lot of solar, and the first hours are worth the most.

8Fitting it into a system

One farm can be made more valuable with a bigger rotor, a battery and a better contract. A system must match output that follows the weather to demand that follows people, every hour of the year. Wind and solar help each other: in Germany solar peaks in summer and wind in winter.

In 2025 German wind made twice as much in January as in July, solar more than five times as much in July as in January, and the sum is flatter than either. Hour by hour it is not: in the stillest week of the year, in mid-January, wind and solar together averaged under 9 GW, while over the year their output peaked at 63 GW.13

Everything in this series is a partial answer to that variability. Bigger, low-specific-power turbines and offshore wind fill more hours. Batteries move energy across hours; transmission and interconnectors move it across places. Flexible demand (heat pumps, electric cars, electrolysers) can soak up surplus. Contracts decide who is paid for each, and grid codes whether inverters hold the system up. None removes the need, in the dark, still week, for firm capacity: plants that can run whenever asked.

The physics of wind and sunlight is settled. What decides how fast the fleet grows is the rest: the queue, the cost of capital, how contracts share risk and how quickly the wires follow. The next essay adds it all up as the price of electricity: what is in a bill, what a plant costs and why some keep getting cheaper.
