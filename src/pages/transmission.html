Wires: The Network That Decides What Gets Built

Essay 4 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

The last essay ended in a power station, with a generator turning out tens of thousands of volts. The kettle it will boil may be hundreds of kilometres away and wants 230 (or, in North America, 120). In between is the network: lines, transformers and substations. For most of the century of falling prices it was the quiet part of the bill. It is not quiet now.

The engine of essay 3, bigger and more efficient plants, made each kilowatt-hour cheaper to generate. It did little for the cost of moving one. Between 2003 and 2023, what major US utilities spent on transmission nearly tripled in real terms, while the electricity Americans used grew by about a tenth.12 In Germany, network charges are about a quarter of a household bill.3

The network has also become the slowest part of the system to build, and the countries differ most here. China has strung a second, ultra-high-voltage grid across the country in fifteen years; India has tripled the capacity between its regions since 2014. The United States, which built about 4,000 miles of high-voltage line in 2013, now manages about 500 a year, and Germany spent about $3.5 billion in 2025 working around lines it has not yet built.

This essay builds the network up from the physics of essay 2: why lines run at high voltage, the transformer that makes that possible, what limits how much a line can carry, what happens when it is full, why the longest lines carry direct current, and why new ones take a decade in some countries and a few years in others.

1Why high voltage

Essay 2 followed charge through a wire and found that the collisions which slow it down heat the metal: a wire with resistance R carrying a current I turns I²R watts into heat. The power the wire delivers is the voltage times the current, P = V × I. So the same power can travel as a large current at low voltage or a small current at high voltage, and because the loss grows with the square of the current, the second wins by a wide margin. Ten times the voltage means a tenth of the current and a hundredth of the loss.

At household voltage a few hundred megawatts would melt the wires. At 380 kV, the backbone voltage of much of Europe, the figure’s gigawatt crosses 300 km on one circuit losing about 6%; spread over two circuits, as German grid operators usually build such lines, about 3%.4

So the largest lines climbed from tens of kilovolts a century ago to 380–400 kV in Europe, 500 and 765 kV in North America and India, and 1,000 kV in China. Each step brought a new problem. At very high voltage the electric field at the surface of a thin conductor tears electrons from the air around it. This corona makes the line crackle and glow faintly at night, and it wastes power, most in rain and fog. The cure is to split each conductor into a bundle of two, four or six wires held apart by spacers, which the field treats as one fat wire.

American Electric Power, which runs the largest 765 kV network in the US, publishes what this buys. Carrying 1,000 MW over 100 miles, its six-wire 765 kV lines lose 0.6% to resistance and corona together; a typical 345 kV line loses 4.2%. Over long distances one 765 kV line on a strip of land about 61 m wide carries as much as six 345 kV lines, each needing about 46 m.5 Higher voltage costs more per kilometre of line and much less per megawatt carried.

But it creates a problem of its own. Generators work at around 20 kV, and nobody wants 380 kV in a kitchen. Something has to change the voltage on the way without wasting much of the power.

2The transformer

In 1831, the year he found the effect behind the generator, Faraday also wound two coils of wire around an iron ring. Switching a current on or off in one coil gave a brief kick of current in the other; a steady current gave nothing. No magnet had to move. The magnetic field through the second coil only had to change.

Make the current in the first coil alternate, as a generator’s does, and the field never stops changing. Iron is thousands of times more permeable than air, so it gathers almost all of the field into a closed loop, the magnetic flux, and threads it through the second coil. Every turn of wire around the core sees the same changing flux and gets the same voltage, so each coil’s voltage is proportional to its number of turns.

The flux does not depend on the load. When the load draws current, the primary draws a matching current and their magnetic effects cancel; with nothing connected, the primary still draws a small magnetising current to keep the flux going. Voltage goes up exactly as current goes down, so power in equals power out, less a small loss.

That is the whole machine: two coils on a shared iron core. Wire a generator to one with eighteen times the turns on the far side and it gives eighteen times the voltage at an eighteenth of the current.

A generator’s 21 kV is stepped up to 380 kV for the journey, then down in stages to 230 V at the socket, so almost every unit of electricity passes through four or five transformers on its way. The largest waste less than a quarter of one per cent of the power through them and routinely last forty years.78

Switch the supply to direct current and the transformer stops working: the flux rises once and stays, and nothing is induced. That is why Edison’s DC networks could not use transformers, and why alternating current won once engineers at the Ganz works in Budapest built practical closed-core transformers in 1885. It is also why a direct-current line, later in this essay, needs a costly converter station at each end.

The same physics makes transformers big. The voltage per turn is set by how fast the flux changes, which is the frequency times the flux the core carries. Copper is costly and resistive, so designers want few turns, and they push special grain-oriented steel close to the most flux it can carry before it saturates. At 50 or 60 cycles a second, that leaves one way to reach high voltage: a thick core. A large power transformer weighs 135–365 tonnes, is designed for its site, travels on a special rail car and is still largely built by hand.8 That will matter in section 6.

The transformers deep dive opens the box further: where the losses go, the paper insulation whose ageing sets a transformer’s life, and the tap changers that hold the voltage at your socket.

3What limits a line

A line looks like a pipe of fixed size, but how much it can carry depends on its length and on the weather. Three things set the limit: heat, which binds first on short lines; the voltage drop on medium ones; and, on long ones, keeping the two ends in step.

Heat and sag

An overhead conductor is a heater: the current and the sun warm it, wind and radiation cool it. The aluminium of a standard steel-reinforced conductor is usually kept below about 80 °C, partly to protect its strength but mainly because metal expands. A 400 m span a few tens of centimetres longer hangs metres lower, and a line that sags too close to a tree can flash over. In 2003 an overloaded line sagging into trees in Ohio started the cascade that blacked out 50 million people in North America.9

So a line’s rating is the current that keeps it below its maximum temperature. Operators traditionally set a static rating from pessimistic weather: a hot day, full sun, barely any wind. Most hours are kinder.

Wind matters far more than air temperature: a breeze of a few metres per second across the conductor can add half again to its capacity. And wind is what wind farms need, so a line carrying their output is often best cooled when it is most loaded. Dynamic line rating uses sensors or weather data to set the rating hour by hour; in trials collected by the US Department of Energy in 2019, Oncor in Texas raised its ratings by 30–70% on average.12 US regulators made a simpler step, ratings that follow the air temperature, mandatory in 2021; German operators have used weather-based ratings on parts of their network for years.913

Reactance and the power angle

A line is not only a resistor. Its current makes a magnetic field around it, as the current in a transformer’s coil does, and in an AC system that field builds and collapses a hundred times a second, inducing a voltage that opposes the change. The line is a long, thin inductor, and its opposition to alternating current is its reactance, X: about a quarter of an ohm per kilometre on a 380 kV line, ten times its resistance. Reactance wastes no energy, since what the field takes in one quarter-cycle it gives back in the next. But it decides how much power the line can carry at all.

Because of it, the voltage at the sending end of a loaded line runs slightly ahead of the voltage at the receiving end, by an angle δ. Ignoring resistance, the power carried is

P = V1 V2 sin δ / X

Power flows from the leading end to the lagging one, and more power needs a larger angle: the AC grid’s version of water running downhill, with the angle as the drop in height and reactance as the narrowness of the pipe. And sin δ can never exceed 1.

At 90° the line carries the most it ever can. Push further and the power falls; the generators at the sending end speed up, the angle grows, and they fall out of step with the rest of the grid. Real systems keep a wide margin, often no more than 30–45° across a path, so they can survive losing a line. And the ceiling falls with length: twice the length, twice the reactance, half the ceiling.

Length brings the third limit. A line is also a long capacitor, conductors and ground separated by air, so its far-end voltage drifts up when it is lightly loaded and sags when it is heavily loaded. In 1953 H. P. St. Clair summed up decades of experience in a curve planners still use: a short line carries what its heat allows, a long one a fraction of that. On American Electric Power’s figures a single circuit 480 km long can carry about 400 MW at 345 kV, 900 MW at 500 kV and 2,200–2,400 MW at 765 kV.5

That is the physics behind China’s 1,000 kV lines and India’s 765 kV backbone: what a line can carry over a long distance grows with the square of its voltage. Past a few hundred kilometres, it often pays to give up on alternating current altogether (section 5).

More from old wires

Because new lines are slow to build, operators are squeezing the old ones. Besides dynamic ratings there is reconductoring: keep the towers, replace the wire. Modern conductors have a carbon-fibre or composite core that barely expands, so they can run far hotter without extra sag, like the carbon-core option in the sag figure. A 2024 study from UC Berkeley and GridLab found that reconductoring roughly doubles a line’s capacity within its existing corridor at less than half the cost of a new line.15 Software and power electronics that reroute flows add more: a 2021 Brattle Group study found that, together with dynamic ratings, they could more than double the wind and solar Kansas and Oklahoma could connect without new lines.16

None of this removes the angle and voltage limits of long lines, or the limits of towers and substations. But it is cheap and fast, which new lines are not.

4Congestion and redispatch

A gas operator can choose which pipe the gas goes down. A grid operator mostly cannot. In a meshed AC network every line obeys the angle rule at once, so power from a source to a sink flows along every path between them, split by the paths’ reactances like current through parallel resistors.

Central Europe is the textbook case. Germany’s wind is in the north and the North Sea; its heaviest demand is in the industrial south and west. Power sent from Hamburg to Munich does not stay in Germany: part loops west through the Netherlands, Belgium and France, part east through Poland and the Czech Republic. These loop flows use up neighbours’ lines without their say.

With 14 GW of northern surplus, two-fifths takes the long way round, and the direct German lines overload before the neighbours’ do. There are only two physical levers. A phase-shifting transformer adds an angle to one line, making that path look “uphill”; Poland installed four on its German border in 2016.18 But a phase shifter only moves flow between paths. A direct-current link, section 5 will show, carries exactly the power it is told to.

A line is congested when the flow that physics sends down it would break its thermal or stability limit, so cheap power behind it cannot reach the demand beyond. Germany’s power market treats the whole country as one zone with one price and ignores the lines inside it. Then the grid operators check the flows, and when they do not fit, which in Germany is most days, they redispatch: they pay generators north of the bottleneck, often wind farms, to produce less, and those south of it to produce more. Every gigawatt the loop-flow figure says “must be redispatched” is one of those payments.

The bill peaked in 2022, when southern gas plants burned crisis-priced fuel, and was about $3.5 billion in 2025.20 It is paid through network charges, so it lands on every bill in the country.

The same pattern, cheap power far from the cities, appears almost everywhere. In 2016 China curtailed 17% of its wind output, 43% in Gansu, before new ultra-high-voltage lines and local reforms brought the rate down.21 In India, congestion between regions constrained about 16% of the power traded on its exchanges in 2013–14.22 India’s answer was one national grid: it joined its five regional grids into a single synchronous system at the end of 2013, and the capacity between its regions has since more than tripled, to about 120 GW.23 Texas answered by building first: its CREZ programme, finished in 2014, strung about 3,600 miles of 345 kV line from its western wind zones to its cities.24

Essay 5 shows how markets price congestion, and why Germany keeps one zone and spreads the bill. The physical fix is more lines, and over long distances, lines that ignore the angle altogether.

5HVDC: where direct current wins

Direct current has no reactance and no angle. A DC line carries whatever power its converters command, up to its conductors’ thermal limit, at any length, and needs two conductors instead of three. Its problem was always the ends: a transformer cannot work on DC, so a converter station must turn AC into DC at one end and back at the other. Until the 1970s that took room-sized mercury-arc valves.

Today two kinds of converter dominate. Line-commutated converters use thyristors, switches that turn on at will but off only when their current falls to zero. They are robust, cheap per megawatt and efficient, losing about 0.6–0.8% per station, and carry the world’s largest links, but they need a strong AC grid at both ends. Voltage-source converters use transistors that switch on and off at will, building a near-perfect sine wave from hundreds of small steps. They lose a little more, about 1% per station,25 but can restart a dead grid and connect offshore wind farms that have no grid of their own. They are large-scale relatives of the inverters in wind, solar and battery plants.

The economics follow from the shape of the costs. DC has an expensive fixed part, the two converter stations, and a cheap variable part: fewer conductors, lighter towers, lower losses. AC is the reverse. Somewhere the lines cross.

Overhead, DC wins over hundreds of kilometres, which is why China and Brazil move remote hydro, coal, wind and solar to their cities on DC. Under the sea it wins after 50 to 100 km, so almost every subsea interconnector and far-offshore wind farm uses it. And because a DC link is fully controllable, short back-to-back ones join grids that are not synchronised with each other, such as the three US interconnections.

The extremes are Chinese. The Changji–Guquan line, energised in 2019, runs 3,293 km from Xinjiang to Anhui at ±1,100 kV and carries 12 GW, about a dozen large power stations’ worth.27 It is one tier of a second grid China has laid over its 500 kV network: by October 2025, 45 ultra-high-voltage projects were operating, 21 of them 1,000 kV AC lines and 24 DC, most carrying power from the west and north to the eastern coast.32 No other country has built anything like it.

Under the ground

Nobody wants a pylon in their view, and in Germany that objection changed the grid. SuedLink, two 2 GW DC circuits over about 700 km from Schleswig-Holstein to the south, was planned in 2013 as an overhead line. After years of protest the Bundestag decided in 2015 that new DC lines should go underground, and the route was replanned. The line is now due in 2028, at a cost of about $11 billion.33

Cables cost more because everything is harder. The insulation is centimetres of plastic that must shed heat into the soil, so cables are thick, laid in trenches with special backfill and jointed every kilometre or so in clean-room conditions. A 2016 study for the German government put underground routes at three to ten times the cost of overhead ones.33 And an AC cable is a long capacitor: beyond about 100 km its charging current alone fills its rating. So long cables are DC, and Germany’s choice was coherent, if expensive and slow.

6Planning, permitting and who pays

Planning starts from one rule: the grid must survive losing any single element, a line, a transformer or a generator, without overloads or dropped customers. This “N−1” rule is why lines normally run well below their thermal rating. The physics is the same everywhere; what differs is who decides that a line is needed, who approves its route, and who pays.

In the United States a line crossing three states needs three state approvals, federal permits and a deal on sharing its cost, and every party can stall it. Germany shows the other half of the problem. It cut its approval times hard: the kilometres of line approved each year rose sevenfold between 2021 and 2025. But a line still takes years to build once approved, and the kilometres put into operation each year have barely moved.34

Germany’s pipeline is now full: more than a third of its legislated kilometres are under construction, and all four big north–south DC lines, SuedLink among them, are due between 2026 and 2028.34 In Germany the Bundestag writes the network plan into law.13

The US boom of the early 2010s came from plans made years before, such as Texas’s CREZ lines. Since then the pipeline has thinned. The Department of Energy’s 2024 study implies a need for about 5,000 miles of high-capacity line a year; the US averaged about 540 in 2020–2024.35 In May 2024 the federal regulator’s Order 1920 told every region to plan at least 20 years ahead across several scenarios, and to weigh getting more from existing lines; compliance filings run into 2026, so steel in the ground is still years away.37 Hardest are lines between regions, the most valuable kind, which have no natural owner. Most are private bets: SunZia, carrying New Mexico wind to Arizona, came online in 2026, more than eighteen years after it was proposed; Grain Belt Express lost its federal loan guarantee in 2025.29

China and India settle need centrally. China builds its ultra-high-voltage lines as national projects, planned by the state and built by state-owned grid companies. India plans its inter-regional lines nationally and runs a Green Energy Corridor programme to carry wind and solar out of the states that have most of them.

However a line is decided, it is paid for the same way. Grid companies are regulated monopolies: a regulator approves their costs and a return on their investment, and they recover both through network charges on every bill over the decades the equipment lasts. A line that lets cheap remote wind or hydro replace gas saves far more than it costs; one that sits half-used does not. Either way, no learning curve has made each new line much cheaper than the last, as one has for solar panels. Essay 8 follows those charges onto household bills.

The transformer queue

The slowest part is no longer always the line. For most of the twentieth century transformers were a mature, slow business: flat demand in rich countries, leisurely replacement, factories sized to match. Then almost every driver moved at once: connections for wind and solar farms, data centres, electrification, replacing units from the 1960s and 70s and, in the US, hardening against storms. US demand for power transformers more than doubled between 2019 and 2025, and factories could not follow.38 After a decade of near-flat prices, transformers became much more expensive from 2021, far faster than prices in general.

Lead times stretched even more: a large unit that came within a year before the pandemic took three years or more by 2024.8

The bottleneck runs down to the steel. Grain-oriented steel and specially insulated copper each make up roughly a quarter of a large transformer’s cost. The US has one producer of that steel, which meets a fifth or less of domestic demand; the rest is imported, much of it inside finished transformers.8 Makers are adding factories, but a large transformer is still wound, stacked, dried and tested largely by hand.38 Most of the public data are American, but utilities everywhere buy from the same factories and the same steel mills, and the wait lengthens the queue for new wind and solar farms.

So this is the first of the costs the engine does not touch. The wires and transformers between plant and socket are governed by physics that has not changed in a century, built from steel, aluminium, land and permission, and they are taking a growing share of the bill and setting the pace at which everything else can change. Before essay 8 adds up what they cost, essay 5 asks how a network this large, with almost no storage of its own, is kept in balance from one second to the next.
