Cheaper Again? A Century of Prices and the Next Twenty Years

Essay 9 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

In 1892 an American household paid about $5 for a kilowatt-hour, in 2002 dollars; by 1970, under 10 cents. Then the price stopped falling.

This last essay asks why the cheapening ended, why the price of a kilowatt-hour reaches into the rest of the economy, and what would have to be true for it to fall again.

1A century of cheapening

The real US residential price fell about 5 per cent a year from 1892 to 1973.1 Then, in nominal terms, it doubled by 1980.2

Why did the cheapening stop? Four layers broke at the same time.

Physics. As the first essay showed, steam temperatures, and with them efficiency, hit a materials ceiling in the 1960s. For seventy years every new plant had burned less fuel per kilowatt-hour than the last. Now they didn’t.

Scale. A 500 MW unit cost far less per kilowatt than a 50 MW one, and demand growing about 7¾ per cent a year let every new unit be bigger. Past about 1,000 MW the units grew unreliable and the economies reversed, Richard Hirsh’s “technological stasis”. Then demand growth itself broke.

Fuel. Crude went from about $3 to $12 a barrel in four months of 1973–74, and utilities passed it straight to customers. Coal and gas followed oil upwards.

Institutions. Pollution rules added scrubbers and cooling towers; nuclear build times stretched from seven years to twelve just as interest rates soared. Why US nuclear costs rose so much is still contested.4

A toy shows how much each layer matters:

Switch the shocks off but stop the engine of improvement, and prices stop falling but barely rise. Run the engine with the shocks on, and prices still jump, but by 1990 they are back below their 1965 level. Only stasis plus shocks makes prices jump and stay up. The plateau did not cause the 1970s spike; it removed the productivity growth that had quietly offset every rising cost.4

Other countries broke differently: France built standard nuclear, the Nordics leaned on hydro, Britain switched to gas. But the lesson travels: electricity gets cheaper only when an engine of productivity runs faster than the costs rising around it. Wind, solar and batteries restarted that engine in the 2010s. The question is whether wires, firming, permitting and capital can keep up.

Why does any of this matter beyond the bill?

2Why the price of electricity moves everything else

Energy looks like a small part of the economy: Americans spent 5.2 per cent of GDP on it in 2024.5 But a small, unavoidable input can have a large effect when its price jumps, because nobody can stop using it quickly.

In 1981 the share was 13.3 per cent, and the energy spikes of 1974, 1980–81 and 2008 each sit next to a recession. James Hamilton found that ten of eleven post-war US recessions were preceded by an oil price rise.6 When energy costs jump, everyone spends less on everything else.

A kilowatt-hour in a motor, heat pump or electric car often replaces two to four of fuel, so electrification spreads fast: electricity was about 20 per cent of the world’s final energy in 2023.7 Increasingly, the price of electricity is the price of energy.

So industrial power prices have become national strategy. American industry paid 8.62¢/kWh in 2025; small German firms about 20¢ in 2026.89 Germany now subsidises industrial power,10 China does it through tariffs, the Gulf through cheap gas: each an admission that the price of a megawatt-hour decides where industry can exist. Essay 8 prices it in aluminium, steel and hydrogen.

Computing sits at the other end: an hour of an H100 GPU uses about ten cents of power, tiny next to its worth. But data centres used about 1.5 per cent of the world’s electricity in 2024, heading for twice that by 2030, and their binding constraint is how fast firm supply can be connected.7

Cheap electricity decides which industries are possible, and where. Can it get cheaper again?

3Futures

Nobody knows what electricity will cost in 2040, but we can play with the mechanisms that would make useful electricity cheaper.

Moving the surplus

The midday solar surplus is the cheapest energy in history and, raw, close to worthless. The direct fix is to move it in time, with batteries and with demand that shifts: cars that charge at noon, heat pumps that pre-heat. A little of both only shortens the negative hours; removing them takes a lot of flexible demand on top of the batteries.

Batteries raise the price at noon and lower it in the evening. Flatten prices enough and the arbitrage that pays for them disappears: storage, like solar, cannibalises its own value. And short batteries solve a daily problem. The harder one is weekly.

The dark week

Every grid has its bad week. Germany has the Dunkelflaute, the still, grey winter days; Texas had Winter Storm Uri in 2021; Brazil fears drought emptying its reservoirs. The figure plays the German kind.

Either side of the lull the wind carries most of the load, but in the lull the batteries empty in one evening and demand goes unserved. The ways out are storage measured in days, or firm capacity that idles most of the year. Neither appears in any LCOE. This is resource adequacy, and it is why in September 2026 the EU approved up to about $40 billion of German support for some 11–12 GW of new, mostly gas-fired capacity, and why markets like PJM pay plants simply to be available.11

Electrifying heat

In cold countries one of the biggest new demands will be heat. A heat pump is a refrigerator run backwards, moving heat from the outside air into the house: three to four units of heat per unit of electricity, its coefficient of performance (COP).

The physics always favours the heat pump: even at −15 °C, with radiators at 45 °C, it delivers more than twice the heat of the electricity it uses. What decides the bill is the ratio of the two prices. At German prices a heat pump needs a COP of about 2.8 just to break even; in Britain, where gas is cheap relative to power, about 3.8. A bill that loads levies onto each kilowatt-hour penalises the most efficient heating there is. Rate design may decide the transition.

What would have to be true

For electricity at the meter to get cheaper in real terms over the next twenty years, these would have to be true. They are conditions, not predictions:

  • The generation engine keeps running. Solar, wind and batteries resume their cost declines after the 2021–26 relapse, and capital stays affordable.
  • The surplus finds a use. Storage and flexible demand move midday energy into the evening, and legacy subsidies stop rewarding output at negative prices.
  • Firm capacity is bought cheaply. The dark week is covered by the cheapest mix of firm plants, long storage, interconnection and demand response, bought through markets rather than rescues.
  • Wires get built faster. About 2,300 GW of US projects sat in interconnection queues at the end of 2024, and the median wait to connect has stretched to over four years.12
  • Network costs grow slower than the energy they carry. New loads, from heat pumps to data centres, spread fixed costs over more kilowatt-hours if the grid is used more intensively.
  • Bills tell the truth. Electricity is taxed less relative to fossil fuels, and tariffs reflect time and place.

Each can fail. The 1970s suggest the most dangerous outcome is not one shock but a stalled engine meeting a run of bad luck.

Still, look at where we started: an electron creeping along a wire and a magnet turning in a coil. Between them and the bill stand transformers, long lines and continents of machines spinning in lockstep, held there by the reflexes of a few thousand power plants and an auction at noon. The bill is a sum of physics, engineering, markets and political choices, and all four are still moving.
