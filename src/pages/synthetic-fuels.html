Deep Dive: Synthetic Fuels

Deep dive · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

Some jobs cannot be done with a cable. An aircraft crossing the Atlantic, a container ship crossing the Pacific and a fertiliser plant all need molecules. Those molecules can be made from electricity, water and carbon dioxide. Every step of the conversion loses energy, so the cost of the fuel follows the cost of the kilowatt-hour more closely than almost any other product in this series.

This deep dive extends essay 9, which prices the electricity inside a tonne of aluminium, a kilogram of hydrogen and an hour of a data-centre chip. Hydrogen is where that arithmetic gets hard: at $60/MWh the power alone in a kilogram costs $3.30, more than three times the US target of $1 all-in.1 What follows takes that line apart: hydrogen into ammonia, methanol and jet fuel, what decides their cost, why so many projects announced in 2020–2022 were cancelled, and where these fuels are worth the losses.

1The conversion chain: electrons to molecules

Every electricity-based fuel starts the same way. An electrolyser passes direct current through water and splits it into hydrogen and oxygen. A kilogram of hydrogen holds 33.3 kWh (its lower heating value), or 39.4 kWh if the water formed when it burns is condensed. These are what the hydrogen gives back; making it takes more, about 55 kWh in a real electrolyser, as section 3 shows.2

Hydrogen is a good fuel and a hard thing to move. It is the lightest gas, a litre of it holds very little energy unless compressed to 350–700 bar or cooled to −253 °C, and it leaks through materials that hold methane. So most plans attach it to something:

  • Ammonia (NH₃): hydrogen plus nitrogen from the air, through the century-old Haber–Bosch process. It liquefies at −33 °C, is already shipped worldwide as fertiliser feedstock, and can fuel adapted ship engines.
  • Methanol (CH₃OH): hydrogen plus carbon dioxide over a copper catalyst. A liquid at room temperature, used as a chemical feedstock and increasingly as a ship fuel.
  • E-kerosene and e-diesel: carbon dioxide is reduced to carbon monoxide, which the Fischer–Tropsch process builds with hydrogen into long hydrocarbon chains. The product can go straight into today’s aircraft.
  • E-methane: hydrogen and CO₂ combined into the main component of natural gas, which can use the existing gas grid.

Each step costs energy. Follow 100 kWh from the wind farm to the job it ends up doing.

The battery car turns about 69 kWh of every 100 into motion. Hydrogen in a fuel cell gets about 22, and an e-fuel in an engine 11: the battery car needs about a sixth of the electricity per kilometre. Heating shows the gap more sharply. A heat pump delivers almost three units of heat per unit of electricity; e-methane in a boiler returns a little under half a unit. The aircraft has no electric option yet: e-kerosene ends with about 32 kWh of fuel in the tank.

The losses differ by product, because the chemistry differs. Ammonia needs no carbon at all; methanol and e-kerosene need a lot of it, and the hydrocarbon synthesis has its own losses. Hydrogen itself is the most efficient product, at about 1.65 kWh of electricity per kWh. Everything built from it inherits that loss and adds its own. E-kerosene needs about 2.8–3.1 kWh per kWh of fuel, or 27–30 kWh per litre, a little below aireg’s 30–38 kWh per litre unless the CO₂ comes from the air. Either way, the price of e-kerosene moves almost one-for-one with the price of power.

2Where the carbon comes from

A litre of e-kerosene contains the carbon of about 2.5 kg of CO₂, which has to come from somewhere. The easy sources are concentrated ones. Fermentation at an ethanol plant or brewery, and biogas upgrading, release almost pure CO₂ that needs only drying and compression, about 130–180 kWh per tonne.9

Industrial flue gas is harder: the CO₂ is 4–25 per cent of the gas, so it has to be scrubbed out with an amine solvent that is then boiled to release it. That takes 2.5–3.7 GJ of steam per tonne.9 Fossil CO₂ from a cement kiln can be used, but the fuel made from it releases fossil carbon when it burns. At best it uses that carbon twice; it does not remove it.

The hardest source, and the only one available everywhere in unlimited quantity, is the air, where CO₂ is 0.04 per cent. Direct air capture blows huge volumes of air over a solvent or a solid sorbent and then regenerates it with heat. The IEA puts the total energy at 5.5–8.8 GJ per tonne for liquid systems and 7.2–9.5 GJ for solid ones, most of it heat.10

Air capture is the cleanest source in principle: the fuel returns to the air only the CO₂ taken from it. It also adds roughly 10–25 per cent to the energy for e-kerosene, depending on the heat supply, and more to its cost, because the plants are still expensive. So nearly every working e-fuel plant uses a concentrated source. Under EU rules a renewable fuel may use CO₂ from fossil power plants only until the end of 2035, and from other fossil industry until the end of 2040; after that the carbon must be biogenic or from the air.13

3Electrolysers: equipment, running hours and cheap hours

There are three commercial designs. Alkaline electrolysers, the oldest, use a liquid electrolyte and nickel electrodes; they are the cheapest per kilowatt and dominate China’s fleet. PEM (proton-exchange membrane) electrolysers use a thin polymer membrane with platinum and iridium catalysts: compact and quick to follow a fluctuating supply, but dearer. Solid-oxide electrolysers split steam at 700–850 °C, where heat supplies part of the energy, so they need much less electricity; in return they need steam and ramp slowly.

All three need direct current at high current and low voltage. A large electrolyser is therefore also a large rectifier, fed through the same kind of transformers that serve HVDC converters and aluminium smelters.

In every one of them, a direct voltage across two electrodes pulls electrons out of water at one side and pushes them in at the other: oxygen bubbles off the anode, hydrogen off the cathode, twice the volume of it, and ions cross the membrane between them to close the circuit. Splitting water needs at least 1.23 volts per cell. Every volt above that is the price of speed:

Push more current through each square centimetre and the same stack makes more hydrogen, so each kilogram carries less equipment cost; but the voltage climbs, and so does the electricity per kilogram. Commercial cells run near 2 A/cm² and 1.9 V, about 51 kWh per kilogram in the stack.

Power electronics, pumps, cooling and gas drying bring a whole new alkaline or PEM system to about 55 kWh per kilogram. Solid-oxide systems need about 40, plus 9 kWh of steam heat.2 This deep dive uses 55 kWh per kilogram throughout; the IEA’s fleet surveys come out close to it.78 The best tested solid-oxide systems, at Idaho National Laboratory, reached 36.7 kWh per kilogram with the steam supplied separately.14

But the economics of electrolysis are set less by the last few kilowatt-hours than by two other numbers: what the equipment costs, and how many hours a year it runs. Installing an electrolyser outside China cost $2,000–2,600 per kilowatt in 2024; in China, $600–1,200. More than half is engineering, construction and contingency; the stacks that actually split water are only 15–20 per cent.15 That money is paid back from the hydrogen. Over 8,000 hours a year it adds little per kilogram; over 2,000 hours, four times as much. Electricity works the other way: the more hours a plant runs, the more of them are expensive. The explorer below combines the two.

At $56/MWh and 4,000 hours, hydrogen costs roughly $7.80 per kilogram, close to three times the $2.90 a European producer pays to make grey hydrogen from natural gas by steam reforming, carbon price included. Two levers dominate: each $10/MWh adds about 55 cents per kilogram, and halving the running hours roughly doubles the equipment share. Efficiency counts as well, because the same equipment then makes more hydrogen: a 10 per cent better electrolyser cuts the cost by about 10 per cent, the same as power $14/MWh cheaper.

4What $/MWh does to each product

Hydrogen is an intermediate. Industry and transport buy ammonia, methanol and jet fuel, each with a fossil competitor at a known price. The explorer above also builds ammonia and e-kerosene from the same hydrogen, against their fossil versions: ammonia from natural gas, and kerosene at IATA’s 2025 average of $86 a barrel.1718

At $56/MWh and 4,000 hours the gaps are large: ammonia costs about $1,530 a tonne against $740 from gas, and e-kerosene about $3.95 a litre against $0.76 for fossil jet fuel at today’s carbon price.

A carbon price would have to reach about $640 per tonne of CO₂ before electric hydrogen matched grey, $520 for ammonia and $1,350 for jet fuel. Cheaper power helps every product: each $10/MWh off takes about 27 cents off a litre of e-kerosene.

The fixed-cost lesson from the electrolyser applies all along the chain. A Fischer–Tropsch plant, an ammonia loop and a CO₂ capture unit all cost money whether or not the wind is blowing. They also prefer steady operation, which means they need hydrogen storage, or an electrolyser that keeps running when the wind is weak.

5Demand that only exists at low prices

An appealing idea is that electrolysers could run on electricity that would otherwise be wasted: the hours when wind and solar are curtailed, when the grid cannot move the power, or when solar floods the midday market and prices go negative. Germany, whose every hourly price is published, makes a clear case: in 2025 the Germany–Luxembourg day-ahead price was zero or below in 655 hours, about 7.5 per cent of the year.19

But an electrolyser that only runs in those hours spends most of the year idle, and pays for its equipment all year. The figure below lets it buy the cheapest hours of 2025 first.

At today’s European equipment cost of about $2,370/kW, an electrolyser that runs only in the free hours makes hydrogen at about $26 a kilogram. The cheapest point is about 6,500 hours and $7.36 a kilogram: the plant must run most of the year on ordinary power to pay off its equipment. Agora reached the same conclusion in 2018: power-to-X plants need to run 3,000–4,000 hours a year at full power and cannot live on surplus power alone.3

Cheaper equipment moves the best point towards fewer, cheaper hours. At $900/kW, near Chinese prices, it is about 2,300 hours and $4.63 a kilogram; at an assumed $340/kW, about 1,300 hours and $2. Only a cheap electrolyser should chase cheap hours. Then it becomes a buyer that lifts prices in exactly the hours when wind and solar farms sell most of their output.

Where it sits matters too. Germany’s cheap hours are mostly windy hours in the north, while demand is in the south and west. An electrolyser at the coast uses power that would otherwise be curtailed; one beside a city pulls more power across congested lines. That is why Germany’s planned hydrogen network follows the same north–south route as its new direct-current lines.

6What gets built, and who pays

Between 2020 and 2022 hydrogen plans multiplied faster than buyers. The IEA’s 2025 review counts only 4.2 million tonnes a year of low-emissions hydrogen operating or past a final investment decision, against about 100 Mt used today, nearly all of it fossil.22

The project that did get built shows what it takes. NEOM in Saudi Arabia pairs 2.2 GW of electrolysers with 4 GW of dedicated sun and wind, and one buyer takes all its ammonia for 30 years:23 very cheap power, many running hours, a guaranteed buyer. Europe’s cancellations show the opposite. Ørsted stopped its FlagshipONE e-methanol plant in 2024 for lack of buyers; ArcelorMittal turned down about $1.5 billion of aid for hydrogen-ready steelworks in 2025; HyScale 100 at Heide was reported abandoned in 2026.24 In each case the hydrogen, or what was made from it, cost far more than buyers would pay; none would take three times the fossil price. Even Chile’s Haru Oni pilot, making e-fuel from Patagonian wind since 2022, has scaled back its expansion targets.25

So e-fuels exist where a law requires them or a subsidy pays the gap. The EU requires synthetic fuel to be 1.2 per cent of its jet fuel in 2030, rising to 35 per cent in 2050, and counts electricity-based ship fuels double until 2033.2627 Subsidies are smaller than the gap. The European Hydrogen Bank pays a fixed premium per kilogram, and its first two auctions paid roughly 20–70 cents, against a gap of about $5 in section 3, so the winners are mostly where power is cheapest: Spain, Portugal and the Nordics.2829 The US credit is larger, up to $3 a kilogram, but only for projects started before 2028.28

India took a third route: a target of 5 million tonnes of green hydrogen a year by 2030, backed by about $2.3 billion of incentives for electrolyser factories and hydrogen output.30

7Where molecules make sense

The efficiency chains in section 1 decide most of the argument. Where a battery, cable or heat pump can do the job, it needs between a third and a sixth of the electricity, so the molecule must be cheaper per kWh by that factor to compete. That rules out e-fuels for cars, vans, most trucks and buildings, and leaves those where no electric option exists or the molecule itself is the product.

Seasonal storage is the one row where neither side wins outright: for a dark windless week in winter, hydrogen in salt caverns may be the cheapest backstop despite returning only a third of the electricity, because cavern space costs so little.

8A plant in 2035

What happens if electricity falls to $10, $20 or $40 per MWh? For electric fuels the answer depends on whether cheap power comes with cheap equipment and long running hours. Place a 2035 plant at the power price and running hours you think it can get; the lines show the carbon price at which it would match the fossil product.

With today’s European equipment the three futures give hydrogen at roughly $7.80, $4.95 and $4.75 per kilogram: very cheap but intermittent power helps little, because the equipment sits idle, and moderately cheap power for many hours does better. At Chinese equipment prices all three fall to $2.60–3.30, close to grey hydrogen with a moderate carbon price, and e-kerosene comes to about $2.15 a litre at the hybrid site: still nearly three times fossil jet fuel at today’s carbon price, but within reach of a mandate.

Read off the lines, the gap is a carbon price. With European equipment the hybrid site’s hydrogen matches grey hydrogen only at about $320 per tonne of CO₂, and its ammonia at about $230, roughly three to four times today’s price of about $85. With Chinese equipment the same site’s hydrogen needs less than today’s price, and its ammonia beats gas-based ammonia with no carbon price at all. E-kerosene with European equipment needs about $580 a tonne even on free power around the clock, which is why aviation gets a mandate rather than a price signal.

Cheaper energy only becomes cheaper molecules if the factories that use it also get cheaper and stay busy. Wind and solar became cheap through mass manufacturing and learning. Electrolysers are at the start of that path; China installed about 65 per cent of the world’s new capacity in 2024, at prices already well below Western ones.15 If they follow solar, cheap electricity will become cheap ammonia and, more slowly, cheap jet fuel. If not, it will mostly be used directly. Whether electricity itself can get cheaper again is the question essay 9 closes on.
