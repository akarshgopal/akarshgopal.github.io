The Machine: Magnets, Steam and the Limits of Heat

Essay 3 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

For eighty years every kilowatt-hour cost less than the one before. Most of that cheapening came from a single machine: a magnet turning inside coils of copper, driven by steam. This essay builds the machine, then follows the two ways it got cheaper and where each one stopped.

Essay 1 showed the American price falling by about 5 per cent a year from 1892 to 1973, and then not falling. Essay 2 showed what is being bought: energy, carried by charge pushed through a circuit by a voltage. Something has to do the pushing. For nearly all the world’s electricity, that something is a generator, and behind the generator, a fire.

1A magnet, a coil and a shaft

A battery pushes charge with chemistry and a solar cell with light, but nearly all of the world’s electricity has, for more than a century, been pushed by the effect Michael Faraday found in 1831: a changing magnetic field pushes charge around a loop of wire. Move a magnet into a coil and a current flows one way; pull it out and it flows the other way; hold the magnet still and nothing happens at all.

What matters is the rate of change. A faster movement gives a bigger voltage. The direction depends on whether the magnetic field threading the coil, its flux, is growing or shrinking, which is why pushing the magnet in and pulling it out give opposite kicks. This push is the “height” of essay 2: a generator is a machine for lifting charge up that hill, over and over.

Pushing a magnet in and out by hand will not light a city. Instead, we turn it. Put the same magnet on a shaft inside a coil, seen here end-on, and turn the shaft. As the north pole swings past one side of the coil and then the other, the flux through the coil rises, falls and reverses. Turn it slowly by hand, and then fast:

Turned steadily, the voltage traces a smooth wave, a sine, because the coil sees the magnet’s pole from a steadily turning angle. One revolution of a two-pole rotor gives one cycle, so a two-pole machine must spin at exactly 3,000 rpm to make 50 Hz and 3,600 rpm to make 60 Hz. A slow hydro turbine with many poles turns at a few hundred rpm and makes the same frequency. The frequency of a grid is the speed of its generators, which is why essay 5 can read the balance of a whole continent off a single number.

Three phases

A single coil’s power pulses: full, nothing, full, nothing, a hundred times a second. Large machines dislike pulsing torque. The fix, standard since the 1890s, is two more coils in the same machine, each a third of a turn round the stator. The magnet reaches each one a third of a turn later, so each makes the same sine wave, 120° behind the last: phase A, phase B and phase C.

Add the three voltages together and they make zero at every instant, and so do the currents into equal loads, so the three wires serve as each other’s return path and no heavy fourth conductor is needed. Add the three powers and the pulses fill each other’s gaps: the total is perfectly steady, and the shaft feels a smooth, constant load. This is why almost every high-voltage line carries its conductors in threes.

That is the machine at the heart of almost every power station: a magnet turning inside three sets of coils. A real one is the same arrangement scaled up to hundreds of megawatts, with an electromagnet in place of the permanent magnet. It is also very good at its job. A large generator turns more than 98 per cent of the mechanical power on its shaft into electricity. If electricity was ever going to get cheaper, it would not be by improving the generator. It would be by finding cheaper rotation.

2Heat into rotation

For most of the twentieth century, and still for most of the world’s electricity today, the rotation has come from heat. Burn coal or gas, or split uranium, boil water into high-pressure steam, and let the steam expand through a turbine that turns the generator. The steam is then condensed back to water by cooling water from a river, the sea or a cooling tower, and pumped back to the boiler.

Fuel was a power station’s biggest running cost, so the question that decided the price was: how much of the fuel’s heat comes out as electricity? Sadi Carnot answered the hardest version of it in 1824. An engine working between a hot source at absolute temperature Thot and a cold sink at Tcold can turn at most a fraction 1 − Tcold / Thot of the heat into work. The rest must go into the cold side. No cleverness gets round this; it follows from the second law of thermodynamics. The only ways to raise the ceiling are hotter steam or colder water.

Carnot’s limit is for an ideal engine that runs infinitely slowly. An engine that delivers power at a useful rate does worse. A simple model of one gives 1 − √(Tcold / Thot), and real steam plants land close to it.1

At 540 °C and 30 °C cooling water, the Carnot ceiling is 63 per cent and the practical estimate 39 per cent. Two-thirds of the fuel’s heat goes up the cooling tower. Raise the steam temperature and both curves climb, so for sixty years engineers chased temperature.

How close real plants got

Around 1900 an American power station turned about one-twentieth of its coal’s heat into electricity. By 1960 the average plant turned nearly a third.3 That sixfold saving in fuel per kilowatt-hour is the first engine of cheapening, and it came from pushing steam from a couple of hundred degrees to over 500 °C, with higher pressures, reheating between turbine stages and better condensers along the way.

Then the line goes flat. From the mid-1960s to today, the average American coal plant has turned about a third of its fuel into electricity. The Carnot ceiling at the same steam temperatures sits near 63 per cent, almost twice as high. Physics had plenty of room left. What stopped was the steam temperature itself.

The reason is metal. Above about 550 °C the steels used for boiler tubes and turbine casings slowly stretch under pressure, a process called creep, and corrode faster. A plant is built to last thirty or forty years, and a few degrees can take years off its life. In 1960 Eddystone 1, near Philadelphia, ran steam at about 650 °C and was the most efficient plant in the world, at around 40 per cent; it spent its life plagued by failures and was run at lower temperatures.2 Utilities drew the lesson and went back to about 540 °C. The plateau was a ceiling of engineering, not of physics.

Two things show it. Gas turbines, whose blades are cooled from inside and coated in ceramics, take their heat at well over 1,000 °C, and combined with a steam cycle on their exhaust they turn about 60 per cent of their fuel into electricity.4 And new nickel-rich steels have let a later generation of “ultra-supercritical” coal units run at 600–620 °C. China built more of them than anyone; its best reach about 47 per cent. Even those sit near the edge of what their steels allow. Each step now buys a point or two, not the factor of six the first sixty years gave.

3Bigger, and then too big

Fuel was one part of the cost of a kilowatt-hour. The other was the plant itself, and here the engine of cheapening was size.

A boiler, a turbine and a generator twice as large cost much less than twice as much to build, and need hardly more people to run. So the largest units grew from a few tens of megawatts in the 1910s to a few hundred by the 1950s, and reached 1,000 MW in the mid-1960s.5 Each step cut the cost of a kilowatt of capacity. Bigger units also ran hotter and at higher pressure, so the two engines turned together.

Size only pays if someone needs the power. What made it work in America was demand that grew by about 7 per cent a year, doubling roughly every decade, for most of the century before 1973.5 A utility could order the biggest unit yet and know the load would grow into it within a few years.

Both halves broke at about the same time. Units above about 1,000 MW proved harder to build and less reliable: they took longer, broke down more often, and a single outage took a large slice of a utility’s supply with it. Construction costs per kilowatt stopped falling and began to rise. The historian Richard Hirsh calls this technological stasis: by the late 1960s neither bigger nor hotter units were making electricity cheaper.5 Then, after 1973, demand growth itself slowed. What happened to prices when fuel and interest rates jumped on top of the stalled engine is the subject of essay 5 and essay 9.

The engine did not stop everywhere. China ran it again, decades later and on a larger scale. Its generation grew about 8½ per cent a year from 2000 to 2025, a little faster than America’s before 1973, from 1,356 TWh to 10,506 TWh.6 With demand growing that fast it could build large, efficient coal units in series and retire small, old ones, and the average efficiency of its fleet rose from about 31 per cent to about 40 per cent in twenty years: the first engine of cheapening, still running. But the newest units now sit at the same steel ceiling as everyone else. India’s generation has grown about 5 per cent a year since 2000, and almost three-quarters of the growth has come from coal.6

4Where the rotation comes from today

The world generated about 31,900 TWh of electricity in 2025, a third of it in China. Coal is still the largest single source, at a third of the total. Coal, gas, oil, nuclear and biomass, all of them heat engines, together made about 69 per cent. Add hydro and wind, which turn generators directly, and about 91 per cent of the world’s electricity still comes from a magnet turning inside coils. Only solar, 9 per cent of the total, has no moving parts.6

The four countries this series follows sit at very different points. The United States has swapped much of its coal for gas, which made 40 per cent of its power in 2025. Germany, a small grid by world standards at about 500 TWh, gets 45 per cent from wind and sun. China still makes 55 per cent from coal and India 71 per cent.

So the engine of the twentieth century had two levers: burn less fuel for each kilowatt-hour, and build each unit bigger. By about 1970 steel had stopped the first and unreliability and slowing demand the second. Solar panels and wind turbines restart the engine by a different road, and get essays of their own, 6 and 7. First, the power has to leave the power station. A generator makes it at around 20,000 volts, often far from the people who will use it, and that is where essay 4 picks it up.
