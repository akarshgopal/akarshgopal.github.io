Cheaper, Then Not: A Century of Electricity Prices

Essay 1 of 9 · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

Electricity is the form of energy that does nearly everything: it lights a room, turns a motor, boils a kettle and runs a computer. In 2025 the world generated about 31,800 terawatt-hours of it, close to 3,900 kilowatt-hours for every person alive.

For most of the twentieth century it also got cheaper, year after year, faster than almost anything else people bought. Then, in the early 1970s, it stopped. This essay tells that story and sets out the question the series is built around: why did electricity stop getting cheaper, and can it get cheaper again?

1Everywhere, and growing

Four countries carry this series: the United States, Germany, China and India. Between them they hold two people in five on the planet and generate more than half of its electricity.1 Each shows a different stage of the same story. The United States has the long record of prices. Germany has some of the dearest household electricity in Europe.2 China is building power stations, lines and solar factories at a pace no other country matches. India is still near the start.

Start with how much each of them uses.

An American uses about 13,000 kilowatt-hours a year, a German about 6,000, a person in China about 7,500 and a person in India about 1,400. The shapes say more than the levels. American use per person rose until about 2000 and has been flat since. German use has drifted down, by about a tenth since 1985. China’s has grown nineteenfold and passed Germany’s in 2023. India’s has grown almost sixfold, and nearly everyone there now has a connection: in 2000 four Indians in ten did not.3

In total, China now generates more than twice as much electricity as the United States, about a third of the world’s.

Electricity is also taking over more of the work that energy does. It supplies about a fifth of the energy the world finally uses, in homes, vehicles and factories, and each electric car, heat pump and electric furnace adds to that share.4 More and more, the price of electricity is the price of energy.

2A century of cheapening

A long record of that price exists for only one of the four. Germany, China and India keep price statistics too, but none of them is available here as a single line reaching back a century. The United States is the exception: the census’s Historical Statistics and the Energy Information Administration together reach back to the 1890s.5

In 1892 an American household paid about $5 for a kilowatt-hour, in 2002 dollars: roughly what a modern fridge uses in a day. By 1970 the same kilowatt-hour cost under 10 cents. Over those eight decades the real price fell by about 5 per cent a year on average, which halves a price every fourteen years.6

On a logarithmic scale a steady rate of decline is a straight line, and that is what the black line below is.

Then the line ends. Between 1973 and 1980 the nominal price more than doubled, from 2.5¢ to 5.4¢, and the old fall never came back.5

Had it continued at 5 per cent a year, a kilowatt-hour would now cost about half a cent in 2002 dollars. Americans paid 17.3¢ in 2025. Once inflation is taken out, that is roughly what they paid in 1970.8 Eighty years of cheapening were followed by more than fifty years of none.

A lot happened around the break, and the lanes under the curve list some of it: hotter steam and bigger plants, an oil embargo, new rules and delayed reactors. Sorting those events into an explanation is the work of the whole series.

3The question

So the question is easy to state. Why did electricity stop getting cheaper, and can it get cheaper again?

It is a sharper question now than at any time since 1973, because something has started falling again. A new solar farm produced its power for about a tenth as much in 2025 as in 2010.9 Yet the American household price rose from 12.6¢ in 2016 to 17.3¢ in 2025.5 If the cheapest new power in history is already here, why is the bill not falling?

Each of the four countries has its own version. In the United States, data centres and new factories are asking for more power than the grid can connect, and the cost of the wires keeps climbing. In Germany, power on the wholesale exchange is often cheap and sometimes free, while households pay more than twice the American price.2 China keeps industrial power cheap and builds at a pace that drives down the price of panels for everyone. India has connected almost everyone and buys solar power at some of the lowest prices ever recorded, through distribution companies that lose money.

These are not four different questions. They are the same one, asked at different stages.

4An engine and the costs around it

The series answers it with one simple picture, which later essays fill in.

Think of the price of a kilowatt-hour as the result of a race. On one side is an engine of productivity: better machines and bigger ones. For most of the twentieth century each new power station turned more of its coal into electricity than the last, and, being bigger, cost less for each kilowatt it could deliver. Demand grew fast enough that every new unit could be the biggest yet.

On the other side are the costs that rise by themselves or that nobody can engineer away: the price of fuel, the cost of borrowing the money to build, the wires that reach every house, and the taxes and levies governments add. They do not fall just because the machines get better.

While the engine runs faster than the costs rise, the price falls, even if some of the costs are climbing. When the engine slows, every rising cost shows through. When the engine stops and a cost jumps at the same moment, the price jumps and stays up.

That is the shape of the curve above. What slowed the engine, why a jump in fuel prices reached every kilowatt-hour so quickly, and whether today’s new engine can outrun today’s costs are the substance of the series. The short version is that the old engine stalled for reasons of engineering, not physics, and the new one, built from panels, turbines and batteries, makes energy cheap but not the rest of the bill.

5How the series answers it

Nine essays, read in order, each build one thing the answer needs.

First, the thing being sold. Essay 2 explains what a kilowatt-hour is: charge and current, voltage as a kind of height, and resistance as collisions that turn electricity into heat. Losses and heat set the rules for everything that follows. Essay 3 builds the machine that makes it, a spinning magnet driven by steam, and shows how bigger and hotter plants ran the engine for seventy years and why it stalled.

Then the system around the machine. Essay 4 follows the wires: why power travels at high voltage, what limits a line, and why the network now takes a growing share of the bill. Essay 5 shows how supply and demand are matched every second, and how the price of every kilowatt-hour is set by the last, most expensive plant needed, which is why a fuel shock passes straight through.

Then the new engine. Essay 6 follows solar power from photon to panel and the learning curve that made it cheap. Essay 7 adds wind, and the limit both run into: the more of them there is, the less each new kilowatt-hour is worth.

Then the money. Essay 8 adds up what a kilowatt-hour costs, from a power station’s capital and the interest on it to a household bill in four countries. Essay 9 puts the pieces together: why the cheapening stopped in the 1970s, why the price of electricity moves the rest of the economy, and what would have to be true for it to fall again.

Two deep dives sit beside the series for readers who want more: one on transformers, the other on fuels made from electricity.
