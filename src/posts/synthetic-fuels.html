Synthetic Fuels and the Price of a Kilowatt-Hour

An interactive essay · electrolysers, carbon, efficiency chains and the cost of cheap power · written for Akarsh Gopal, October 2026

Some jobs cannot be done with a cable. An aircraft crossing the Atlantic, a container ship crossing the Pacific and a fertiliser plant all need molecules. Those molecules can be made from electricity, water and carbon dioxide. Every step of the conversion loses energy, so the cost of the fuel follows the cost of the kilowatt-hour more closely than almost any other product in this series.

The main essay’s calculator showed what a megawatt-hour becomes: aluminium, steel, water, computation and hydrogen. This part takes the hydrogen line apart. It follows a kilowatt-hour through an electrolyser into ammonia, methanol and jet fuel, asks where the carbon comes from, why cost depends on the power price and the running hours, and why so many projects announced in 2020–2022 were cancelled. It ends with where these fuels are worth the losses and where plugging in directly wins.

1The conversion chain: electrons to molecules

Every electricity-based fuel starts the same way. An electrolyser passes direct current through water and splits it into hydrogen and oxygen. A kilogram of hydrogen holds 33.3 kWh (its lower heating value), or 39.4 kWh if the water formed when it burns is condensed. These are the floors; real systems need about 50–58 kWh per kilogram, as section 3 shows.1

Hydrogen is a good fuel and a hard thing to move. It is the lightest gas, a litre of it holds very little energy unless compressed to 350–700 bar or cooled to −253 °C, and it leaks through materials that hold methane. So most plans attach it to something:

  • Ammonia (NH₃): hydrogen plus nitrogen from the air, through the century-old Haber–Bosch process. It liquefies at −33 °C or under modest pressure, is already shipped around the world as fertiliser feedstock, and can be burned in adapted ship engines.
  • Methanol (CH₃OH): hydrogen plus carbon dioxide over a copper catalyst. A liquid at room temperature, used as a chemical feedstock and increasingly as a ship fuel.
  • E-kerosene and e-diesel: carbon dioxide is first reduced to carbon monoxide, then hydrogen and carbon monoxide are built into long hydrocarbon chains by the Fischer–Tropsch process (developed in Germany in the 1920s) and upgraded. The product can go straight into today’s aircraft. A newer route goes through methanol to jet fuel.
  • E-methane: hydrogen and CO₂ combined into the main component of natural gas, which can use the existing gas grid.

Each step costs energy. Pick a pathway below and follow 100 kWh from the wind farm to the job it ends up doing.

The battery car turns about 69 kWh of every 100 into motion. Hydrogen in a fuel cell gets about 22, and an e-fuel in an engine 11: the battery car needs about six times less electricity per kilometre. Heating shows the gap more sharply. A heat pump delivers almost three units of heat per unit of electricity; e-methane in a boiler returns a little under half a unit. The aircraft has no electric option yet: e-kerosene ends with about 39 kWh of fuel in the tank, and the engine still has to turn that into thrust.

The losses differ by product, because the chemistry differs. Ammonia needs no carbon at all; methanol and e-kerosene need a lot of it, and the hydrocarbon synthesis has its own losses. Build a litre of e-kerosene below from its three ingredients: hydrogen from an electrolyser, carbon dioxide from somewhere, and the energy to join them. Then switch to All products.

Hydrogen itself is the most efficient product, at about 1.65 kWh of electricity per kWh. Everything built from it inherits that loss and adds its own. E-kerosene needs about 2.8–3.1 kWh per kWh of fuel, 34–37 MWh per tonne or 27–30 kWh per litre, a little below aireg’s 38–47 kWh/kg (about 30–38 kWh per litre) unless the CO₂ comes from the air (the main essay’s calculator used its middle, 34 kWh per litre). Either way, the price of e-kerosene moves almost one-for-one with the price of power.

2Where the carbon comes from

A litre of e-kerosene contains the carbon of about 2.5 kg of CO₂, which has to come from somewhere. The easy sources are concentrated ones. Fermentation at an ethanol plant or brewery, and biogas upgrading, release almost pure CO₂ that needs only drying and compression, about 130–180 kWh per tonne.7 The Haru Oni pilot in Chile (section 6) uses brewery CO₂.

Industrial flue gas is harder: the CO₂ is 4–25% of the gas, so it has to be scrubbed out with an amine solvent that is then boiled to release it. That takes 2.5–3.7 GJ of steam per tonne.7 Fossil CO₂ from a cement kiln can be used, but the fuel made from it releases fossil carbon when it burns. At best it uses that carbon twice; it does not remove it.

The hardest source, and the only one available everywhere in unlimited quantity, is the air, where CO₂ is 0.04%. Direct air capture (DAC) blows huge volumes of air over a solvent or a solid sorbent and then regenerates it with heat. The IEA puts the total energy at 5.5–8.8 GJ per tonne for liquid systems and 7.2–9.5 GJ for solid ones, most of it heat.8

In the litre builder above, switch CO₂ from to the two air options and watch the carbon segment grow. Air capture is the cleanest source in principle: the fuel returns to the air only the CO₂ taken from it. It also adds roughly 10–25% to the energy for e-kerosene, depending on the heat supply, and more to its cost, because the plants are still expensive. So nearly every working e-fuel plant uses a concentrated source. Under EU rules a renewable fuel may use CO₂ from fossil power plants only until the end of 2035, and from other fossil industry until the end of 2040; after that the carbon must be biogenic or from the air.11

3Electrolysers: capex, utilisation and cheap hours

There are three commercial designs. Alkaline electrolysers, the oldest, use a liquid potassium hydroxide electrolyte and nickel electrodes; they are the cheapest per kilowatt and dominate China’s fast-growing fleet. PEM (proton-exchange membrane) electrolysers use a thin polymer membrane with platinum and iridium catalysts: compact and quick to follow a fluctuating supply, but dearer.

Solid-oxide electrolysers (SOEC) split steam at 700–850 °C, where part of the energy to break the water molecule can come as heat, so they need much less electricity per kilogram. In return they need heat and steam, and ramp slowly.

All three need direct current at high current and low voltage. A large electrolyser is therefore also a large rectifier, fed through the same kind of transformers that Part 3 described for HVDC converters and smelters.

Inside every one of them the same thing happens. A direct voltage across two electrodes pulls electrons out of water at one side and pushes them in at the other: oxygen bubbles off the anode, hydrogen off the cathode, twice as much of it, and ions cross the membrane between them to close the circuit. Splitting water needs at least 1.23 volts per cell. Every volt above that is the price of speed:

Push more current through each square centimetre and the same stack makes more hydrogen, so each kilogram carries less equipment cost; but the voltage climbs, and so does the electricity per kilogram. Commercial cells run near 2 A/cm² and 1.9 V, about 51 kWh per kilogram in the stack, which is where the DOE’s stack values sit.

Measured as whole systems, commercial alkaline and PEM electrolysers need about 55 kWh per kilogram when new and around 57 averaged over the stack’s life, as their cells wear; solid-oxide systems about 40, plus 9 kWh of steam heat.1 Those are system figures: the stack, where the water is split, needs about 51, and power electronics, pumps, cooling and gas drying add the rest. This part uses the new-system value, 55 kWh per kilogram, throughout, as does the main essay. Fleet surveys use their own accounting: the IEA puts the installed fleet’s 2024 average at 63% efficiency, about 53 kWh per kilogram, and China’s at 58%.412 Swap them in the section 1 builder: the electrolyser decides most of a litre’s energy. Best-case numbers are lower. In tests at Idaho National Laboratory, Bloom Energy’s 100 kW solid-oxide systems reached 36.7 kWh of DC electricity per kilogram, with the steam supplied separately.13 But the economics of electrolysis are set less by the last few kilowatt-hours than by two other numbers: what the equipment costs, and how many hours a year it runs.

Installing an electrolyser outside China cost $2,000–2,600 per kilowatt in 2024, about €1,770–2,300 (midpoint about €2,035; the figures below round to €2,100); in China, $600–1,200. More than half is engineering, construction and contingency; the stacks that actually split water are only 15–20%.14 That money is paid back from the hydrogen. Over 8,000 hours a year it adds little per kilogram; over 2,000 hours, four times as much. Electricity works the other way: the more hours a plant runs, the more of them are expensive. The explorer below combines the two, the hydrogen version of the main essay’s €/MWh calculator.

At €50/MWh and 4,000 hours, about €2,100/kW gives hydrogen at roughly €7 per kilogram, close to three times grey hydrogen, which a European producer makes for about €2.50 including today’s carbon price. Two levers dominate: each €10/MWh adds about €0.55 per kilogram, and halving the running hours roughly doubles the equipment share. Efficiency matters much less: a 10% better electrolyser saves about 10% of the electricity cost and nothing on capital.

Why the power price matters twice. The main essay’s calculator treats a megawatt-hour as a fixed input and asks what it buys. For an electrolyser the price and the hours are linked. Cheap hours are scarce, as Part 2’s cannibalisation section showed, and running more hours means buying dearer ones. Section 5 uses a full year of real German prices to find the balance.

4What €/MWh does to each product

Hydrogen is an intermediate. Industry and transport buy ammonia, methanol and jet fuel, each with a fossil competitor at a known price. Switch the explorer above to Ammonia or e-Kerosene: it builds each from the same electrolyser and sets it against the fossil version, ammonia from natural gas through a reformer and Haber–Bosch, and kerosene at IATA’s 2025 average of $86 a barrel.1617 The carbon price slider applies to all three.

At €50/MWh and 4,000 hours the gaps are large: electric hydrogen about €6.90 per kilogram against €2.50 grey; ammonia about €1,360 per tonne against €650; e-kerosene about €3.50 a litre against €0.66 for fossil jet fuel at today’s carbon price. The readout shows where a carbon price closes each gap: about €570 per tonne of CO₂ for hydrogen, €460 for ammonia and €1,200 for jet fuel. Cheaper power helps every product; each €10/MWh off takes about 27 cents off a litre of e-kerosene, two-fifths of what fossil kerosene costs.

The fixed-cost lesson from the electrolyser applies all along the chain. A Fischer–Tropsch plant, an ammonia loop and a CO₂ capture unit all cost money whether or not the wind is blowing. They also prefer steady operation, which means they need hydrogen storage, or an electrolyser that keeps running when the wind is weak.

5Demand that only exists at low prices

An appealing idea is that electrolysers could run on electricity that would otherwise be wasted: the hours when wind and solar are curtailed, when the grid cannot move the power (Part 4’s congestion section), or when solar floods the midday market and prices go negative. Such hours are real. In 2025 the German–Luxembourg day-ahead price was negative in 573 hours and zero or below in 655, about 7.5% of the year.18

But an electrolyser that only runs in those hours spends most of the year idle, and pays for its capital all year. The figure below sorts every hour of 2025 from cheapest to dearest, and lets the electrolyser buy the cheapest hours first.

At today’s European cost of about €2,100/kW, running only in the 655 free hours gives hydrogen at about €23 per kilogram. The cheapest point is about 6,500 hours and €6.50 per kilogram, buying power at an average €69/MWh: the plant must run most of the year on ordinary power to pay off its equipment. Agora reached the same conclusion in 2018: power-to-X plants need 3,000–4,000 full-load hours and cannot run on surplus power alone.2

Cheaper equipment moves the optimum. At €800/kW, near Chinese prices, it is about 2,300 hours and €4.10 per kilogram; at €300/kW installed (the US DOE’s 2031 target is $150/kW for the electrolyser system before installation), about 1,300 hours and under €1.80.1 Only a cheap electrolyser should chase cheap hours. Then it stops being just another load and becomes a buyer that lifts prices in the hours that matter most to wind and solar owners, improving their capture rates and solar’s capture price.

Where the electrolyser sits matters. Germany’s cheap hours are mostly windy hours in the north, while the demand is in the south and west. An electrolyser placed behind a congested line takes power that would otherwise be curtailed and paid for through redispatch. One placed beside a city pulls power across the same congested line. That is why grid planners prefer electrolysers at the coast, and why the hydrogen “core network” follows the same north–south route as the HVDC corridors. Moving energy as gas through pipelines competes directly with moving it as electricity.

6Projects, delays and cancellations

Between 2020 and 2022 hydrogen plans multiplied faster than buyers. In the IEA’s 2025 review, low-emissions hydrogen promised for 2030 fell from 49 to 37 million tonnes a year, and only 4.2 Mt is operating or past a final investment decision, against about 100 Mt used today, nearly all fossil.21

The project that did get built shows what it takes. NEOM in Saudi Arabia pairs 2.2 GW of electrolysers with 4 GW of dedicated sun and wind, and one buyer takes all its ammonia for 30 years; first ammonia is due in 2027.22 Very cheap power, a high combined capacity factor, a guaranteed buyer. Chile’s Haru Oni pilot, making e-fuel from Patagonian wind since 2022, has since scaled back its expansion targets.23

Europe’s cancellations show the opposite. Ørsted stopped its FlagshipONE e-methanol plant in 2024 for lack of buyers; ArcelorMittal turned down €1.3 billion for hydrogen-ready steelworks in 2025; HyScale 100 at Heide was reported abandoned in 2026.24 The common thread: power far above the prices at which the numbers work, and buyers unwilling to pay three times the fossil price.

7Mandates, auctions and credits

E-fuels exist where a law requires them or a subsidy pays the gap. ReFuelEU Aviation requires synthetic fuel to be 1.2% of EU jet fuel in 2030, rising to 35% in 2050;25 sustainable fuels of every kind were about 0.7% of the world’s jet fuel in 2025.16 FuelEU Maritime counts electricity-based ship fuels double until 2033.26

The European Hydrogen Bank auctions a fixed premium per kilogram: its first two rounds paid €0.20–0.60, far below the €4–5 gap of section 4, so the winners are mostly in Spain, Portugal and the Nordics.2728 The US credit is larger, up to $3 a kilogram, but only for projects started before 2028.27

India took a third route: a target of 5 million tonnes of green hydrogen a year by 2030, backed by about $2.3 billion of incentives for electrolyser factories and hydrogen output.29

8The honest limits

The efficiency chains in section 1 decide most of the argument. Where a battery, cable or heat pump can do the job, it needs between a third and a sixth of the electricity, so the molecule must be cheaper per kWh by that factor to compete. That rules out e-fuels for cars, vans, most trucks and buildings, the uses that dominated early marketing, and leaves those where no electric option exists or the molecule itself is the product.

  • Chemicals and fertiliser. Ammonia and methanol are already made from hydrogen, about 100 Mt of it a year, almost all from natural gas and coal.21 Swapping grey hydrogen for electric hydrogen adds no new conversion step, so this is the most obvious first market.
  • Long-haul aviation. Batteries hold about 50 times less energy per kilogram than kerosene. Short flights may go electric or hydrogen, but intercontinental flights need liquid fuel. Waste-based biofuels are limited, so e-kerosene is the backstop that the EU’s sub-quota is designed to force.
  • Deep-sea shipping. Ships can carry heavy tanks, but not enough batteries to cross an ocean. Ammonia (no carbon needed) and methanol (easy to handle) are the leading options.
  • Steel. Hydrogen can replace coal as the chemical reducing agent for iron ore, but ArcelorMittal’s decision shows the price at which it pays has not yet arrived in Europe.
  • Seasonal storage. Batteries handle hours to days. For a dark windless week in winter, hydrogen stored in salt caverns may be the cheapest backstop despite returning only about a third of the electricity (section 1), because its storage capacity costs so little.

9Futures at $10, $20 and $40 per MWh

The main essay asked what happens if electricity falls to $10, $20 or $40 per MWh. For electric fuels this is the central question, and the answer depends on whether cheap power comes with cheap equipment and high utilisation. So site your own plant for 2035: pick what it makes, then drag it to the power price and running hours you think you can get. The lines show the carbon price at which it would match the fossil product.

With today’s European equipment the three futures land at roughly €7, €4.40 and €4.20 per kilogram: very cheap but intermittent power helps little, because the equipment sits idle, and moderately cheap power for many hours does better. At Chinese equipment prices all three fall to €2.30–2.90, close to grey hydrogen with a moderate carbon price, and e-kerosene comes to about €2 a litre: still three times today’s jet fuel, but within reach of a mandate.

Read off the lines, the gap is a carbon price. With European equipment the hybrid site’s hydrogen matches grey hydrogen only at about €280 per tonne of CO₂, and its ammonia at about €200, three to four times today’s price. With Chinese equipment the same site’s hydrogen needs about €40, below today’s price, and its ammonia beats gas-based ammonia with no carbon price at all. e-Kerosene with European equipment needs more than €500 a tonne even on free power around the clock, which is why aviation gets a mandate rather than a price signal.

The lesson is wider. Cheaper energy only becomes cheaper molecules if the factories that use it also get cheaper and stay busy. Wind and solar became cheap through mass manufacturing and learning, as Part 1 traced for solar. Electrolysers are at the start of that path; China installed about 65% of the world’s new capacity in 2024, at prices already well below Western ones.14 If they follow solar, cheap electricity will become cheap ammonia and, more slowly, cheap jet fuel. If not, it will mostly be used directly, as the main essay’s capture-rate figure suggested it should be anyway.
