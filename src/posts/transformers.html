Transformers: The Quiet Bottleneck

An interactive essay · magnetism, heat, steel and supply chains · by Akarsh Gopal · October 2026

Almost every unit of electricity you use has passed through four or five transformers on its way to you. They have no moving parts, routinely last forty years, and the best waste less than a quarter of one per cent of the power through them. Since 2020 they have also become one of the scarcest parts of the energy transition: in 2024 a large one took three years to arrive.1

A transformer is two coils on an iron core: voltage up, current down. It is why the grid runs on alternating current and why transmission at hundreds of kilovolts is possible. Inside the box are a few elegant ideas from the 1830s and 1880s, a lot of specialised steel, a heat problem that sets the machine’s lifetime, and the story of how a boringly available product became a bottleneck.

1Flux, turns and why it needs AC

In 1831 Michael Faraday wound two coils around an iron ring and found that switching a current on or off in one gave a brief kick of current in the other, while a steady current gave nothing. A changing magnetic field induces a voltage; a steady one does not. That is the whole principle of the transformer.

Make the current alternate and the field never stops changing. The iron core, thousands of times more permeable than air, gathers almost all of it into a closed loop, the flux, and threads it through the second coil. Every turn around the core sees the same changing flux and gets the same voltage, so each coil’s voltage is proportional to its turns:

Voltage and current trade places exactly. Double the secondary turns and you double its voltage and halve its current, so the power is unchanged.

The core flux does not depend on the load. When the load draws current, the primary draws a matching current and their magnetic effects cancel. With nothing connected, the primary still draws a small magnetising current to keep the flux going.

The supply alone sets the flux, because the changing flux must induce in the primary a voltage that balances it. More turns, a faster cycle or a fatter core each mean less flux density is needed for the same voltage. Written out, that is the transformer equation V = 4.44 f N A B, with A the core’s cross-section and B the peak flux density.

The equation also explains why transformers are big. Engineers want few turns, because copper is costly and resistive, so they push B to 1.6–1.7 teslas in modern steel, not far below where it saturates at about 2. At 50 or 60 Hz that leaves one way to reach high voltage: a thick core. Raise the frequency and the core can shrink, the idea behind the solid-state transformer at the end.

Switch the supply to DC and the transformer stops working: the flux rises once and stays, nothing is induced, and only the copper’s resistance limits the current. That is why Edison’s DC networks could not use transformers, and why alternating current won once Zipernowsky, Bláthy and Déri at the Ganz works in Budapest built practical closed-core transformers in 1885. A small DC offset on top of the AC is a milder form of the same problem, and it returns in section 8.

2Core and copper losses

A transformer loses energy in two places. The iron core loses some every time its magnetisation reverses, a hundred or more times a second, loaded or not. The copper windings lose the rest as heat, growing with the square of the load.

Core loss has two parts. Hysteresis: magnetic domains stick and snap rather than swing freely, so magnetising and demagnetising follow different paths, and the area of the loop is energy turned to heat each cycle. Eddy currents: the changing flux induces voltages in the iron itself, driving currents round inside the core.

Materials science has attacked both. About 3% silicon raises iron’s resistance and cuts eddy currents. In 1934 the American metallurgist Norman Goss found how to roll and anneal silicon steel so its grains line up; this grain-oriented electrical steel (GOES) magnetises far more easily along the rolling direction, with a much narrower loop. Building the core from thin insulated sheets, usually 0.23–0.30 mm, traps eddy currents in each sheet, and because eddy loss grows with the square of thickness, halving the sheet quarters it. In a solid plate the eddy loss swamps everything else, which is why no one builds cores that way.

Amorphous metal goes further: an alloy cooled so fast, in ribbons about 0.025 mm thick, that it never crystallises. Its core losses are a fraction of silicon steel’s, but it is brittle, saturates lower and is harder to build with. China and India have adopted it far more widely than the United States, with 1.1 and 1.3 million amorphous-core transformers.1

Copper losses are simpler: current squared times resistance. Since core loss is fixed and copper loss grows with load squared, a transformer is most efficient where the two are equal. The EU’s ecodesign rules set maximums for both at every rating, and the figure uses them:4

A 1,000 kVA unit built to the 2021 limits may waste at most 693 W in its core and 7,600 W in its windings at full load, and its efficiency peaks at 99.5% near 30% load.4 Those fractions sound negligible, but core loss runs every hour for decades on transformers that are mostly lightly loaded, so utilities “capitalise” losses, paying more up front for a lower-loss core.

3Heat, oil and paper

Every watt of loss becomes heat, and heat is what limits a transformer. A large unit is a steel tank of mineral oil with the core and windings submerged. The oil insulates and carries heat by convection: up through the windings, out to radiators, down again as it cools. With nothing but convection the rating is ONAN (oil natural, air natural); fans on the radiators (ONAF) let the same unit carry a third or more extra load.

What wears out is the paper, not the copper or iron. Each conductor is wrapped in cellulose, which slowly loses strength with heat until, decades later, a fault tears it. The loading guide IEC 60076-7 puts a number on it: ordinary kraft paper ages at its normal rate at a hot-spot temperature of 98 °C, the hottest point of the winding, and twice as fast for every 6 °C above that; upgraded paper is referenced to 110 °C.5

The figure opens with the evening peak at 130%: the hot spot reaches about 112 °C for an hour or two and the paper ages five times faster than normal. Yet the whole day costs only about half a normal day of life, because the lightly loaded night ages it hardly at all.

On a 35 °C day the same load drives the hot spot to about 127 °C and costs more than three days. Because the law is exponential, a few hot hours dominate. A transformer that reaches its rating only on the hottest evenings can far outlive its nominal forty years; one loaded hard every day in a hot climate can be worn out in half the time. That is why utilities fear a heatwave plus an air-conditioning peak, and why a street that adopts heat pumps and electric cars may find its transformer running hot every evening.

4Three phases, stars and deltas

The grid is three-phase: each generator has three coils a third of a turn apart, making three voltages 120° apart. A three-phase transformer has a limb, and a winding, per phase. Take the three windings out and there are two ways to connect them. Join one end of each at a common point and you have a star (Y); join them end to end in a loop and you have a delta (D).

With equal loads the three currents cancel at the star point and the neutral carries nothing. Unbalance them and the difference flows home along the neutral. In delta there is no common point at all; each winding sits between two lines, so it sees the voltage between them, √3 times bigger and 30° ahead. The choice therefore changes each winding’s voltage by √3, decides whether there is a neutral to earth, and can rotate the output’s phase. Real transformers combine the two:

The neighbourhood transformer in 230 V countries needs a star with an earthed neutral on the low side, to give each house 230 V between a phase and neutral, and a delta on the high side, to keep the circulating currents of unbalanced loads off the medium-voltage network. Because a delta winding sees the voltage between two phases, the combination also shifts every phase by 30°, one hour on a clock face. IEC 60076-1 writes it Dyn11: delta high side, star low side with neutral, low-voltage phasor at eleven o’clock.

The shift is harmless alone but matters when transformers meet: two units can run in parallel only if their clock numbers match, otherwise, as the readout shows, their outputs differ by half the voltage. Turned into a control knob, the same idea gives the phase-shifting transformer, which injects an adjustable shifted voltage to push power onto or off a line, one of the few ways to steer power flow in a meshed grid.

5Tap changers and voltage

Every customer should receive a voltage within a band: in Europe, EN 50160 allows ±10% around the nominal 230 V.7 But voltage drops along a line with the current, so the far end of a long feeder sees less than the near end, more so as demand rises. The transformer is where it can be corrected.

A tap changer changes the number of turns in use on one winding, in steps of about 1–1.5%. The on-load tap changer, developed in the 1920s, does it under full current, using a diverter switch and resistors so the circuit is never broken. Substation transformers have one, and an automatic voltage regulator steps it every few minutes to hold a set-point.

On a winter evening, with every house drawing power, a fixed set-point at the top of the band keeps the far end above the limit. On a sunny Sunday the problem reverses: rooftop panels push power back towards the substation, voltage rises along the feeder and the far end climbs above +10%. One tap changer at the start cannot fix both, since whatever it does to the near end applies to the far end too. Utilities answer with load-compensated control, tap-changing neighbourhood transformers, and rules requiring solar inverters to absorb reactive power when voltage rises.

6The family

Between a power station and a socket, electricity usually passes through four or five transformers, from machines the size of a house to boxes on a pole. They are all the same machine, two coils on an iron core, built at different sizes. Take one and scale it from 1 kVA to 1 GVA, a millionfold:

Two things change as it grows. The efficiency floor rises: a 25 kVA unit may lose about 1.6% of what passes through it at its best load, a 100 MVA unit only 0.23%. And the weight grows far more slowly than the rating: across the catalogue, 25 times the rating brings only seven times the mass.

That is geometry. Double every dimension of a transformer and the core’s cross-section and the window for the copper each grow fourfold, so the rating grows about sixteenfold while the volume, the weight and most of the losses grow only eightfold. Big transformers are cheaper per kVA and waste a smaller share, which is why the network concentrates power through a few very large ones.

At the top are large power transformers, 100 MVA and up, which the US Department of Energy estimates handle 90% of American electricity at some point. They weigh 135–365 tonnes, are custom-designed for their site, and travel on special rail cars.1

At the bottom are distribution transformers, cheap and numerous: the US has an estimated 60–80 million.10 A European neighbourhood unit is typically 400–630 kVA in a kiosk; an American 25 kVA can on a pole serves a handful of houses.

Only the last step changes much around the world. Europe, China, India, Russia, most of Africa and Australia deliver 230 V single-phase from a 400 V three-phase network, the IEC standard. The US, Canada and Mexico deliver 120/240 V split-phase from small transformers that each serve a few houses, so North America has far more, smaller units per customer. Japan uses 100 V, at 50 Hz in the east and 60 Hz in the west.11

Renewables add a layer. A 1,000 MW gas plant connects through one or two large step-up transformers; 1,000 MW of wind or solar needs hundreds of small ones at each turbine or inverter block, a collector network and a substation transformer. That is one reason the shortage hit renewable developers so hard, and why generator step-ups saw the biggest jump in demand.12

7The shortage

For most of the twentieth century transformers were a mature, slow business: flat demand in rich countries, leisurely replacement, factories sized to match. Then almost every driver moved at once: renewable connections, data centres, electrification, replacing units from the 1960s and 70s and, in the US, storm hardening. US demand for power transformers more than doubled between 2019 and 2025.12 Factories could not follow.

After a decade of near-flat prices, transformers rose over 60% in price between 2020 and 2023, and have not come down: in August 2026 the US producer price index stood 88% above its 2019 average, against 31% for consumer prices.13

Lead times stretched more. Before the pandemic a large unit came within a year; in 2024 the Department of Energy reported 36 months as common and some quotes of five years.11014

The bottleneck runs down to the steel. GOES and specially insulated copper each make up roughly a quarter of a large transformer’s cost. The US has one GOES producer, Cleveland-Cliffs, which meets a fifth or less of domestic demand; the rest is imported, much of it inside finished transformers, and the best “domain-refined” grade came from a single supplier, Nippon Steel.1

The same mills can make non-oriented steel for electric-car motors instead, and motors pay better. When the US tightened distribution-transformer standards in 2024, it set them so that about three-quarters of designs could still use GOES rather than amorphous metal, to avoid disrupting supply; compliance starts in 2029.15

Makers are adding factories, but a large transformer is still largely hand-built: windings wound by skilled workers, cores stacked sheet by sheet, the finished unit dried and tested before it ships.12 Most public data are American, but utilities everywhere buy from the same factories and the same steel, and the wait lengthens the queue for new wind and solar farms.

8Failure and resilience

Transformers rarely fail, but when a large one does, replacing it takes months with a spare and years without. The threats are varied.

The most exotic is the sun. In a severe geomagnetic storm, swings in the Earth’s field induce slow voltages across hundreds of kilometres of ground, pushing quasi-DC currents through transformer neutrals. They add a DC offset to the flux, exactly as in the first figure: the core saturates every half-cycle, draws huge distorted currents, consumes reactive power and heats up. On 13 March 1989 a storm tripped the compensators holding up voltage on Hydro-Québec’s long lines, and the network collapsed in about 90 seconds, leaving six million people dark for nine hours. The same storm wrecked a step-up transformer at the Salem nuclear plant in New Jersey.16

Other threats are mundane. In April 2013 attackers fired rifles at PG&E’s Metcalf substation in California, damaging 17 transformers; operators avoided a blackout, but repairs took nearly a month. In December 2022 gunfire at two substations in Moore County, North Carolina, cut power to up to 45,000 customers for days.17 Fires, floods and old age take out more units than either.

The defences are mostly organisational: spares held by utilities or shared through programmes such as Grid Assurance in the US, standard designs so a spare fits several sites, mobile “recovery transformers”, and devices that block storm currents in the neutral. All cost money for kit that may never be used, and in a shortage a spare in a yard is a transformer not connecting someone’s wind farm.

9Solid-state transformers

The transformer equation suggests an escape from size and steel: raise the frequency. At 400 Hz, the aircraft standard, a transformer needs far less iron than at 50 Hz, and at tens of kilohertz less still. A solid-state transformer uses power electronics to get there: it rectifies AC to DC, chops it into high-frequency AC with fast silicon or silicon-carbide switches, passes it through a small transformer and converts it back.18

Slide the frequency up and the core shrinks dramatically, but the box on the right grows a chain of converters. Each stage loses perhaps 1%, more than a conventional transformer’s single half-percent, and every switch, capacitor and fan can fail, unlike a tank of oil and copper that runs for half a century. In exchange, a solid-state transformer can regulate voltage continuously, offer a DC port for batteries, solar or electrolysers, control power flow and block faults.

So the copper-and-iron box keeps winning in the bulk grid while power electronics spread at its edges: HVDC converter stations, trains, fast chargers, data centres and electrolysers that need DC anyway, and the inverters of every solar farm and battery. The likely future is not fewer transformers but many more, and more of them next to a converter.
