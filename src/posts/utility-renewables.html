Utility-Scale Renewables: Building and Running the Fleet

An interactive essay · aerodynamics, queues, contracts and control · written for Akarsh Gopal, October 2026

At the end of 2024 the United States had about 1,320 gigawatts of power plants in operation, and 2,290 gigawatts of new projects waiting in line to plug into the grid. Most of the waiting projects are solar farms, batteries and wind farms. Most will never be built.1

The main essay showed why wind power rises with the cube of wind speed, and Part 1 followed a photon into a solar cell and out into a market. This part is about building those machines by the hundred, in fields and at sea, and making them earn a living on a grid designed around spinning generators. The engineering is only half of it. A modern wind or solar farm is also a contract, a grid connection and a control system, and each has become, in turn, the thing that decides whether it gets built.

1Project anatomy and the queue

A wind or solar farm starts life as a land option, a measurement mast and a spreadsheet. The developer secures land, measures the resource, applies for permits and asks the grid operator to connect; only with all that, and a buyer, can it be financed. Construction is often the shortest phase: a year for a large solar farm.

The request to connect has become the bottleneck. The grid operator must study the plant’s effect on every nearby line and transformer, decide which upgrades it needs and who pays. In the United States those studies happen in a public queue, compiled each year by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory:

The queue dwarfs the fleet: 2,290 GW waiting, mostly solar and storage. Most of it is speculative: of requests made in 2000–2019, only 13% were operating by 2024. And the median wait has stretched from under two years to over four.1

Entering was cheap, so a developer with ten possible sites filed all ten and let the studies sort them, slowing everyone else. Since 2023 US reforms study projects in batches and demand bigger deposits; in 2024 the queue shrank for the first time.1 Behind the paperwork, the real constraint is the upgrades: transformers in short supply and lines that take a decade.

Why a new plant can need upgrades to lines hundreds of kilometres away, and who should pay for them, is the subject of Part 4 on power flow and transmission planning. The main essay’s network figure shows the basic physics: power takes every path at once.

Most of the world runs auctions instead: the state decides how much capacity it wants and where, often supplies land and grid connection, and developers bid the price at which they will sell for 20–25 years. South Africa’s REIPPPP cut its solar tariff by 91% in real terms between 2011 and 2021.2

Indian solar bids reached 2.7 US cents per kWh in 2020,3 and Saudi Arabia’s Shuaibah 1 set the record at 1.04 cents.4 Auctions put the risks of land, permits and grid on the state, and in exchange deliver some of the cheapest electricity ever sold.

2Wind physics at scale

The main essay derived two facts about turbines. The power in the wind through a rotor is ½ρAv³, proportional to swept area and the cube of wind speed, and no rotor can capture more than 16/27 of it, the Betz limit. Modern three-bladed turbines reach 45–50% at their best.

Both facts reward size. Swept area grows with the square of blade length, so doubling the rotor quadruples the energy. And wind speeds up with height, away from the drag of the ground; because of the cube, a few percent more speed matters. So turbines have grown relentlessly:

The average new American onshore turbine in 1998–99 had a 48-metre rotor and a 0.72 MW rating; in 2024, a 137 m rotor, a 106 m hub and 3.5 MW.5 At sea, with no roads to carry blades and no neighbours to object, the largest machines have rotors of 220–236 m, each blade about the length of a football pitch, and ratings of 13–15 MW.

A blade turns because each slice of it is a small wing. The slice meets the wind coming at the rotor, but it is also moving sideways through the air, and near the tip far faster than the wind itself. What it feels is the sum of the two, an apparent wind arriving almost edge-on. Lift acts at right angles to that apparent wind, so it leans slightly into the direction of rotation, and that small lean is the torque that drives the generator:

Because the slice speed grows from root to tip while the wind does not, the apparent wind swings from about 30° off the rotor plane near the hub to about 5° at the tip. A flat blade would meet it at the right angle at only one radius: near the root it stalls. So blades are twisted, steep at the root and nearly flat at the tip. Raise the wind with the rotor speed fixed and the angle of attack grows everywhere; real turbines then pitch the whole blade, or let the outer sections stall, to cap the power.

On a wind farm the largest losses come from turbines stealing wind from each other. A rotor leaves behind a wake, a tube of slower, more turbulent air that recovers only over many rotor diameters, and a turbine standing in it loses much more power than speed. The classic engineering model, by Niels Otto Jensen at Risø in 1983, treats the wake as a cone widening at a fixed rate.7 It is crude, but gets the essentials right:

The figure opens with the wind blowing straight down a row: the second turbine makes far less than the first, and those behind settle lower still, each in the merged wakes upwind. At Horns Rev, an early Danish offshore farm at seven-diameter spacing, the second turbine made only about 60% as much as the first at 8 m/s with the wind within two degrees of a row.9 The model gives 59%.8 That is the worst case, though: with the wind from every direction in turn, the whole array loses only about 12% of its output. Turn the wind a few degrees and the wakes slide between the turbines and the losses largely vanish, hence the sharp dips in the curve below.

The farm is offshore. Open More and switch the site to onshore: rougher air over land mixes wakes in faster. Smooth sea air keeps them longer, so offshore turbines are spaced further apart, and in a crowded North Sea one farm’s wake can cut the output of a neighbour tens of kilometres downwind. Spacing costs cable and seabed; large offshore farms lose of the order of 10–20% of their output to wakes.9

3Capacity factor is a design choice

The capacity factor, average output over rated power, is often quoted as a property of the site. It is largely the designer’s choice, through one ratio: generator size relative to rotor, or specific power, watts per square metre of swept area. The American fleet’s specific power fell from 393 W/m² in 1998–99 to 237 W/m² in 2023, because rotors grew much faster than generators.5

Keep the rating fixed and enlarge the rotor: the turbine reaches full power at lower winds, the duration curve fills out and the capacity factor climbs. Shrink the generator under the same rotor and it rises too, but by throwing away the strongest winds. Both moves are deliberate. Low specific power gives steadier output, worth more on a grid that already has plenty in storms, and suits weaker-wind sites closer to cities.

The fleet shows it: US wind projects built in 2023 averaged a 37.5% capacity factor in 2024, against 34% for the fleet built since 1998.5 Solar developers make the same choice with the inverter loading ratio, accepting some clipping at noon for more output in the shoulders. What is being optimised is value, not energy, which is why the next sections are about money.

The solar version of this trade-off (trackers, east–west racking and inverter ratios across the world’s climates) is worked through with real hourly data in Part 1: a kilowatt around the world.

4Offshore, supply chains and the cost reversal

Offshore wind has some of the best wind resources in the world, strong, steady and close to coastal cities, and the hardest engineering. Turbines stand on steel monopiles driven into the seabed, or on lattice jackets in deeper water, and the power reaches shore through export cables and an offshore substation, or for distant farms through HVDC. Only a handful of vessels in the world can lift the largest turbines.

For a decade its cost fell faster than almost anyone forecast. The clearest record is Britain’s auctions for contracts for difference (next section), where developers bid the price they will accept for fifteen years:

Winning bids fell from about £115–120/MWh (2012 money) in 2015 to under £40 by 2019 and £37.35 in 2022. Then the curve turned. Interest rates rose, steel and copper jumped, and turbine makers who had sold at a loss raised prices. In the fifth round in 2023, capped at £44, not one offshore project bid.

Ørsted cancelled its 2.2 GW Ocean Wind projects off New Jersey in October 2023, booking DKK 28.4 billion of US impairments, and in May 2025 dropped the 2.4 GW Hornsea 4, eight months after it won a contract.12 Later UK rounds cleared at about £54–59 and then £64–65, roughly £90/MWh in 2024 money: about 75% above the 2022 low in real terms.11

The lesson reaches beyond offshore wind. Wind and solar have almost no fuel cost, so they are acutely sensitive to the cost of capital (interest rates rising from 2% to 5% raise the cost of a megawatt-hour by roughly 30%) and to the price of steel, copper and magnets. Solar largely escaped, because Chinese module overcapacity kept prices falling. Wind turbines, made by a few Western firms with thin margins, did not. Every technology has its own bottleneck: Chinese wafers and magnets for solar; vessels and cable factories for offshore wind; and large transformers for everything.

Where the turbines are

The auction story is British, but most of the world’s wind is not. In 2025 wind made about 2,700 TWh, 8.5% of global electricity and eight times its 2010 output. China generated 42% of it, more than the EU and US together. Brazil’s north-eastern trade winds, among the steadiest on land, supply 116 TWh, more than India’s 104. Germany gets over a quarter of its electricity from wind.13

5Who carries the risk

A wind farm’s costs are known the day it is built; its income is not. It depends on how windy the year is, on the price in each hour, and on how far that price falls when the wind blows everywhere at once: volume, price and shape. Lenders charge less for predictable income, so whoever takes those risks also sets the cost of the electricity.

The figure runs one German onshore wind farm through eleven real years, in which wind’s market value swung from about €25/MWh to €168,14 and asks what it would have received under each kind of contract:

A merchant plant sells into the market and takes what comes, ruinous years and windfall years. A feed-in tariff, which built Germany’s and Spain’s first fleets, pays a fixed price per megawatt-hour and passes the price risk to consumers. Germany now uses a sliding premium: the plant sells in the market and gets a top-up whenever wind’s market value falls below its auction strike price.

The figure opens on a two-sided contract; look at 2022. Under the sliding premium the farm kept the whole windfall. Now imagine the top-up also works in reverse: when the market pays more than the strike price, the farm hands the excess back to consumers, the red slice. That is a two-sided contract for difference (CfD), as in Britain, and the EU’s 2024 reform makes it the default for new state-supported plants from 2027.17

Increasingly the buyer is a company: in a power purchase agreement (PPA), a data centre or steelmaker buys a farm’s output at a fixed price for ten to fifteen years. A pay-as-produced PPA leaves the buyer with the shape risk; a baseload PPA promises a flat block, so the generator must buy in when the wind is weak. Pick it in the figure and look at 2022.

6Capture prices and curtailment

Every panel produces in the same hours and pushes the price down in exactly those hours, so solar sells at a discount to the average; the main essay showed that discount deepening and Part 1 traced why. What a technology earns per megawatt-hour divided by the average price is its capture rate. The dots below are Germany’s real years. The dashed curve asks what 2025 would have looked like with more or less solar: it takes every hour of the year, scales the solar output up or down, and re-prices each hour at what the market actually paid in 2025 when that much demand was left for other plants to meet.

In 2025 German solar earned 52% of the average price, down from 93% in 2019; onshore wind held at 87%, and offshore wind, which blows more at night and in winter, at 96%. The capture rate falls as output grows, faster the more bunched in time it is.14 The re-priced 2025 tracks solar’s real slide closely: at 2019’s share it gives 92%, at 2025’s 57%. Double the solar with nothing else changed and solar earns about an eighth of the average price, with prices below zero for about 1,900 hours. Nothing else changing is the catch: batteries, exports and flexible demand grow where midday power is cheap, and 40 GW of two-hour batteries would lift that eighth to about 30%. The same slide is under way wherever solar grows fast. In California negative prices fall almost only in sunny late-spring hours; in South Australia the five-minute price averaged zero or below in 2,620 hours of 2025, 30% of the year.20

A low price is the market’s signal. The physical version is curtailment: farms told to turn down while the wind blows. Some happens because nobody will take more power, so prices go negative and plants stop. In Germany most has another cause: the wind is in the north, demand in the south and west, and the lines between are full.

About 9.4 TWh was curtailed in each of 2024 and 2025, roughly 3.5% of German renewable output, or the consumption of about 2.7 million homes (at 3,500 kWh a year each). Offshore wind, landing on the north coast, is curtailed most; solar curtailment almost doubled in 2025, increasingly because local distribution grids are full. Congestion management costs about €3 billion a year.21 In the United States, the Southwest Power Pool, the windiest market, curtailed 10.5% of its wind in 2024.5

Curtailment is the visible cost of a grid that has not kept up. How congestion is managed, and what it would take to build the lines that relieve it, are the subject of Part 4: congestion, getting more from existing wires and interconnection between countries. The other answer is to use the surplus where it falls: the subject of Part 5 on cheap demand and the main essay’s moving the surplus.

7Hybrid plants and batteries

If noon is worth little and the evening a lot, store some noon output. Batteries are now cheap enough, and the best place for them is often the solar farm itself. A hybrid plant shares one grid connection, the scarce asset of section 1, between panels and batteries: it charges from output the connection would otherwise clip and discharges in the evening when the panels are idle.

The figure builds one from a real year: hourly output of a tracking solar plant near Seville in 2023, selling at that year’s Spanish day-ahead prices.

The figure opens with a 0.4 MW, four-hour battery behind a connection of 0.75 MW per MW of panels, the US benchmark ratio from Part 1. Little is lost to the connection; the bigger cost is the low noon price. The battery moves a slice of noon into the evening peak and lifts the plant’s average price from about €73 to €89/MWh, slightly above the year’s average, and revenue by about a sixth, though its losses mean fewer megawatt-hours sold.

Each extra hour of storage adds less, because most days have only a few dear hours to fill. Open More and shrink the connection: the battery recovers output that would be lost, which is why developers now design panels, storage and connection together: in the US queue, nearly half of all solar is proposed as part of a hybrid plant.1

The figure leaves out the battery’s own cost, which decides whether the extra revenue is worth it, and the other markets a battery can serve, frequency reserves above all. But the result is general: storage lifts solar’s value most where there is a lot of solar, and the first hours are worth the most.

What batteries can and cannot do for a whole grid (hours of shifting versus weeks of dark, still weather) is the subject of the main essay’s dark week, and its clean-grid builder lets you size them yourself.

8Grid-forming inverters

The main essay showed the grid’s frequency as its heartbeat: large generators spin in lockstep at 50 or 60 Hz, and when a plant trips, the missing power comes first from their kinetic energy. They slow together, which is what a falling frequency is, and the more spinning mass, or inertia, the slower the fall. Governors then open valves to arrest it; if they are too slow, relays start disconnecting customers to save the rest.

Wind, solar and batteries connect through power electronics, and most today are grid-following: they measure the grid’s voltage and frequency and inject current to match, like a dancer following a partner. They add no inertia, so system inertia falls as they replace coal and gas. A grid-forming inverter sets its own voltage and frequency and pushes back at once when the grid moves, much like a synchronous machine, provided it has stored energy to draw on, typically a battery.

Slide the inverter share up with no grid-forming plant and the fall steepens and deepens; past a point the frequency hits the load-shedding threshold before reserves respond. Add grid-forming capacity, or batteries that inject power within a second, and stability returns, mostly without fuel. That is why Britain and Australia, with high wind and solar shares on smallish grids, buy inertia and grid-forming capability, and why grid codes increasingly demand it.

Spinning machines also held voltage steady for free, by absorbing and supplying reactive power. On 28 April 2025, mainland Spain and Portugal blacked out within seconds. ENTSO-E’s expert panel, in its final report, found no single cause: oscillations earlier that day, too little dynamic voltage control, renewable plants at a fixed power factor, reactors switched by hand and a wide permitted voltage range together let voltage climb until plants tripped in a cascade, Europe’s first overvoltage blackout.

The panel’s main recommendation: require new generators, wind and solar included, to control voltage actively.25 The inverters can do it; until recently the rules did not ask them to.

The voltage on the grid is also held by transformer tap changers and reactors: Part 3 has a feeder where rooftop solar pushes the voltage past its limit; how those devices behave under stress is part of Part 3: resilience.

9Fitting it into a system

One farm can be made more valuable with a bigger rotor, a battery and a better contract. A system must match output that follows the weather to demand that follows people, every hour of the year. Wind and solar help each other: in Germany solar peaks in summer and wind in winter.

The main essay’s model day shows why. It opens on a winter weekday evening with a fair wind; switch the season and watch the sources trade places.

In 2025 German wind and solar made about 207 TWh against demand of 466 TWh. Wind made twice as much in January as in July, solar more than five times as much in July as in January, and the sum is flatter than either. Switch to the hourly view, which opens on the stillest week of 2025 in mid-January, when wind and solar averaged under 9 GW. Over the year their combined output ranged from under 1 GW to 63 GW, and the price followed: Germany had 573 hours of negative prices.14

Everything in this series is a partial answer to that variability. Bigger, low-specific-power turbines and offshore wind fill more hours. Batteries move energy across hours; transmission and interconnectors move it across places. Flexible demand (heat pumps, electric cars, electrolysers) can soak up surplus. Contracts decide who is paid for each, and grid codes whether inverters hold the system up. None removes the need for firm capacity in the dark, still week; the main essay’s clean-grid builder and What would have to be true sketch how the pieces add up.

The physics of wind and sunlight is settled. What decides how fast the fleet grows is the rest: the queue, the cost of capital, how contracts share risk and how quickly the wires follow. The next parts follow those wires, first through the transformers that change the voltage, then along the transmission lines themselves.

What becomes of the surplus megawatt-hours that no battery or wire can use is the subject of Part 5: what €/MWh does to each product and the main essay’s what a megawatt-hour unlocks. Part 5 also shows why an electrolyser cannot live on curtailed hours alone until its equipment gets much cheaper, and tests this against every hour of 2025.
