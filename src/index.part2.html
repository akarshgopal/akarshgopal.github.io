5Fifty hertz

Here is the strangest fact about the grid. Continental Europe, from Portugal to Turkey, is a single synchronous machine, and so is the eastern half of North America. Thousands of large generators turn in lockstep, their magnetic fields locked to each other through the wires, like the rotors in the figure below.

And the grid stores almost no energy. At every instant, generation must equal consumption plus losses. When they differ, the difference comes, in the first seconds, from the only store there is: the kinetic energy of all those spinning rotors. If consumption wins, the rotors slow and the frequency falls. Frequency is therefore a live, continent-wide measurement of balance, reported by every socket: 50 Hz across Europe, most of Asia, Africa and Australia, 60 Hz across most of the Americas.

Press Trip a 1 GW plant: one large power station, gone in an instant. The rotors slip, the frequency dips, and the reserves catch it well clear of trouble. Now open More and press Trip 3 GW. That is the reference incident Continental Europe is designed to survive: the sudden loss of three gigawatts, roughly two large nuclear units. Several things happen, on different clocks:

In the first instant, nothing but physics responds. The missing power is drawn from the rotating mass of every machine on the grid, and the frequency starts to fall at a rate set by how much rotating mass there is — the inertia. Then some load relief appears on its own: many motors draw slightly less power when they turn slightly slower.

Within seconds the primary reserve wakes up. Selected plants and batteries measure frequency locally and raise output in proportion to the deviation, with no instruction from anyone. The fall stops at the nadir, about 49.75 Hz in this model; Europe’s rules allow at most 800 mHz of instantaneous deviation. By design, primary reserve only holds the frequency at a new, slightly low level.

Over minutes, the secondary reserve takes over. A central controller at the grid operator sees the persistent error and dispatches more generation until the frequency is back at exactly 50.000 Hz, freeing the primary reserve for the next incident. After fifteen minutes or so, slower tertiary reserves and the market replace the lost plant for good.

Now press Lose 15 GW, also under More, far beyond the design case. Primary reserve is overwhelmed, the frequency falls through 49.0 Hz, and automatic relays disconnect slices of customers so the rest can survive. That is how Europe survived its split in November 2006, when a line switching in northern Germany cascaded into three islands.

Try the inertia slider under More. Large spinning machines provide inertia for free; wind turbines and solar panels behind power electronics do not, unless their inverters are programmed to imitate it. With less inertia the frequency falls faster and deeper after the same trip, giving the reserves less time. That is why fast batteries, synchronous condensers and “grid-forming” inverters are joining the toolkit, and why holding reserves costs money that ends up on the network side of the bill.

Inverter-based plants have no spinning mass; how they can still hold frequency is in Part 2 on grid-forming inverters, which also covers what the April 2025 Iberian blackout revealed about voltage control. Solar inverters themselves are introduced in Part 1.

6A day on the grid

Zoom out from seconds to a whole day and the balancing problem changes. Demand has a shape: low at night, a morning ramp, a working-day plateau, an early-evening peak. Solar is a hump at noon and wind wanders over hours and days. Whatever they don’t cover, something else must: the residual load.

The figure opens on a spring Sunday with about the solar Germany now has installed, some 110 GW. Drag the cursor to 13:00. The residual load has all but vanished, the price sits below zero, and some wind and solar is switched off because nobody can use it. Then, as the sun sets, the residual load ramps steeply back up and the evening price climbs. Slide the solar down and the price at noon recovers; under More, switch to a weekday and the dip shrinks.

California named this shape. Its grid operator, CAISO, drew the duck curve in 2013: a midday belly of net demand that deepens every year, then a steep evening neck. It is now a fact of life there. In 2024 CAISO curtailed 3.4 TWh of wind and solar, up 29% in a year, 93% of it solar and most of it in spring, when sunshine is strong and air conditioners are off.5 Pick the solar-heavy grid under More to see the same pattern on a fleet built around midday sun.

Negative prices are not a glitch. Germany’s day-ahead market saw 457 negative hours in 2024 and 573 in 2025 (655 at zero or below).6 Some producers are paid per kilowatt-hour whatever the market price (many older solar panels are on fixed feed-in tariffs), and some plants are expensive to stop and restart. Both will pay to keep producing.

This has a cruel consequence for solar itself. Every panel produces in the same hours as every other panel, so the more there are, the lower the price in exactly those hours. A panel ends up selling at a discount to the day’s average, and the discount deepens as the fleet grows. That ratio, what solar earns per megawatt-hour over the average price of all hours, is its capture rate:

This is called cannibalisation. It doesn’t mean solar is bad; it means the value of the next panel depends on what is already on the grid, and that the cheapest kilowatt-hours are cheap partly because they arrive when nobody needs them. Remember this when we get to the cost of building power plants in section 11: a cost per kilowatt-hour is only half of the story; the other half is when those kilowatt-hours arrive.

7The merit order

How does the market decide which plants run, and at what price? In most liberalised markets, from Europe’s day-ahead auction to Texas and Australia, every generator submits, for every hour (or quarter-hour) of tomorrow, how much it is willing to produce at what price. Every buyer submits how much it wants to buy. An algorithm then stacks all the offers from cheapest to most expensive — the merit order — and finds where supply meets demand.8

What does a plant offer? In a competitive market, roughly its short-run marginal cost: what one more megawatt-hour costs it to make right now. For wind and solar that is close to zero, since the fuel is free; for nuclear it is low; for a fossil plant it is mostly fuel, plus carbon permits where carbon is priced. Here are a country’s offers for one hour, in the order they happened to arrive:

Sorted cheapest first, the offers form a staircase: the supply curve. Demand is a vertical line, how much the country needs this hour. Every plant to the left of it runs; every plant to the right waits. And everyone who runs is paid the same price, the offer of the last plant needed. This is uniform pricing, or pay-as-clear, and it is the most misunderstood feature of electricity markets. Drag the demand line across the edge of a block and the price jumps, not just for the extra megawatt-hours but for all of them.

Why not pay each plant what it bid? Because then nobody would bid their real cost. A wind farm that expects gas to set the price would bid just under it, and so would everyone else: roughly the same price, but with everyone guessing and dearer plants sometimes running ahead of cheaper ones. Pay-as-clear makes bidding your true cost the sensible strategy.

Now let the wind blow. A wind farm’s offer sits at the bottom of the stack, so more wind does not add a block at the top: it widens a block at the bottom and pushes every other plant to the right.

The demand line now crosses the staircase lower down, at a cheaper plant. This is the merit-order effect: wind and solar lower the price in the hours they produce, including for the coal and gas plants that still run, and, as we saw in section 6, for themselves.

Last, the fuel. A fossil plant’s height is its fuel price divided by its efficiency, plus the carbon price times its emissions. Lignite burns cheap fuel but emits about twice as much CO₂ per unit of heat as gas, so its block climbs steeply with the carbon price. Here is the full market, with both prices to play with:

Then push gas to around €150 per megawatt-hour of heat, as in 2022 ($170 at this essay’s 2025 exchange rate), or pick the 2022-style gas shock under More. The marginal plant in most hours is gas-fired, and its cost sets the price for everyone, including wind farms whose costs didn’t change at all.

Switch on Who profits? under More. Every plant below the marginal one is paid the clearing price but spends only its own running cost; the shaded gap is what it keeps. That keep is called infra-marginal rent, and it is normally how plants with low running costs pay back the cost of building them. In 2022 the rents were extraordinary, and governments taxed them.

8Markets in time

The merit order clears once a day, at noon, for all of tomorrow. But tomorrow is uncertain. A wind farm that sold 10 GW for 18:00 may learn by morning that the wind will be weaker than forecast. What does it do?

Electricity is traded in a sequence of markets, each closer to real time.10 The day-ahead auction sets most of tomorrow’s schedule and the price quoted in the news. The intraday market then lets traders adjust as forecasts improve, until shortly before delivery. After that, the grid operator activates balancing reserves and charges each party an imbalance price for its error.

American markets organise the same physics differently. Most US regions have a day-ahead market and then a real-time market that clears every five minutes, and the real-time price serves as the final settlement for deviations.11 Same problem, different labels.

Two extremes bracket everything else. On a sunny, windy holiday the market is oversupplied and prices go negative, in Europe down to the auction floor of −500 €/MWh at worst. In a cold, dark, windless winter week almost every plant is needed and the price is set by the dearest peakers, or by buyers’ willingness to stop consuming. Both kinds of hours are becoming more frequent, and a market of mostly zero-cost wind and solar is increasingly decided by them. We return to the dark week in section 15.

9Markets in space

So far we have pretended the grid is a copper plate. It isn’t. Every line has a thermal limit, and you cannot choose which route the power takes: in an AC network it flows along every path between source and sink, split by impedance. Send power north to south and some goes the long way round, through your neighbour’s country if the physics says so.

The figure opens in nodal mode, how most large US markets work, with the northern wind set at the exact moment the West–South line fills. Drag the wind down a notch and all four nodes share one price. Push it up and the prices split: power is trapped in the north, where wind is curtailed and the price can fall to zero, and scarce in the south, where a local peaker must run. The small chart traces that fork across every wind level. These locational marginal prices show where new generation, storage or demand is worth most; the price gap across a full line, times its flow, is the operator’s congestion rent.

Now switch to zonal, which is how Europe, Australia’s NEM and Japan work: Germany and Luxembourg, for example, are one bidding zone with one price. The market clears as if the grid were a copper plate, and the resulting flows break the line limits. So the operators intervene afterwards, paying northern wind farms to curtail and southern gas plants to start. This redispatch never shows up in the wholesale price; it is socialised into network charges, one of the ways the cost of the energy transition migrated into the regulated part of the bill.12

Neither system is free. Nodal pricing is efficient but makes customers in some places pay more; zonal pricing keeps one price but hides the cost of the grid in a socialised charge and blunts the signal for where to build. Splitting Germany into a northern and a southern zone is one of the live debates of European market design; for now Berlin has chosen one zone and more lines.

Nodal and zonal markets are only two arrangements. Most of the world’s electricity is not sold on a spot exchange at all. China’s grid is run by two state-owned companies, with plants paid regulated or negotiated prices, though provincial spot markets are spreading. India’s state distribution companies buy mainly under long-term contracts. Much of Africa and the Gulf has a single state utility or buyer.
